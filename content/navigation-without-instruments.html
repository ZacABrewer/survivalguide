A compass and a map are the best tools for finding your way. If you lose them, the sun, the stars, the moon, and a simple count of your steps can still keep you moving in a straight line. This chapter teaches each method, tells you how accurate it really is, and shows which "nature tricks" are myths. If you are lost, read the last section first.

How Good Is Each Method?

No method here is as good as a compass. Most give a rough direction, good enough to pick a heading and hold it. Check one method against another. If they disagree, trust the one rated highest.

Shadows and stars give you true directions (the geographic north and south poles). A compass needle points to magnetic north, which differs from true north by an amount that depends on where you are (see Part 9 › Maps & Compass).

Sun and Shadow Methods

What the sun does

The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west. The Army says this shifts with the seasons, and a UK fact sheet says it is due east and west only near the two equinoxes. At its highest point, around local noon, the sun is due south in the northern hemisphere and due north in the southern hemisphere.

Shadow-tip (shadow-stick) method

This is the best sun method. It needs no watch and no math. The Army calls it simple and accurate. The steps are the same in both hemispheres. The sun moves east to west on both sides of the equator, so the tip of a shadow always moves from west to east (this is the guide's reasoning from the Army's description).

  1. Find a flat, open spot in full sun, free of brush. A shadow must fall clearly on the ground.
  2. Push a straight stick upright into the ground. The Army suggests one about 3 feet (1 m) long.
  3. Mark the tip of the shadow with a pebble or a scratch. This first mark is always on the west side.
  4. Wait 10 to 15 minutes. The tip will move a few inches (several centimeters).
  5. Mark the new tip. This second mark is on the east side.
  6. Draw a straight line through both marks. This is your east-west line.
  7. Stand with the first mark on your left foot and the second mark on your right foot. You are now facing north. South is behind you.

How accurate is it? Good, but not perfect. An old National Park Service training paper says errors are usually within about 15 degrees in the tropics and temperate zones, and that the method can fail near the solstices. Treat that as a guide, not a promise. A longer wait between marks moves the tip farther and makes the line easier to draw. Use a level spot, and mark the tip exactly.

Equal-shadow (arc) method

The Army calls this version more accurate. It takes much longer. A morning shadow and an afternoon shadow of equal length lie on opposite sides of midday.

  1. In the morning, set up your stick and mark the shadow tip.
  2. Tie a string to the base of the stick. Hold the string tight to the mark and sweep an arc on the ground.
  3. In the afternoon, watch the shadow lengthen. The moment its tip touches your arc, mark that spot.
  4. Draw a line through the two marks. It runs east and west, like the line in the shadow-tip method.

The Watch Method

You need an analog watch with hands. Set it to true local standard time, not daylight saving time. A digital watch will not work directly. The Army says to draw a clock face on paper and sketch the correct hour hand on it.

Northern hemisphere

  1. Lay the watch flat, or hold it level.
  2. Turn it until the hour hand points at the sun.
  3. Find the point halfway between the hour hand and the 12 mark. A line from the center through that point points south.

Southern hemisphere

  1. Lay the watch flat.
  2. Turn it until the 12 mark points at the sun.
  3. Find the point halfway between the 12 mark and the hour hand. That direction is north.

Daylight saving time

The Army's survival text says to use true local time with no daylight saving adjustment. Its pocket guide offers a second way: on daylight saving time, use the 1 o'clock mark in place of the 12 mark in the steps above. Do one or the other, not both.

An honest rating: poor to fair

Do not trust the watch method with your life. The Army says it gets less accurate closer to the equator, and its land-navigation circular says to check the result against a shadow clock after about an hour. A National Park Service training paper reports that at Big Bend National Park in Texas the answer can be off by about 70 degrees. The same paper says it works better at higher latitudes.

The sources I found give no figures for how the error changes between morning, noon, and evening. Use the watch for a first guess, then confirm it with the shadow-tip method.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: The watch method works the same everywhere. Fact: It can be wrong by a large amount, especially at low latitudes. It is a rough first guess, and the shadow-tip method is more accurate.

The North Star (Polaris)

Polaris sits almost exactly above the north pole of the Earth. The Army says it is less than 1 degree from true north. All other stars appear to circle around it during the night. Polaris is visible only in the northern hemisphere. The Army also says it is too high in the sky to be a useful guide above 70 degrees north latitude.

Finding Polaris

  1. Face the northern sky on a clear night. Find the Big Dipper, a bowl with a long handle.
  2. Pick out the two stars on the outer edge of the bowl, the side away from the handle. They are called the pointer stars.
  3. Draw an imaginary line from the lower pointer through the upper pointer. Extend it about five times the distance between the two pointers. Polaris is there.
  4. Confirm it with Cassiopeia, a W-shaped group of five stars. Cassiopeia sits on the opposite side of Polaris from the Big Dipper. Polaris lies roughly straight out from Cassiopeia's center star.
  5. Look straight down from Polaris to the horizon. The spot on the horizon is true north. Choose a landmark there to walk toward.

Warning

Polaris is the last star in the handle of the Little Dipper, and the Army warns that people confuse it with the other stars of that handle. Use the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia together before you trust a star as north.

Polaris also tells you your latitude

The height of Polaris above the horizon is very nearly your latitude. If it is 30 degrees up, you are near 30 degrees north. The European Southern Observatory and a navigation site both give this rule. It is a rough guide, not a way to fix your spot on a map.

Stars drift. The Army says a star low on the northern horizon stays useful for about half an hour, and that you should recheck every 15 minutes when you walk toward a star in the south.

The Southern Cross and the South Pole

Unlike the north, the southern sky has no bright star right at the pole. You find the south celestial pole by using the Southern Cross (Crux). The Cross is a small, tilted cross of four bright stars. Its long axis points toward the pole. Two bright stars beside the Cross, called the pointers, help you check it. They are Hadar and Rigil Kentaurus (also spelled Rigel Kentaurus).

Method 1: Extend the long axis

  1. Find the Cross and its long axis, from the top star (Gacrux) to the foot star (Acrux).
  2. Extend that line past the foot by about four and a half times the length of the Cross. The point you reach is the south celestial pole.
  3. Drop a line straight down from the pole to the horizon. That spot is due south.

Method 2: Use the pointers

  1. Find the two pointer stars. Draw a line between them.
  2. Find the middle of that line, then draw a second line from the middle at a right angle to it.
  3. The place where this second line meets the long-axis line of the Cross is the south celestial pole. Drop to the horizon, as above.

How accurate is it? Fair

The pole has no star on it, so you are estimating a point in empty sky. Sources disagree on the distance: about 4.5 times the Cross's length on one navigation site, 4 times at EarthSky, and about 5 times in the Army's text. Treat the result as approximate and use the pointer method as a cross-check. The pole's height above the horizon is 90 degrees minus your latitude (about 55 degrees up at 35 degrees south).

Warning

The False Cross is a group of four fairly bright stars that looks like the Southern Cross. It lies much nearer to the bright star Canopus. Before you use any cross, look for the two bright pointer stars (Hadar and Rigil Kentaurus) beside it. If they are missing, you probably have the false one.

Once you have south, pick a landmark on the horizon in that direction. The Army also suggests driving stakes at night to keep the line for the daytime.

Orion's Belt: East and West

Orion's belt is three bright stars in a short, straight row. One of them, Mintaka, the westernmost star of the belt, sits almost exactly on the celestial equator. That is the sky's version of the equator on Earth. Because of that, Mintaka rises almost due east and sets almost due west, as seen from anywhere on Earth. It stays above the horizon about 12 hours, according to an EarthSky article.

So when the belt is low in the sky, the point where it meets the horizon is a rough east or west. On the rising side you see it climb up from the east. On the setting side it drops toward the west. The belt is a rough guide only. The source places only Mintaka on the equator, and I found no error figure, so treat the other two belt stars as approximate.

Orion is not up all year. EarthSky's late-November example: it rises in the east around 9 p.m. and is low in the west by 5 to 6 a.m.

The Moon

The moon shines by reflected sunlight, so its bright side always faces the sun. The Army's survival text gives a simple rule. If the moon rises before sunset, the lit side faces west. If it rises after midnight, the lit side faces east. That gives you a rough east-west clue and nothing more.

The crescent-moon method

  1. On a night with a crescent moon high in the sky, imagine a line through the two tips (horns).
  2. Extend that line down to the horizon. In the northern hemisphere, the point where it meets the horizon is roughly south. In the southern hemisphere, it is roughly north.

Rating: poor. A science-news site says the method works best with the moon high and gives only a general direction. Natural Navigator calls the detailed moon techniques some of the trickiest in the craft. Use stars or the sun first.

Dead Reckoning and Pace Counting

Dead reckoning means working out where you are from three facts: where you started, which way you went, and how far you went. You can do it with no instruments at all. You use a direction from the sun or stars, and you measure distance by counting your steps. It is the basic skill for a walker with no map, because it stops you from drifting in circles.

Set your pace count

A pace is a step length you can repeat. Sources define it differently. The Army's land-navigation circular treats a pace as one natural step, about 30 inches (76 cm) long. A state extension service defines a pace as two steps, starting and ending on the same foot. Either way works. Choose one, and always count the same way.

  1. Before a trip, measure a flat course of 328 feet (100 m). The Army uses courses of 100 to 600 meters on terrain like the land you will cross.
  2. Walk it three or four times at your normal stride, counting paces. Use the average.
  3. Repeat on rough, hilly ground. On a 600-meter course, divide your total by 6.
  4. Write both numbers down and memorize them.

As an example, the Army's 2006 pocket guide gives about 116 paces per 100 meters on level ground, and about 148 cross-country. Your numbers will differ.

Warning

Your pace changes with the ground and weather. The Army says slopes, sand, gravel, mud, snow, rain, fog, darkness, headwinds, and heavy clothing shorten it, so your count rises. Going downhill lengthens it. If you need 120 paces per 100 meters on flat ground, you may need 130 or more uphill.

Keep count with beads, stones, or knots

Do not trust your memory when you are tired or cold. The Army suggests pebbles, knots in a string, or notebook marks. In its pocket guide, you move one pebble from one pocket to the other every 100 meters, so 10 pebbles equal 1 kilometer.

Soldiers also use pace-count beads, often called ranger beads. The cord has nine lower beads and four upper beads, with a knot between them. Slide one lower bead each time you finish your count for 100 meters. After the ninth bead, the next 100 meters makes 1,000 meters. Then you slide one upper bead and push all the lower beads back.

Hold a straight line with backsights

A small bearing error grows with distance, and detours around brush and boulders bend your path. One hiking writer needed five hours to cover a single mile in a straight line. Gooley says lost people often walk in circles without knowing it.

  1. Choose your heading with the sun, a star, or your shadow line.
  2. Pick a landmark on that heading, such as a tree or rock. Walk to it, then pick another beyond it.
  3. Take a backsight. Turn around and look at the landmark you left. It should stay on your line behind you. If it does not, you have drifted.
  4. If you drift, step sideways toward your line, at a right angle to it.
  5. Walk short legs that end at an easy feature, such as a road, stream, or ridge.
  6. When you go around an obstacle, count only the distance gained along your line, then return to the line.

Tip

"Aim off" on purpose. If you want to reach a road or river, aim to one side of the spot you want. Then when you reach the road or river, you know which way to turn. If you aim straight at the spot, you may not know which way to turn.

Write down your headings, counts, and times as you go. A short log lets you retrace your steps (see Part 9 › Travel & Movement).

Natural Signs and How Far to Trust Them

Many books list natural clues for direction. Some work, and many work only in one place. No single clue is reliable. Gooley says he checks seven or eight clues before trusting a conclusion, and he warns that people notice only the signs that match their first guess. Do not walk off on one clue unless other methods agree.

Clue by clue

ClueWhat it is said to showRating and caveats
Moss on treesMoss grows on the north side (northern hemisphere).Very poor. Moss follows moisture and shade, not compass points. It can grow all around a trunk. See the myth below.
Snow melt and slope sunlightIn the northern hemisphere, south-facing slopes get more sun, so snow melts sooner and plants are drier and sparser. North-facing slopes are cooler, moister, and more forested.Fair. A science-education group describes this for the northern hemisphere. In the southern hemisphere the sides logically swap (this guide's inference). Tree shade and local weather can override it.
Lone-tree shapeA tree standing alone tends to have fuller leaves and more branches on its sunny side (the south side in the northern hemisphere), and its sunny branches grow more horizontally.Fair. A neighboring tree or stump can shade one side and reverse the pattern. Gooley says to look for many matching clues, not one.
Prevailing windTrees lean away from the wind. In the UK, wind blows most often from the southwest.Fair, but local. Wind differs by region. Learn the local pattern first.
Church orientationChristian churches are usually built west to east with the altar at the east end. Gravestones often line up the same way.Fair. Natural Navigator calls this a general pattern, not a guarantee. A mosque's prayer niche points toward Mecca, which is a different rule.
Satellite dishesTV dishes aim at satellites parked over the equator, so they face the equator side of the sky (this guide's reasoning). In the UK most face roughly southeast.Fair, local. Natural Navigator's detail is UK-specific. Check several dishes.

The Army text also suggests comparing growth rings on stumps. I found no independent source that confirms it, so it is rated poor in Figure 9.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Moss grows only on the north side of a tree, so it points north. Fact: Moss grows where it is moist, shaded, and cool. A German survival-skills writer calls the idea a myth that can lead you off course. In dense forests, gorges, and humid places moss can cover every side. Even the Army manual says it is not a reliable indicator.

If You Are Lost: Stop First

Navigation without instruments is for people who must move. It is not a reason to leave a spot where rescuers will look for you. The US Forest Service says to stay put and keep a positive attitude if you become lost, and warns that panic is your biggest danger.

Stop, Think, Observe, Plan

  1. Stop. As soon as you think you are lost, halt. Sit down, breathe, and stay where you are.
  2. Think. Recall how you got here and what landmarks you should be able to see. Do not move without a specific reason.
  3. Observe. Look around. Use your compass if you have one. If you are on a trail, stay on it. The Forest Service lists following a stream or drainage downhill only as a last resort, because it is difficult and dangerous.
  4. Plan. Weigh a few options and commit to one. If you are unsure of a route, stay put.

Stay or go?

  • Stay put if you are not sure of your route, if night is coming, if you are hurt, or if you are very tired. This follows the Forest Service advice. Make yourself easy to find (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).
  • Move only with a clear reason and plan, for example to leave a flood zone or reach a road you can see. Pick a heading with the methods above, use a pace count, and leave a note saying when you left and which way you went.

Warning

Walking out is not automatically safer. Move only if staying is more dangerous than going.

Practice these skills before you need them. Take a navigation class from an orienteering club or a search-and-rescue group, and carry a map, a compass, and a whistle on every trip (see Part 9 › Maps & Compass).
