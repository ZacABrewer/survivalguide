Sooner or later in a long emergency, someone hurt has to get somewhere else: to a hospital, to a road, or just to a safer spot. This chapter helps you decide whether to move a patient, how fast, and how to do it without making things worse. It covers the four basic choices, how to send a written call for help, the carries and litters that wilderness and rescue teams use, and how to work with rescuers and helicopters when they arrive. It is written at wilderness first responder level. Moving a hurt person is a skill; take a hands-on WFA or WFR course and practice with a real litter before you need one.

Deciding: Stay, Walk, Carry, or Race?

Courses teach evacuation as a judgment call with no perfect formula. One NOLS paper on field evacuations describes a spectrum of choices from no evacuation at all to self-help, simple carries, and full litter carries. Its guiding rule is short: "when in doubt, get the patient out." The Red Cross wilderness guide asks: stay or go, and if go, fast or slow?

This guide groups those ideas into four levels. The grouping is ours. Use it as a thinking tool, not a rulebook.

LevelWhat it meansTypical situations
Stay and waitKeep the patient warm and protected while help comes or conditions improve.Help is on the way. It is dark, stormy, or the terrain is dangerous.
Walk outThe patient walks, alone or with a helper, slowly, with stops.Red Cross "go slow" cases: a mild head injury with good balance, a wound you cannot close, a suspected cracked rib, a first-time dislocation.
Assisted carry-outRescuers carry the patient by hand, with a seat carry, or on a litter.Walking is unsafe, or the patient is too weak. Needs many people and many hours.
Rapid evacuationSpeed matters most. Send for help now and take the fastest safe route to care, maybe by helicopter.Red Cross "go fast" cases: falling alertness or vital signs, severe hypothermia, serious head, chest, or belly problems, open or pelvic or thigh breaks.

Red flags that push you toward fast evacuation

A JEMS article on wilderness EMS lists the situations where fast evacuation is ideal: threat to life or limb, worsening vital signs, a falling level of alertness, severe blood loss, and a spine injury. The Red Cross list adds more. These matter most:

  • A level of responsiveness that keeps dropping (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs), or a heart rate that keeps climbing.
  • Bleeding you cannot control, or signs of shock.
  • Trouble breathing that is getting worse, especially after a chest injury.
  • A suspected spine injury, severe head injury, or numbness or lost movement below an injury.
  • Severe hypothermia, confusion from heat illness, or serious altitude illness.
  • A fall of more than 15 feet (4.6 m) or any injury that caused a loss of consciousness. The NOLS paper treats these as reasons to evacuate.

The Costs and Risks of Moving or Staying

Neither choice is free. Weigh both before you commit.

Moving costs

  • Time and people. The NOLS paper calls a litter carry time-consuming, resource-hungry, and dangerous to everyone involved. A JEMS article says litter travel runs about one mile per hour with 15 to 20 people in ideal conditions, and that most evacuations take many hours.
  • Rescuer injuries. Simple carries risk falls and back injuries to the bearers. The NOLS paper says they are not right for patients who need spine protection.
  • Weather and terrain. The NOLS paper gives a case where wet snow on loose scree argued against an evacuation. A JEMS article puts rescuer safety ahead of patient condition.

Staying costs

  • Waiting too long. The same paper warns that a patient who could have walked early may get worse and then need a full litter carry.
  • Exposure. Ready.gov says to cover an injured person and put something under them, because chilling raises the risk of shock.

The default rule

The NPS outdoor emergency plan says to stay put and move an injured person only when there is an imminent threat to life. Ready.gov says to move someone away from continuing danger, such as fire or traffic, if you can do it safely.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: You must never move an injured person. Fact: Avoid moving someone with a possible head, neck, or spine injury. But fire, rising water, or rockfall is a good reason to move anyone. Do it gently and in line.

Tip

A short pause to plan after you decide to leave is usually fine. The NOLS authors say a few minutes' delay probably will not change the outcome, while a rushed start can hurt people.

Sending for Help: Who Goes and What They Carry

If you cannot call out, someone may need to go find help. Full rules are in Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming. The radio and phone script is in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication.

  1. Treat and stabilize the patient first, and make them warm and sheltered.
  2. Send two or more people if the group is large enough. The NPS plan says the rest stay with the patient.
  3. Give the messengers a written message (below) and a map with your spot marked. If you are alone with the patient, the NPS plan says to stabilize them first, then go.

What the written message should contain

A stressed, tired messenger forgets things. NOLS teaches a SOAP note: what happened and the history, what you found, your assessment, and your plan. Keep it short and practice reading it aloud. The Red Cross adds what rescuers need: how bad the injuries are, hazards, how long help may take, the rest of the party, equipment, and the weather and terrain.

Warning

Do not send a lone messenger into rough ground at night or in a storm unless staying is worse. A hurt messenger means two patients.

Before You Move Anyone: Treat, Splint, Insulate

Treat life threats first (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Then package the patient for the trip.

  1. Check breathing and bleeding. Dress open wounds and splint any suspected fractures (see Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine).
  2. Take a set of vital signs and write it down with the time, so you can see change on the trail.
  3. Remove wet clothing and wrap the patient in dry layers. The Red Cross says to insulate them from the ground and use dry blankets. For a cold patient, see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.
  4. Strap the patient in. A US Army trainee text suggests at least two straps: one across the chest and one across the legs below the knees. Add more on rough or steep ground. Put the straps on after the patient is on the covered litter.

Spine caution

The Red Cross says that if you suspect a head, neck, or spine injury, you should keep the person still and support the head in the position found. If they are face down, turn them face-up as a team, keeping head, neck, and body in line. The Wilderness Medical Society's 2024 guideline sets the goal as spinal motion restriction, not rigid immobilization: no rigid collars, a backboard only for a short move, and the neck supported in a position of comfort. To get a pad or litter under the person, WMS prefers a lift-and-slide (many hands lift straight up a few inches while another slides the litter under) over a log roll. Use a log roll only if you lack the hands to lift, and avoid it if the pelvis may be broken. For most people with a suspected spine or pelvic injury, waiting for a trained rescue team beats a long hand carry. Full details are in Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine.

Carries and Drags for One or Two Rescuers

Hand carries are for short moves: out of a hazard, onto a litter, or over an obstacle. The Army manual says not to use them on a person with a neck or spine injury unless a life-threatening hazard is near. For every carry, keep your back straight, lift with your legs, move in unison, and rest often.

Drags

A drag is quick and needs no gear, but it is rough on the patient. Always pull in line with the body.

Blanket drag

  1. Spread a blanket, tarp, or sleeping bag beside the patient.
  2. With helpers, lift and slide the patient onto it (or log roll if you lack hands), with the head about 2 feet (60 cm) from one corner.
  3. Wrap the edges of the blanket around the patient.
  4. Face the head end. Keep your back straight and pull with your legs, in as straight a line as you can.

Clothes (shoulder) drag

  1. Kneel at the head and grip the patient's clothing under the shoulders.
  2. Place your forearms on both sides of the head to support it.
  3. Pull in a straight line. You must bend at the waist, which is its main drawback.

An ankle pull is faster but leaves the head unsupported. Use it only over short, smooth ground.

The Army neck drag (patient's hands looped around your neck) is for short distances. Do not use it with a broken arm or suspected neck injury.

One-person carries

  • Pack-strap carry. Stand the patient up with support. Turn your back to them, draw their arms over your shoulders, cross them in front, and grasp the wrists. Lean forward so the patient's weight rides high on your back. Keep the patient's arms palms-down. It suits moderate distances, roughly 165 to 1,000 feet (50 to 300 m).
  • Fireman's carry. The patient lies across your shoulders. Army texts call it one of the easiest one-person carries, but lifting the patient into place takes a strong rescuer. The Army first-aid manual (FM 4-25.11) warns to keep the head from snapping back during the lift, which could injure the neck. The patient hangs head-down across your shoulders with weight on the chest and belly, so do not use it when injuries make that unsafe, and never with a suspected neck or spine injury.

Warning

One-person carries load your back and twist the patient. Do not use them for a suspected spine, pelvic, or thigh injury. If your back strains, stop and put the patient down.

Two-person carries

Seat carries use two bearers' arms, or a real chair, as a seat, so the patient must be able to sit.

Two-handed seat

  1. Kneel or squat on either side of the patient at hip level, facing each other.
  2. Each person slides one arm under the patient's back and the other under their thighs.
  3. Each person grips the other's wrists.
  4. Stand together on a count, using your legs, and walk in the direction the patient faces.

The Army text uses this for short distances, up to about 165 feet (50 m), and for placing a patient on a litter. Sources describe the arm positions a little differently, so practice first.

Four-handed seat

Use this only for a conscious patient who can sit up and hold on. The Army manual suggests it for a moderate distance (about 165 to 1,000 feet, or 50 to 300 m), such as with a head or foot injury.

  1. Stand side by side. Each bearer grips one of their own wrists with the opposite hand, and then grips a wrist of the other bearer with the free hand. The four forearms form a square.
  2. Lower the seat to about knee height.
  3. The patient sits on the square and puts an arm around each bearer's shoulders.
  4. Stand up straight together, using your legs, and walk forward in step.

Chair carry

Use this on stairs or narrow, uneven ground. Seat the patient in a sturdy chair, not a swivel, light folding, beach, or resin patio chair. One rescuer at the head grips the sides of the chair back and tilts it onto its rear legs. The other faces the chair and grips the front legs. Both stand on the head rescuer's command. Do not carry anyone in a wheeled chair.

Two-person arms carry

Two bearers kneel on one side and slide their arms under the patient's shoulders, waist, hips, and knees. They lift together and turn the patient toward their chests as they stand. The Army manual calls this the safest manual carry for a suspected back injury in an extreme emergency, best with two more helpers keeping the head and legs in line.

Rope carries

An American Alpine Club article describes a split-coil rope seat that lets one or two rescuers carry a patient piggyback, with the rope spreading the load. It says to use it only when there are no life-threatening injuries and no concern about head or spine injury. It takes practice and the right rope, so learn it from an instructor (see Part 3 › Cordage, Knots & Lashings).

Improvised Litters

A litter supports the patient along their whole body. You need two strong poles and something to span them. Test each pole with your own weight first. Pad it, and insulate the patient from the litter.

Blanket-and-pole litter

  1. Lay a large blanket, tarp, or poncho flat. Use two poles longer than the patient.
  2. Lay the poles across the blanket so that it is divided into three equal parts.
  3. Fold one outer third over the first pole.
  4. Fold the other outer third over the second pole and toward the first.
  5. Move the patient onto the litter with a lift-and-slide (preferred) or, if you lack hands, a log roll. Then pad and cover them.

Jacket litter

Button or zip two or three coats or heavy shirts, then turn them inside out so the fasteners are underneath. Leave the sleeves open. Lay them flat and pass the two poles through the sleeves.

A blanket with two opposite edges rolled inward makes handholds for four or more bearers (US Army text). With no poles, we suggest it only for short moves.

Rope or webbing litter

RopeWiki, a community climbing wiki (a secondary source), describes a webbing litter built from loops, with handle loops near the neck, center, and knees, and a rope hammock litter. It gave no written steps for the rope version, so we give none. Learn it in a rope-rescue course.

Warning

A litter that sags in the middle bends the spine. The more technical the ground, the more you need a rigid stretcher (JEMS). With a suspected spine, pelvic, or thigh injury, a rescue team with a proper litter is the safer choice.

Carrying a Litter Safely

Litter carrying is a team job. One person must lead.

Team size

JEMS (a trade journal) recommends six bearers on smooth terrain and eight on rough terrain, and ideally 18 or more people, so you can swap on a long carry. Another JEMS article says a litter team of 15 to 20 moves only about 1 mile (1.6 km) per hour, even in ideal conditions. The Army manual lifts a litter with two or four bearers, but that is for short military moves. For a long carry over real ground, plan for six to eight on the litter plus at least two full relief teams.

Rules for the carry

  • One leader gives all commands. The Army text says only one person calls the lift and set-down. Use the same words every time. The command STEADY slows everyone down.
  • Move gently. Keep the litter level, especially over ditches and logs. Rear bearers watch the front bearers and match their steps.
  • Rotate often. The JEMS article suggests switching about every 400 to 500 yards (365 to 460 m), or every 10 to 15 minutes, and says to adjust for safety. One mountain-rescue method (ASRC) keeps the litter moving: relief bearers walk ahead, step aside to let the litter pass, and grab on at the rear; everyone shifts forward; and the front pair steps off, crosses sides, and rejoins the relief line ahead. Bearers switch carrying arms each time. Plan rest stops.
  • Watch the patient. Assign one person to watch and talk with the patient and recheck them. The head end is the easiest spot for this.
  • Carry on slopes with the right end first. Sources differ on level ground: Army texts carry feet first, while one mountain-rescue group usually goes head first. Going uphill, head first. An Army text adds that for a leg fracture the order flips so the patient's weight does not press on the injury: feet first uphill, head first downhill. Follow your team leader's choice.

Common deadly mistakes

  • Starting a long litter carry with too few people, then dropping the patient when bearers tire.
  • Several people calling commands at once.
  • Carrying across loose scree, steep snow, or a river in the dark.
  • Leaving a hypothermic or injured patient uncovered on a litter.

Working With Rescuers

When trained people arrive, you become the best source of information. Hand off clearly and help them get started.

  1. Pick one communicator. The NOLS paper says to name one person to talk to the rescue leader, and to tell rescuers everything important before they leave.
  2. Hand over the written message and your notes. Read the SOAP note aloud. Include the times of vitals and treatments.
  3. Pass on the medicines, allergies, and history, and tell them about hazards you saw.
  4. Follow their lead. They train for this and carry the right gear. Ask for a role if they have one for you.
  5. Keep the group together. Do not let anyone wander off to look for the rescuers.

Staying put after you call

After you reach dispatch, stay where you said you would be, unless they tell you otherwise or there is a direct threat to life. The NPS plan says to stay put and minimize movement. If you must move, leave a note and signs (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming). Keep your phone on, with its battery saved, in case dispatch calls back.

What dispatch needs from you

The exact script is in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication. The NPS plan adds: the injury and any medical conditions, your first-aid gear, GPS coordinates if you have them, the trail or area name, and landmarks.

Helicopter Evacuation

A helicopter is fast, but it is not a taxi. The crew chooses whether it can fly or land. JEMS says air transport should go to patients who benefit from it, such as those with a time-sensitive injury or illness. If air may be delayed, start ground transport and plan to meet the aircraft. Never delay care waiting for one that may not arrive.

Body signals and basic landing-zone rules are also in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication. This section adds what the ground party does.

Preparing the landing zone

The crew will give instructions. In general:

  • Size. Sources disagree. The NOLS handbook says a zone about 60 feet (18 m) across is best for backcountry helicopters. Texas state EMS training and several air-medical programs ask for at least 100 by 100 feet (30 by 30 m), with no overhead wires or branches. Use 100 by 100 feet as your minimum, and more at night or when the approach path has trees.
  • Surface. Flat and firm. Limits vary: Texas state EMS training says 3 degrees or less, one air-medical program says no more than 5, another under 10. Aim for 3 degrees or less, which looks nearly flat. Clear rocks, logs, and trash. Flatten tall grass, and cut brush flat at ground level, not to sharp points.
  • Hazards and loose items. Look for wires, poles, antennas, and trees. Rotor wash lifts blankets, hats, tarps, and bags, so stow or weigh down everything.
  • Marking. Anchor bright markers at the corners. At night, use lights inside cones, and never point a light at the aircraft.
  • Distance. One program says keep people and vehicles at least 200 feet (60 m) from the touchdown spot. Others say 100 to 150 feet (30 to 45 m) from the aircraft. Use the larger number: 200 feet. Only a ground guide, if you have one, stands closer, just outside the zone.
  • No flares or fire. Texas state EMS training says never use road flares to mark a landing zone. Use cones with lights, strobes, or bright anchored markers.

Radio contact with the pilot

If you have a radio, an air-medical program advises naming one person to talk to the pilot. Report the zone's readiness, obstacles, wind direction, and ground surface, and say where the ground guide stands. Give locations by compass direction or clock position, with 12 o'clock at the helicopter's nose. The program teaches "Go around" if a hazard appears during approach and "Stop, Stop, Stop" for immediate danger. The pilot has the final say.

Hand signals

The FAA's helicopter safety circular lists common signals: arms crossed overhead means stop, arms crossed and extended downward means land, an arm held out sideways with the palm down means hover, and a thumb up with the arm bent means all clear. The signaler stands where the pilot can see them, with their back to the wind. If you are not trained, stand clear and let the crew direct you.

Safety around the aircraft

  1. Do not move toward the helicopter while the rotors turn, until a crew member signals you.
  2. Approach from the front or side in the pilot's view, staying low, and walk. On a slope, approach from the downhill side.
  3. Never walk toward the rear, where the tail rotor is.
  4. Carry long items level at waist height or lower, never upright. Keep stretchers, straps, and blankets under control.
  5. Do not chase anything the rotor wash blows away. If dust blinds you, crouch and wait for help.
  6. Let the crew load the patient. Leave the way you came.

Do not

  • Do not approach a running helicopter without a crew signal.
  • Do not shine lights at the pilot or leave loose gear where rotor wash will lift it.
