A plan is a decision you make while you are calm, so you do not have to make it while you are scared. This chapter shows how to write a family emergency plan, pick rally points, map two ways out, protect your documents and cash, and decide whether to shelter in place or leave. It ends with drills, because a plan nobody has practiced is only a piece of paper.

Why Write It Down

Stress narrows your thinking. A written plan gives every person the same answers: who to call, where to meet, when to go. Ready.gov asks four questions to start: which disasters could hit your area, how will you reach each other, where will you meet, and what special needs does your family have. Update the answers when your family changes.

Most chapters in this guide teach a skill. This one organizes the skills into a single set of decisions. It pairs with Part 1 › Kits & Gear (what you carry), Part 1 › Priorities (what comes first), and Part 9 › Natural Disasters (hazard-by-hazard actions).

Build the Family Plan

Use a free template from Ready.gov, FEMA, or the Red Cross, or draw your own. The card below shows the parts any good plan includes. Fill it in at the kitchen table with everyone present, including children old enough to help.

Household roles

Give each job to a named person, and name a backup. Jobs that matter most:

  • Kit and papers: grabs the go-bag and the document folder.
  • Children: one adult is responsible for each young child. The University of Florida extension advises naming who picks up children from school, or who cares for them if they are not at home.
  • Pets: carries them and their records. Check ahead which shelters accept animals (UF/IFAS).
  • Utilities: knows how to shut off water, and what officials say about gas and power. Learn the actual steps for your house before you need them.
  • Radio and information: listens for official instructions. UF/IFAS suggests taking turns monitoring a radio while sheltering.

Plans for school and work

You may be apart when trouble starts. Ask each school and workplace for its emergency plan: where children go, who may pick them up, and how families are told. UF/IFAS advises keeping contact numbers for work and for children's schools. Write the approved pickup people on the card.

Radiation event: do not go get the kids

The CDC says that if loved ones are in schools, day cares, hospitals, or nursing homes during a radiation emergency, you should not go get them. Going outside could expose you and them. Those places have plans to keep people inside safely. Talk to your child's school now about its plan.

The communication plan, in brief

The full plan is in Part 9 › Communication. In short, Ready.gov says to have every family member contact the same person who lives out of town, since that person may be easier to reach. Plan to text rather than call, because texts leave phone lines open for emergency workers. Put the plan on paper in backpacks and wallets, and save it in each phone.

Rally Points at Three Levels

A rally point is a place you can all find without a phone. Ready.gov says to choose familiar, easy-to-reach spots at home, in your neighborhood, and outside your city or town. Choose places that accept pets and service animals if you have them.

  1. Level 1, home. Pick the safest room for each hazard (see Part 9 › Natural Disasters). Pick one outside meeting spot a safe distance away, so you can count heads after a fire.
  2. Level 2, near home. Pick a spot you can reach on foot if the street is blocked. Choose a second one in the other direction.
  3. Level 3, outside town. Pick a friend, relative, or known place beyond the likely disaster zone. Write the address and phone number on the card.
  4. Walk to each place together once. Children learn routes by walking, not by reading.

Tip

Give every rally point a backup. A bridge may be closed or a building locked. Write both on the card, and tell the out-of-town contact where you plan to be.

Evacuation Routes and Go-Time Triggers

Two ways out

Plan two exits from every room in your home, and two routes out of your town. This is standard good practice rather than a sourced rule, but the logic is simple: one road can flood, burn, or jam. UF/IFAS adds three points. Follow the routes officials recommend instead of shortcuts or a navigation app. Keep a paper map because cell service can fail. And fill the gas tank early, because stations may close or run out.

Drive both routes once in daylight. Note gas stations, hospitals, and spots that flood. Plan a destination for each route in a different direction, so one hazard cannot block both.

Go-time triggers

A trigger is a condition you agree on in advance that means "we leave now." Deciding in advance removes the argument when time is short. Ready.gov's hazmat guidance says to leave immediately when officials tell you to evacuate. Beyond that, set your own triggers and write them on the plan card. Examples (this guide's suggestions, not agency rules):

  • An official evacuation order for your zone, or an official advisory that you should leave.
  • Fire, smoke, or a gas smell inside your home or building.
  • Floodwater rising toward the building, or a flood warning for your street.
  • A wildfire in your area, even before an order: early departure leaves roads clear.
  • A health need you cannot meet at home, such as a medicine supply or power for medical equipment.

Leave early when you can. UF/IFAS suggests locking doors and windows, turning off water, wearing sturdy shoes and protective clothing, and leaving a note with your route and destination. Take the go-bag, medicines, phone and chargers, and pets (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear).

Do not

  • Wait for a second warning when the first one tells you to go.
  • Drive into flooded roads (see Part 9 › Natural Disasters).
  • Take shortcuts that officials did not list.
  • Leave without telling your out-of-town contact where you are going.

Important Documents and Cash

After a fire, flood, or long evacuation, paperwork decides how fast you recover. Ready.gov says to gather photo ID, birth certificate, Social Security card, military records, housing payment records, insurance policies, proof of income, tax statements, medical contacts, immunization records, and a list of medicines. The FDIC adds copies of both sides of your bank cards and the front of checks, since you may need account numbers to authorize payments. It also advises an inventory of belongings, backed by receipts, appraisals, or photos.

How to protect them

  • Keep a go-bag set. The FDIC advises copies of key documents with your emergency supplies in a secure but easy-to-reach spot.
  • Make digital copies. Ready.gov suggests a safe deposit box, an external drive, or the cloud. The FDIC says to choose files you can open on a phone or computer, encrypt sensitive ones, and lock devices with a password, fingerprint, or face unlock.
  • Seal for water. The FDIC notes safe deposit boxes often are not waterproof. It advises sealing papers in waterproof bags or containers, especially where flooding is possible. Use zip-top freezer bags or a hard case with a gasket. The agencies do not rate fire-proof boxes, so this guide gives no advice on them.
  • Guard against scams. Ready.gov warns not to share bank account, Social Security, or card numbers unless you are sure the request is legitimate.

FEMA also publishes an Emergency Financial First Aid Kit toolkit with forms and checklists. This guide's authors could not open that file, so the lists above follow Ready.gov and FDIC. Look for the current version on Ready.gov.

Cash

Ready.gov says to keep a small amount of cash at home in a secure place, in small bills, because ATMs and cards may not work in a disaster. The FDIC agrees: keep most of your money in an insured bank account, and set aside a little cash for food and supplies. Neither agency gives a dollar figure. Pick an amount you can use for a few days of food and fuel without risk if it is lost or stolen.

Caches

A cache is supplies stored away from where you live, so one fire or flood cannot take everything. The agencies cited here do not cover caches, so this section is this guide's general advice. It is not sourced to an authority.

What to store

Think of a cache as a small, sealed kit for a specific job. Examples: a car kit if your route is long (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear); a relative's closet with water, food, and spare clothes for your family; copies of key papers and some cash. Keep items you can use without special tools. Do not store loaded weapons, fuel, or anything hazardous in a place others can reach.

Where, and the legal limits

Legal note

Cache only on your own property, or where the owner has agreed in writing or in a message you keep. Burying items on public land, parks, or someone else's land without permission can break trespass or land-use laws, and rules differ by state and agency. Digging can also hit buried pipes or cables, so check with local utilities first. Also check your lease, homeowners association rules, and local fire code (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear on fuel limits).

Containers and rotation

  • Choose food-grade containers with tight lids, as the University of Georgia Extension advises for dry food storage (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear).
  • Use metal or hard plastic with a gasket. Avoid glass.
  • Add a label with the contents, the date, and the check-by date.
  • Keep the location secret from all but the people who need it, and write it in the plan.
  • Rotate on a schedule: check twice a year, replace food and medicine before dates pass, and swap batteries.
  • Keep an inventory. If you cannot remember what is in a cache, you will not use it.

Shelter in Place or Evacuate?

This is the hardest decision in a plan, and the answer depends on the hazard. Ready.gov says the right choice follows official instructions and the threat: sometimes staying indoors is safer, and sometimes leaving is. Monitor local radio, TV, or alerts for the call.

When officials order you out

Leave immediately, as Ready.gov says for hazmat events. The same page says you can text SHELTER and your ZIP code to 43362 to find the nearest public shelter. If you can, learn where the hazard is and move away from it. If the chemical is inside your building, leave without crossing the contaminated area; if you cannot, move as far from it as possible and shelter in place.

Chemical release: shelter-in-place steps

The CDC and Ready.gov agree on this sequence. Practice it once, so you are not reading it for the first time during an emergency.

  1. Go indoors with your pets. Close and lock windows and doors.
  2. Turn off fans, air conditioners, and the furnace, and close the fireplace damper. Or set a system to full recirculation so no outside air comes in.
  3. Take your go-kit, medicines for people and pets, your phone, and chargers.
  4. Go to your safe room. The CDC says pick a large room, on the highest floor possible, with few or no windows and a water source. Shut the door.
  5. Seal the windows with taped plastic sheeting, tape over the vents, and tape around the door. Out of plastic? Stuff towels, sheets, or clothes in the vents and under the door.
  6. Seal gaps around window air conditioners, bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans, and stove and dryer vents.
  7. If fumes may have entered, breathe shallowly through a cloth or towel.
  8. Do not eat or drink anything that may be contaminated.
  9. Listen to a radio or news app. Tell your out-of-area contact where you are and that you are safe.
  10. Stay until police, fire, or other officials give the all-clear. That may be hours, or days.

Build a sealing kit ahead of time: duct tape, scissors, towels, and plastic sheeting. The CDC says to cut 2 to 4 mil plastic to fit each window and label every piece. Cutting the plastic beforehand saves time when it matters.

Not at home?

A car is not a sealed room, so do not count on it to protect you for long. Use the radio, an app, or your phone to find the nearest shelter, go there, and follow officials. If you are outside, the CDC and Ready.gov say to move away from the release and stay upwind if you can. In a car, close the windows and vents and turn off the heater and air conditioner. If you cannot get clear quickly, Ready.gov says to find the closest building and shelter in place there.

Radiation or fallout: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned

Ready.gov and the CDC use three short commands for a nuclear explosion or other radiation emergency.

  1. Get inside. Go to the nearest building at once and move away from windows. Ready.gov says you typically have about 10 minutes or more before fallout arrives, so head for the closest sturdy building or basement within a few minutes. Brick or concrete buildings, multi-story buildings, and basements protect best. Underground garages and subways also shelter well.
  2. Stay inside. Go to the basement or the middle of the building, away from exterior walls and the roof, because fallout settles on the outside. Turn off fans, air conditioners, and forced-air heat. Close windows, doors, and fireplace dampers. Bring pets in.
  3. Stay tuned. Follow official instructions on a radio, TV, or phone. The CDC recommends a battery or hand-crank radio, preferably a NOAA weather radio. Texts may get through when calls fail.

Stay in the best shelter for at least the first 24 hours, as both agencies advise, because radiation levels fall quickly over that time. Leave sooner only for an immediate danger such as fire, a gas leak, building collapse, serious injury, or when officials say it is safe. Once inside, remove your outer clothing, which the CDC and Ready.gov say can take off up to 90 percent of radioactive material. Do it carefully to avoid shaking dust loose. Then wash with soap and water, or wipe exposed skin and hair with a clean damp cloth. Ready.gov says do not use household cleaning wipes or hand sanitizer on skin, because they do not remove radioactive material.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If something terrible happens, drive away as fast as you can. Fact: For fallout, the CDC says cars offer poor protection, and any building is safer than being outside. For a chemical release, officials may tell you to stay put. Get your direction from official instructions, not from panic.

Do not

  • Do not leave a good shelter early because you feel restless. Wait for the all-clear.
  • Do not eat uncovered food or garden produce from outdoors during a radiation event until officials say it is safe (Ready.gov). Food and water that were inside buildings are safe.
  • Do not go to schools or hospitals to get family during a radiation event (CDC).

Drills and Plan Reviews

Ready.gov says to hold regular household meetings to review and rehearse the plan. The agencies do not set a fixed schedule, so the calendar below is this guide's suggestion. Pick days you will remember, like when the clocks change.

  1. Pick one drill for the month and tell everyone when it is.
  2. Run it with a timer. Note what went wrong. Who could not find the flashlight? Which door stuck?
  3. Fix the problem the same week. Update the card.
  4. Reprint the plan card and replace the old copies in backpacks, wallets, and the go-bag.
  5. For children, make drills calm and practical. Reward the practice, and do not scare them.

Tip

Test your go-time triggers by asking, "What would make us leave tonight?" If family members give different answers, your plan has a gap.

Common Mistakes

Do not

  • Write a plan and never share it, practice it, or update it.
  • Rely on one route, one rally point, or one phone.
  • Store your only copy of key documents in one place.
  • Wait for certainty before you go when your triggers have been met.
  • Ignore pets, medicines, and people with mobility needs.
  • Cache supplies on land you do not own.

Take a CPR and first-aid course, and ask your local emergency management office what hazards and alert systems your community uses. A plan is a living document: write it, practice it, then improve it.
