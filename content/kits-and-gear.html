A kit is a plan you can pick up. This chapter walks from what you carry every day, to your car, to a go-bag, to a home stockpile that grows from two weeks to a year. It then shows how to choose knives, axes, saws, lights, stoves, and sleep gear, and how to keep it all working. Start small, buy skills before gadgets, and test everything before you need it.

Think in Layers, Not Piles

You will not always be home when trouble starts. So build kits in layers that follow you: your pockets, your vehicle, your bag, and your house. Each layer covers the gap until the next one. The Red Cross plans for 3 days away from home and 2 weeks at home.

  • Budget order. First use what you already own. Next buy water, light, warmth, and first aid. Last buy specialty items. A $15 headlamp you carry beats a $300 pack you left home.
  • Skills first. Gear fails, gets lost, and runs out. Training does not. Take a first-aid course (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines) and learn fire, shelter, and water basics (see Part 2 › Fire, Part 2 › Shelter, Part 2 › Water).
  • Test it. Light the stove. Sleep one night in the bag in your yard. Find what fails while help is close.

Legal note

Laws on carrying knives and multi-tools vary by state and city. Many places also ban them in schools, courthouses, and on aircraft. Check your local rules before you carry.

If you are stranded in winter

  1. Stay with your car unless you can see a safe place close by. It shelters you and helps rescuers find you.
  2. Call 911 or roadside help. If your signal is weak, try a text.
  3. Make the car easy to see: bright cloth on the antenna, hazard lights, and reflectors.
  4. Check the tailpipe often and clear snow, ice, or mud from it.
  5. Run the engine only about 5 to 10 minutes each hour, with one window cracked, as Michigan State Police advises.
  6. Do not dig hard. Sweat chills you later.
  7. Layer up, close windows when the engine is off, and share warmth.

Carbon monoxide in a stuck car

A blocked tailpipe can fill the cabin with carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell. Headache, dizziness, and weakness are early signs. Open the doors and get into fresh air at once (see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning for CO poisoning). The CDC also says never to run a vehicle in an attached garage, even with the door open.

Summer: the parked car is an oven

The National Weather Service says a car can warm about 20°F (11°C) in 10 minutes and reach 120°F (49°C) within minutes. Cracked windows help little. Never leave a child or pet in a parked car, even briefly.

Water storage

The CDC says to store at least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 days and, if you can, 2 weeks. Use food-grade containers with tight lids. Never reuse containers that once held bleach, pesticides, or other toxic products. Wash the container, sanitize it with 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach in 1 quart of water, then let it air-dry. Label it "drinking water" with the date. Keep it at 50 to 70°F (10 to 21°C), away from sun and gasoline. Replace home-filled water every 6 months. Purification steps are in Part 2 › Water.

Food storage and rotation

Store food cool, dry, and dark. Write the date on each package and eat the oldest first. Sources differ on how long cans last, so use the shorter time.

Do not eat from bad cans

Throw away cans that bulge, leak, or are deeply rusted. Do not taste food to test it (see Part 5 › Food Safety).

When the power goes out

The CDC says a closed refrigerator keeps food safe about 4 hours, a full freezer about 48 hours, and a half-full freezer about 24 hours. Keep doors shut. After 4 hours without power, throw out refrigerated meat, fish, eggs, milk, and leftovers. Eat fridge food first, freezer food second, and pantry food last.

Fuel storage

Fuel can burn, explode, or poison you. Rules vary by town and fire code, so call your local fire department before you store any. As one example, Lehi City, Utah, cites the 2012 International Fire Code and allows up to 25 gallons of gasoline, preferably in a detached shed, no more than 10 gallons in an attached garage, and none in a basement. Propane cylinders stay outdoors. Lehi allows only two 1-pound cylinders indoors. Lehi also advises far less than the maximum. Your own limits may differ.

  • Keep fuel in labeled, approved, non-glass containers, away from furnaces, water heaters, and sparks. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) gives this advice for generator fuel.
  • Do not store gasoline in the living space.
  • Use the oldest fuel first and refill. Fuel stabilizers vary, so follow the label.

Generator safety

Generators kill with carbon monoxide

The CPSC and CDC both say to run a generator only outdoors and at least 20 feet (6 m) from doors, windows, and vents. Never run one in a garage, basement, crawlspace, or shed, even with doors open. CO can reach deadly levels in minutes. Put battery-powered CO alarms near sleeping areas, test them, and replace them as the maker directs or about every 5 years (CDC). For CO poisoning first aid, see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.

  1. Place the generator outside, well away from all openings.
  2. Use outdoor-rated, heavy-duty extension cords with a rating above your load.
  3. Turn it off and let it cool before you refuel. Spilled gasoline on hot parts can catch fire.
  4. Never plug it into a wall outlet to power the house ("backfeeding"). It can electrocute utility workers.

The CDC adds that a portable gas camp stove, a grill, and burning charcoal also make CO. Never use any of them indoors, in a tent, or in a garage.

Choosing Gear

Buy for the job, not the label. A used or mid-priced item is often enough.

Knives

A knife is the first cutting tool. Pick the type by the job.

  • Small folding knife (about 2.5 in / 6 cm blade). Good for carving, food, and fine work. A weak pivot cannot take chopping or batoning (Backpacker magazine).
  • Fixed blade (about 4 in / 10 cm). Stronger and simpler. Backpacker magazine says to look for at least a three-quarter tang. A partial tang failed for that tester under load.
  • Steel. Stainless resists rust but can chip. Carbon steel is tougher but rusts if left wet.
  • Grind. BladeHQ rates convex strongest, scandi next, flat a good all-round balance, and hollow the sharpest but "somewhat frail." For one all-purpose blade, pick flat or scandi.
  • Skip serrations for outdoor use (Backpacker). Add a lanyard.

Knife handling and sharpening are in Part 3 › Cordage, Knots & Lashings and Part 3 › Hunting & Fishing Tools.

Axes, hatchets, and saws

The Forest Service describes a single-bit felling axe as having a head of 3 to 6 lb (1.4 to 2.7 kg) and a handle of 30 to 36 in (76 to 91 cm). It says hatchets have short heads and handles and are used in building, camping, and hunting. A splitting axe is for firewood. An axe cuts best on a fine edge, so a double-bit axe keeps one blade sharp for felling and one blunter for knots and ground-level work. A saw gives you more control and needs less room than an axe.

Axe and saw safety

Clear a space around you before every swing. Keep your legs and feet out of the swing path. Keep tools sharp, because dull ones slip. Wear sturdy shoes, gloves, and eye protection. Learn from an experienced person (see Part 2 › Firewood & Woodlots).

Containers

For water, use the CDC rules above. For dry food, use food-grade 5-gallon buckets with tight lids (University of Georgia Extension). Boiling water needs a metal pot (see Part 2 › Water). Avoid glass in bags and cars.

Lights

The Red Cross lists flashlights with extra batteries, and a battery or hand-crank radio. A headlamp keeps both hands free. Use one battery size across your kits, and keep a backup light. The Red Cross advises battery lights instead of candles during outages. Michigan lists a candle for a stuck car. Conservative advice: use a battery light.

Sleep systems

A sleep system is a bag, a pad, and a shelter. Cold ground pulls heat from you, so the pad matters as much as the bag.

Bag ratings: EN/ISO 23537

The standard bag test (formerly EN 13537, now ISO 23537) uses a heated manikin on an insulating mat. It gives three main temperatures, explained here by Australian Hiker (a secondary source):

  • Comfort: a standard woman sleeping relaxed. A man usually stays comfortable at a lower temperature.
  • Lower limit: a standard man sleeping curled up for 8 hours. Warm sleepers use it as the coldest temperature the bag suits.
  • Extreme: a survival-only rating. Below the lower limit you can expect strong cold and a risk of hypothermia.

REI says real comfort varies with your metabolism and your pad. It advises adding a 10 to 15°F (6 to 8°C) buffer to the coldest night you expect. So for 20°F (-7°C) nights, shop for a rating near 5 to 10°F (-15 to -12°C). Pick a bag a few inches longer than you, not roomy, because empty space is hard to warm. Down is lighter and compresses well but loses insulation when wet. Synthetic keeps warmth when damp, dries faster, and costs less, but it is heavier and bulkier (REI).

Pads: R-value (ASTM F3340)

R-value measures how well a pad blocks heat from flowing into the ground. Higher is warmer. ASTM F3340 is a single test method that brands began using around 2020, so R-values can be compared across brands. Look for "ASTM F3340" near the number. Older pads may use a different test. REI adds that R-values add up when you stack pads, and recommends a closed-cell foam pad under an air pad for extra insulation and puncture protection.

Myths and Deadly Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A big, expensive bug-out bag makes you ready. Fact: Most people shelter at home, which is why the Red Cross plans a 2-week home supply. Fit, skills, and a tested plan matter more than the size or price of a bag.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A sleeping bag's "comfort" number is the coldest it can go. Fact: Comfort is the warm end. The lower limit is colder, and the extreme rating is for survival only.

Do not

  • Run any engine, stove, or generator in an enclosed space, even with doors open.
  • Store fuel in the living space or in glass.
  • Leave a child or pet in a parked car.
  • Eat from bulging, leaking, or badly rusted cans.
  • Reuse bleach jugs or chemical containers for water.
  • Buy gear you have never tested.

Take a first-aid and CPR course, and practice every skill your gear needs. Gear without training is just weight.
