This chapter is a quick-action index. Each playbook runs the same way: warning signs, then the first hour, first day, and first week, with pointers to the chapters that hold the details. Use it to decide what to do next when you are stressed and short on time, then go deeper once you are safe.

How to Use the Playbooks

Every playbook rests on the ranking in Part 1 › Priorities: handle life threats, protect yourself from weather, be findable, secure water, and only then worry about food. Each playbook below bends that order to fit one situation. If anyone is hurt, start with Part 0 › Red Pages.

Tip

Tell someone your plan before every trip, and keep a charged phone, a light, a whistle, and a medical card on you (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear and Part 1 › Planning). Most playbooks below get easier if someone already knows where you are.

Lost in the Wilderness

The Forest Service teaches STOP: stop, think, observe, plan. Its default is to stay put, because panic is the biggest risk and moving makes you harder to find.

StageActions
Warning signsLandmarks stop matching your map. You have not seen a trail marker in a while. Light is fading. Act at the first doubt, not after an hour of guessing.
First hourDo STOP. Check yourself for injury. Put on layers. Get out of wind and rain. Try your phone from a high, open spot, and save its battery. Put a whistle, mirror, or bright cloth within reach.
First dayStay unless the conditions in Figure 2 say to move. Build shelter before dark. Drink and treat water. Make your spot large and bright from the air. If you must go, leave a note with your destination and condition, and mark your route.
First weekKeep the same routine: shelter, water, signals, rest. Do not follow a creek downstream; the Park Service calls it among the most dangerous routes in some mountains. Use the Forest Service's stream-downhill idea only as a last resort and with care.

Stranded Vehicle in Winter

Your car is shelter and a bright target. Stay with it unless safety is clearly close. The two killers are cold and carbon monoxide.

StageActions
Warning signsA winter storm warning, whiteouts, or an icy road. Best move: do not travel. If you must, carry a winter kit, a full tank, and a charged phone.
First hourCall 911 or roadside help, or text if your signal is weak. Turn on hazards. Tie bright cloth to the antenna. Follow Figure 3 before every engine start.
First dayPut on every layer. Use blankets from the kit. Move arms and legs often. Sip water and eat. Raise the hood after the snow stops so plows and rescuers can see you.
First weekKeep the same routine: warmth, fuel discipline, signals, and rest. Leave the car only if you can see a safe place close by, and use the stay-or-go test in Part 1 › Priorities first.

Warning

A blocked tailpipe can fill the cabin with carbon monoxide, which you cannot see or smell. Headache, dizziness, or weakness means get into fresh air now.

Stranded in the Desert

Sun and sweat are the threat. The Park Service and the Army both say to drink the water you have and ration your sweat, not your water.

StageActions
Warning signsWater low, hot day, no shade, a long way from help. Rising thirst, headache, or dark urine means act now.
First hourGet into shade. Stay with your vehicle if you have one; it is easier to spot. Keep your clothes on. Sit at least 12 inches (30 cm) off hot ground. Put signals out.
First dayBuild a shade shelter: at least two layers of cloth, held 12 to 18 inches (30 to 45 cm) above you, outer layer white. Move only at dawn or dusk, and only if you are sure your water will get you there. Do not eat if water is short.
First weekStay put and signal. Hiking needs at least 1 gallon (3.8 L) per day, and more in heat. Do not camp in a dry wash because flash floods come from far away. Digging costs sweat (see Part 2 › Water).

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Save your water and sip it. Fact: The Army and the Park Service say to drink it and save your sweat instead. Rationing in the heat leads to heat illness.

Grid-Down at Home

The power fails, and maybe water, phones, and fuel too. Plan in three time frames. The Red Cross plans for 2 weeks at home.

StageActions
Warning signsStorm forecasts, grid alerts, or a sudden outage that lasts past a few minutes. If someone needs powered medical equipment, move to a place with power or use backup batteries.
First hourKeep fridge and freezer doors shut. Use battery lights, not candles. Unplug fridges and electronics so a surge does not damage them. Start a battery or hand-crank radio. Never heat with a gas stove or oven.
First dayAn unopened fridge holds cool about 4 hours, a full freezer about 48 hours (a half-full one about 24). Eat fridge food first. Know that your water heater tank is a water source. Run a generator only outdoors. Check on neighbors.
First weekPlan for 2 weeks at home. Water first: at least 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day. Set up a bucket toilet and handwashing. Keep a log. Add a long-term plan for heat, light, and water if power may stay off.

After power returns, the Ready.gov guidance is to discard food held at 40°F (4°C) or warmer for 2 hours or more. If the outage lasted more than a day, discard refrigerated medicines unless the label says they remain usable, and call a doctor or pharmacist for replacements. If no replacement can be had, do not simply go without a life-sustaining medicine such as insulin. Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines explains the FDA guidance that insulin can stay at 59 to 86°F (15 to 30°C) for a limited time; follow the product label and ask a pharmacist as soon as you can.

Flood

StageActions
Warning signsA flood watch or warning, heavy rain, rising streams. Flash floods can come from rain miles upstream.
First hourGo to high ground now. Never walk, swim, or drive through floodwater, and never drive around a barricade. Turn around, don't drown.
First dayStay out of floodwater: it can be electrified and can carry sewage and sharp debris. Do not touch electrical equipment while wet.
First weekReturn only when officials say so. Throw out food that got wet or warm. Dry the house in 24 to 48 hours or assume mold. Wear boots, gloves, eye protection, and an N95 respirator to clean.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Flood and Flash Flood).

Wildfire

StageActions
Warning signsSmoke or ash nearby, local fire alerts. You may get no formal order, so leave if you feel threatened.
First hourGrab your go-kit and leave by your planned route. Follow officials. The sources used here give no safe tactic for being trapped, so do not wait to test one.
First dayKeep away from smoke. Check local alerts for road closures and shelters. Take medicines and records with you.
First weekGo home only when officials say it is safe. Avoid hot ash and burned trees. Clean up in gloves, long clothes, goggles, and an N95 respirator.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Wildfire, defensible space).

Tornado

StageActions
Warning signsA tornado watch means be ready. A warning means go now. Tornadoes can form within minutes and at night, so rely on alerts.
First hourGo to a storm shelter, basement, or windowless interior room on the lowest floor. Cover your head. Leave mobile homes for a sturdy building. Do not shelter under an overpass.
First dayWait for the all-clear. Wear sturdy shoes. Watch for glass, nails, and downed lines. Use flashlights, not candles.
First weekText family instead of calling. Follow local officials. Prepare for power loss (see Grid-Down at Home).

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Tornado).

Hurricane

StageActions
Warning signsA hurricane watch comes about 48 hours before tropical-storm-force winds, and a warning about 36 hours before. You get days of notice, so use them.
First hourIf ordered to evacuate, go at once on official routes. Water, not wind, is usually the deadliest hazard. Do not stay in a surge zone because the wind looks mild.
First dayIf you stay, shelter in an interior room away from windows. If flooding traps you, move to the highest floor. Mobile homes and RVs are not safe.
First weekNearly half of hurricane deaths happen after the storm. Stay out of floodwater, keep generators outdoors, 20 feet (6 m) from openings, and drink bottled or boiled water.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Hurricane and Typhoon).

Earthquake

StageActions
Warning signsUsually none. Some regions send a phone alert seconds before strong shaking.
First hourDrop, cover, and hold on. Stay away from windows and exterior walls. Outdoors, move to open ground. Driving, pull over and stay inside. Do not use elevators. The "triangle of life" is a myth.
First dayPut on shoes. Expect aftershocks and repeat drop, cover, hold on. If you smell gas, leave and call from outside, with no lighters or switches. On the coast, go to high ground at once.
First weekCheck the outside of the house before going in. Use flashlights. Plan for days without help (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming).

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Earthquake).

Tsunami

StageActions
Warning signsStrong or long shaking near the coast, a loud roar from the sea, or the water suddenly rising or pulling back. Do not wait for a siren.
First hourMove on foot to high ground or inland. With no local plan, aim for at least 100 feet (30 m) above sea level or 1 mile (1.6 km) inland. Do not go back to look.
First dayA tsunami is a series of waves, and the first is often not the largest. Stay up until officials say it is safe. Waves can last 8 hours or more.
First weekDangerous currents can last for days. Avoid floodwater and damaged buildings. Dry the building within 24 to 48 hours.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Tsunami).

Volcano

StageActions
Warning signsOfficial unrest notices and closed trails or zones. Hazard zones change as an eruption grows.
First hourLeave hazard zones at once and obey closures. Lahars (mudflows) can cut your exit, so go to high ground away from valley floors.
First dayIn ashfall, stay indoors and seal door and window gaps. Outdoors, wear a well-fitted N95 mask and goggles. Avoid driving.
First weekWait until ashfall stops before cleaning, unless a roof must be protected. Roof falls cause many injuries. Keep following official updates.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Volcano).

Blizzard

StageActions
Warning signsA storm watch or warning, strong wind, whiteouts, or freezing rain. Do not travel.
First hourAdd layers and share warmth. Keep flammables 3 feet (1 m) from heaters. Never heat with an oven or stove.
First dayRun generators and stoves outdoors only. Use battery lights. If you feel sick or dizzy while a heater is on, get fresh air.
First weekShovel in short bursts with a partner. If the house is too cold, go to a warming center. Check on older neighbors.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Blizzard and Ice Storm), Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies, and the winter car playbook above.

Extreme Heat

StageActions
Warning signsA heat advisory or warning, or weather hotter and more humid than normal. Older adults, young children, and people with chronic illness are at highest risk.
First hourGet to air conditioning. Drink fluids, wear light clothes, and avoid hard work. A fan alone will not keep you safe in extreme heat. Never leave a child or pet in a parked car.
First dayKnow the signs. Confusion in the heat means heat stroke until proven otherwise. Call 911 and start cooling at once.
First weekHeat illness can still appear when the heat eases. Keep resting and drinking, and check on older neighbors.

See: Part 9 › Natural Disasters (Extreme Heat), Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Pandemic and Quarantine

The details change with each germ, so follow your local health authority first. This playbook gives the basics that hold for most respiratory illnesses. It is not a diagnosis tool.

StageActions
Warning signsAn outbreak notice, symptoms in your area, or a household member with fever, cough, or breathing trouble. Stock food, water, and routine medicines before it spreads.
First hourPut the sick person in a single room with good airflow. Limit their movement. Open windows in shared spaces. Pick one healthy caregiver who has no risk conditions. No visitors.
First dayCaregiver wears a snug mask in the sick room, does not touch it, and swaps it when it is wet or soiled. Clean hands after contact, before eating, and after the toilet. Do not share towels, utensils, or drinks. Watch for warning signs.
First weekDisinfect frequently touched surfaces daily. Wash bedding and clothes in hot water with detergent. Keep fluids up. Stay home until symptoms improve and there has been no fever for at least 24 hours without fever-reducing drugs, then take extra precautions for 5 days.

Warning

Trouble breathing or chest pain needs emergency care right away. Do not wait.

The WHO's cleaning advice is a 1 to 99 mix of bleach (5% sodium hypochlorite) and water. Bag soiled linen without shaking it, and wear gloves when cleaning. Waste goes in a lined bin in the room. The WHO steps above come from interim guidance written in January 2020 for one virus, and its isolation length differs from the CDC's. This guide uses the CDC's return rule for common respiratory illness. For any named outbreak, follow your local health department. For fluids during stomach illness, see the oral rehydration recipe in Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming.

Boat or Small Plane Stranding and Life Rafts

The Coast Guard's rule is plain: the vessel is your best shelter, so stay with it as long as it floats. The same default applies to a downed aircraft on land (see Part 1 › Priorities). Once you are in a raft, stay with the raft.

StageActions
Warning signsEngine failure, flooding, fire, bad weather, or loss of position. Send a radio call early. Put on a life jacket. Gather water, flares, and the beacon.
First hourCall for help on the radio, the Coast Guard's main signal. Go into the raft only if the vessel is sinking or unsafe. Stay tied to it while safe. In cold water, control your breathing first, then get out of the water (see Part 1 › Priorities).
First dayFire one flare as soon as you can and save the rest for when rescuers are in sight. Turn the beacon on and put it outside. Stream the sea anchor. Post a lookout. Raise the canopy, bail, and wring out clothes. Take over-the-counter seasickness medicine, as directed on the label, before anyone is ill.
First weekRation water by a set daily amount; the Army manual says to base it on your supply and the group's condition. Rest and stay shaded to cut your need, and collect rain and dew. If you have no water, do not eat. Keep morale up and keep the lookout.

Warning

Do not drink seawater or urine. Do not skip drinking early, either. The AMSEA training page (a secondary source) says some raft manuals wrongly advise against drinking in the first 24 hours. Follow a ration plan from the start.

Urban Survival and Civil Disruption

The aim is to avoid trouble, stay low-profile, and leave calmly when staying is unsafe. This section covers lawful, defensive steps only.

StageActions
Warning signsPlanned protests that may turn violent, curfews, or alerts. Sign up for local alerts. Plan alternate routes. Keep a household plan with a meeting place and an overnight bag ready.
First hourStay away from the area. At home, lock doors and stay away from windows. If told to shelter in place, use a small interior room. Keep a battery or crank radio. Follow officials' orders.
First dayIf you are in a crowd, move to its edge and out. Do not drive through a crowd; turn onto a side road, or park, lock, and move to a safe spot. If you fall, curl up to protect your head and neck, then get up and move. Stay calm and avoid confrontation.
First weekKeep checking official updates and avoid rumors. Keep cash and supplies (see Grid-Down at Home). Return to an area only when it is safe, and check in with family by text.

Legal note

Follow curfews and the lawful directions of police and officials. Nothing here advises force, blocking roads, or any unlawful act. Leaving is always the safer choice.
