Survival is a ranking problem. You cannot do everything at once, so you must know which threat will hurt you first. This chapter explains the rule of 3s and how rough it really is, the order that military and sea-safety guides use, how that order shifts in cold, desert, wet, sea, jungle, and city settings, and how to decide whether to stay or go.

The Rule of 3s, and How Rough It Is

The rule of 3s is a memory aid. You can last about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather, 3 days without water, and 3 weeks without food. Its real value is the ranking: the fastest threat comes first. It is not a law of physiology. An encyclopedia entry on the rule calls it a rule of thumb that is not scientifically exact. It also traces the "3 days" figure to a 1944 experiment in which two scientists ate only dry food. One quit after 3 days and the other after 4, long before anyone was in danger.

Other "3" rules float around: 3 seconds to react, 3 months without hope. The same encyclopedia entry says these are usually not scientifically accurate either.

Cold water is a different clock

The Army's teaching version, called 1-10-1, says you have about 1 minute to control your breathing after a cold plunge, under 10 minutes before your arms and legs stop working well, and about 1 hour before you pass out from cold. The Army page adds that cold water drains heat 25 to 30 times faster than cold air. A cold-water safety group argues these numbers are too tidy. It says your abilities fade gradually, and that drowning from incapacity is likely before hypothermia. The Army advises wearing a life jacket and getting as much of your body out of the water as you can. The safety group warns that some life jackets do not keep an unconscious person's face clear of the water. Treat the numbers as a warning, not a timer.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: You have exactly 3 days to find water. Fact: You may have hours in desert heat when you are working hard, or more than 3 days in cool weather at rest. Plan for the short end of the range.

The Priority Sequence Used in Courses

The rule of 3s tells you what kills fastest. It does not tell you what to do first, because some fast killers are easy to fix and some slow ones take a day to solve. Survival courses and manuals therefore use ordered lists. The lists do not match each other exactly.

SourceOrderNotes
US Army FM 21-76 (2006 copy)Food, water, shelter, fire, first aid, signalsSays to adjust to your setting. If you are hurt, first aid comes first in any climate.
Multi-Service Survival Manual (Army, Marine Corps, Navy, Air Force)First 24 hours: shelter, water, signals, fire. Second 24 hours: tools, then food.Says to size up your resources before starting any task.
US Coast Guard "7 Steps of Survival" card (boating)Recognize the emergency, take inventory, shelter, signals, water, food, play (keep a positive mind)The boat is your best shelter. Stay with it as long as it floats.

The sources disagree most on food. The Army manual's list puts it first. The joint manual and the Coast Guard push it to the end, which matches the rule of 3s. This guide follows the later view.

The order this guide uses

  1. Stop and think. Panic and haste cause injuries and wrong turns. The Army manual urges you to use all your senses before you act.
  2. Handle life threats. Airway, severe bleeding, and shock come first. If you are hurt, this overrides everything below (see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).
  3. Protect yourself from the weather. Clothing, shelter, and sometimes fire (see Part 2 › Shelter and Part 2 › Fire).
  4. Be findable. Rescue ends most survival stories. Make signals ready early (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).
  5. Secure water. Drink, find, collect, and treat (see Part 2 › Water).
  6. Food and tools. Only after the steps above are settled.

Fire is a tool that serves steps 3, 4, and 5. It warms you, signals, boils water, and dries clothes. That is why it floats in the published lists. Treat the order as a default and change it when the setting demands it, as the next section shows.

Remember "SURVIVAL"

The Army manual offers a memory aid, one letter per habit:

  • Size up the situation: surroundings, your body, your gear.
  • Use all your senses. Do not rush.
  • Remember where you are.
  • Vanquish fear and panic.
  • Improvise.
  • Value living. The will to live matters.
  • Act like the locals: watch what animals and people in the area do.
  • Live by your wits, but learn basic skills now.

Skills first, gadgets second

The manual's last letter makes the point that you learn skills before you need them. Practice building a shelter and lighting a fire in good weather, with a friend nearby.

Priorities by Environment

A priority pyramid shows the order of work. The wide base is what you do first, and the tip is what can wait. Each pyramid is a default. If you are injured, treat the injury first (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Cold

In cold, your body loses heat faster than it makes it. Wet clothing and wind speed this up. Get dry and insulated, then enclose yourself in a shelter. Snow is an excellent insulator, and the joint manual advises an enclosed, insulated shelter, with a vent when you burn anything inside it. The manual also says that if you cannot see your breath inside, the shelter is probably too warm. Melt snow before you drink it, because eating snow chills you. For details, see Part 2 › Shelter, Part 2 › Fire, and Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Hot desert

In the desert, sun and sweat are the enemy. The joint manual suggests a shade shelter with at least two layers of cloth held 12 to 18 inches (30 to 45 cm) above you, the outer layer white. Do not travel unless you are certain your water will get you to your destination. If you must go, move at dawn or dusk.

The sources disagree about rationing. The Army manual says to conserve sweat, limit effort, and ration water. The National Park Service says to drink the water you have rather than rationing it. The safe middle path is to ration your effort, not your drinking, and to remember that digging in the heat costs sweat. In a sandstorm, lie down, protect your nose, mouth, and eyes, and wait for it to pass. For water methods, see Part 2 › Water.

Wet and temperate (rain, mild cold)

Cool rain is a quiet killer. Hypothermia can begin in air between 30 and 60°F (-1 and 16°C) once wind and rain join in. Keep the wet off, keep wind off, and put insulation between you and the ground before you worry about anything else. Rain also gives you drinking water, but it needs care (see Part 2 › Water). Light a fire early while your tinder is still dry (see Part 2 › Fire).

At sea and in a life raft

At sea, the water around you is the danger and the water in it is not drinkable. Do not drink seawater. The Coast Guard card says to treat the boat as your best shelter and to stay with it while it floats, and to rely on the radio as your main signal. It says to secure fresh water, and that without water you should not eat. The joint manual adds that you should collect rain and dew. It also suggests deploying a sea anchor in currents and building shade or a sail in wind.

If you go overboard in cold water, use the first minute to get your breathing under control. Then get as much of your body out of the water as you can. If you cannot, the Army describes the HELP position (knees drawn up, arms around your legs) to save heat. Groups can huddle with linked arms. Handle rescued people gently, because cold survivors can collapse into cardiac arrest after rescue (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Jungle and rain forest

The joint manual's jungle advice leads to this order:

  1. An enclosed, raised shelter. It keeps you off damp ground and away from insects.
  2. Water. Rain, vines, and bamboo can give you water, but the manual says to purify all of it (see Part 2 › Water).
  3. Signals. Get your signals ready, and stay put if you are lost (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).
  4. Movement only in daylight. Avoid thickets and swamps, and part vegetation instead of grabbing it.
  5. Food. Last, and only from sources you know.

Urban disaster

After an earthquake or similar event, the American Red Cross says to wait a moment before you get up and to check for immediate dangers. Put on sturdy shoes, because broken glass and debris cover the ground. Care for your own injuries first, then help others. If you smell gas, leave right away and call emergency services. Do not use matches, lighters, or switches until you are sure there is no leak. Use flashlights, not candles. If you are near the coast, move to higher ground without waiting for a warning. The Red Cross also advises a 3-day go-kit and a 2-week supply at home. Water is usually your first supply gap (see Part 2 › Water and Part 9 › Natural Disasters).

First 10 Minutes, First Hour, First Day

Checklists beat memory when you are scared. These follow the logic of the Army and joint manuals: think, handle life threats, protect yourself, then plan. Adjust the lists to your setting.

First 10 minutes

  1. Stop. Breathe slowly. Do not run or move until you have looked around.
  2. Scan for danger: fire, rising water, traffic, loose rock, bad air, downed power lines.
  3. Check yourself, then others. Look for blocked breathing and severe bleeding (see Part 0 › Red Pages).
  4. Get out of the wind, rain, or sun, even if only behind a rock or tree.
  5. Count your people and gear.

First hour

  1. Treat injuries and watch for shock (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).
  2. Put on every piece of clothing you own in cold, or cover up in the sun. Get dry.
  3. Decide stay or go (next section).
  4. Start your shelter or shade.
  5. Put a signal within reach: whistle, mirror, light, phone, bright cloth.
  6. Mark where you are, so others can find you and you can find your gear.

First day

  1. Drink what you need, then find and treat more water.
  2. Build a fire if you are cold, wet, or need to boil water.
  3. Make your signals big and bright. Plan when you will use them.
  4. Take inventory and sort your gear.
  5. Rest. Check each other for cold, heat, dehydration, and clear thinking.

Stay or Go?

This is the hardest early choice. The joint manual's default in a non-combat setting is to stay with your vehicle or aircraft. It says to leave only when one of these is true: danger forces you to; you know where you are, have a known destination, and can get there; you can reach water, food, shelter, or help; or you are convinced rescue is not coming. The National Park Service tells lost hikers to stay put, because moving makes them harder to find. Move only when staying puts your life in immediate danger (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

When going is right

  • A flood, rockfall, fire, or lightning is about to hit you.
  • You know exactly where help or water is and you can reach it with what you carry.
  • You are convinced rescue is not coming.

If you go

  1. Leave a note at your starting point: destination, route, your condition, and your supplies.
  2. Take water and your most useful gear. Decide what to leave behind.
  3. Travel in daylight, and in hot country at dawn or dusk.
  4. Mark your route. Rest often. Stop when the light fades.

For maps, bearings, and walking safely, see Part 9 › Maps & Compass and Part 9 › Travel & Movement. For moving an injured person, see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions.

Common Deadly Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Food is the first priority. Fact: Hunger is slow. Water, warmth, and being found come first.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Keep walking and you will find your way out. Fact: Wandering lost wastes water, tires you, and makes you harder to find. Walk only when the chart in Figure 8 says to.

Do not

  • Skip the first aid check because you feel fine. Shock and adrenaline hide injuries.
  • Wait for the rule of 3s to run out before you act. Shelter and signals come early.
  • Drink seawater, urine, or alcohol (see Part 2 › Water).
  • Eat snow to get water in the cold.
  • Leave a safe vehicle or camp without a note and a clear reason.
  • Burn a fuel fire in an enclosed shelter without venting it.

Train before you need it

Take a wilderness first aid course and practice shelter, fire, and signaling skills with a partner in good weather. Tell someone your plan before every trip.
