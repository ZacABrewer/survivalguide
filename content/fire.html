Fire gives you warmth, dry clothes, hot food, safe water, and a way to signal for help. It can also burn you, poison you with smoke, or start a wildfire. This chapter shows you how to build a fire from the first spark to the last cold ash, and how to keep every step safe.

The Fire Triangle

A fire needs three things: heat, fuel, and oxygen (air). Take away any one and the fire dies. The U.S. Army survival manual says the same thing and adds that you learn the right balance of the three only by practice.

You use this idea in two ways. To build a fire, supply all three in the right order. To put one out, take one away. Water removes heat. Stirring ash exposes hidden embers to water. Smothering with dirt cuts off air, but the National Park Service warns that dirt kicked on coals can insulate them and keep them hot.

Tinder, Kindling and Fuel

Think of fire as a ladder. Each rung is bigger than the last, and each one has to be burning well before you add the next.

  • Tinder catches from a spark or small flame. It must be bone dry and fluffy or thin.
  • Kindling is small sticks. It turns a tiny flame into enough heat to light bigger wood. REI defines it as sticks under about 1 inch (2.5 cm) around.
  • Fuel is larger wood that burns slowly and keeps the fire going.

Gather before you light

Fires burn out faster than you expect, and you cannot leave a hungry fire to hunt for sticks. Before you strike a spark, pile up three heaps within arm's reach: a big handful of tinder, an armload of pencil-size kindling, and a stack of thumb-to-wrist-size fuel. A common rule of thumb is to make the kindling pile bigger than you think you need, then fuel for the whole time you plan to burn. No source gives an exact amount, so judge by your goal. A cooking fire needs less than an all-night warming fire.

Where gathering is allowed, take only dead, downed wood from the ground. Do not break branches off living or standing trees. Leave No Trace and the Forest Service both ask for this, and green wood burns poorly anyway. Many national parks ban collecting wood, so buy firewood near where you camp. Hauling it from far away can spread tree pests.

Tinders: Wild and Made

Natural tinders

  • Birch bark. It contains a water-resistant, flammable compound, so it lights even when damp. Take loose, peeling strips or bark from dead trees. Never peel bark all the way around a living trunk (girdling), which can kill the tree.
  • Fatwood. This is resin-soaked pine, usually from the base of dead stumps and old, gray wood. It lights easily, resists wind, and burns hot. Shave it or split it thin.
  • Cedar bark. Scraped inner and outer bark fibers form a fluffy nest. Take it from dead trees, not living ones.
  • Cattail fluff. It flares from a lighter or sparks but burns out fast. Have slower tinder ready to take over.
  • Dry grass. Twist it into a bird's nest. Use standing dead grass, not grass lying on damp ground.
  • Punky (rotted) dry wood. Soft, dry, crumbly wood from dead trees can take a coal and smolder. A California Cadet Corps survival handout lists the rotted parts of dead trees as tinder.
  • Inner bark, fine shavings, dead evergreen needles, and sawdust. All appear in military survival tinder lists.

Tree fungus

Fomes fomentarius is called tinder fungus or horse's hoof fungus. It grows as a hoof-shaped bracket, often on birch, and is silvery gray to nearly black. Dead, dry pieces can be shaved or ground into dust for tinder. Thin chips can stand in for char cloth with flint and steel. It smolders for hours, so people have used it to carry fire. Chaga (Inonotus obliquus) is a different fungus that also grows on birch. A large piece smolders for hours, and dry powder takes a spark.

Warning

Do not use a fungus as tinder unless you are sure what it is. Bracket fungi look alike. Use fungus only as fire tinder. Never eat it. Learn the local species from an expert before you rely on it.

Made tinders

  • Cotton balls and petroleum jelly. Rub jelly into a cotton ball and pull it apart to fluff it. Keep a few in a zip bag. Outdoor Life lists jelly-soaked cotton as a good wet-weather starter.
  • Char cloth. This is cloth turned to black, spark-hungry char. Use 100% cotton, cut in small pieces, packed loosely in a tin with a tiny vent hole in the lid. Put the tin in a fire for about 10 to 20 minutes. When smoke stops coming from the hole, take the tin out and let it cool before you open it. Synthetic fibers do not char the same way, so avoid them. Store char cloth dry and airtight.

Warning

If the char-cloth tin flames up, it has too much air. Start over with a smaller hole. Use tongs or gloves. The tin gets very hot.

Ways to Light It

Carry at least two ways to make fire. Keep matches and lighters in a waterproof container.

Lighter and matches

These are the best tools. Use them first. Always light with a match or lighter, never with gasoline or other flammable liquids. A liquid fuel can flash back and burn you badly.

Ferro rod

A ferrocerium rod makes hot sparks, not a flame. The sparks must land on very dry, fluffy tinder.

  1. Scrape off the black coating at the end of the rod until you see bare metal.
  2. Make a tinder nest. Dry grass, fluff, and char cloth work well.
  3. Put the rod's tip right next to the nest.
  4. Press the squared spine of a knife (never the sharp edge) against the rod at about 45 degrees.
  5. Hold the striker still and pull the rod back with firm strokes, so the sparks land on the tinder instead of knocking it away.
  6. When something glows or flames, nurse it gently with your breath.

Flint and steel

Strike a hard flint or quartz edge with a piece of high-carbon steel. Stainless steel does not make good sparks. The sparks land on char cloth (or thin fungus chips). When the char glows, set it into a tinder nest and blow.

Sunlight and a lens

On a bright, sunny day, a magnifying glass, a camera lens, or binocular lens can focus a hot dot on dark, fine tinder. Hold it still until the tinder smolders. It does not work at night or under cloud.

Battery

The Army manual says you can run wires from both terminals of a battery and touch the bare ends together by the tinder. The spark can light fine tinder. Do this only with a battery you can spare and never near fuel fumes.

Fire piston

A fire piston is a small tube with a plunger. Slamming the plunger in squeezes the air so fast that it gets hot enough to light char cloth or amadou (fungus felt) on the plunger tip. Pull the plunger out right away and tip the glowing tinder into your nest.

Friction fire: bow drill

A bow drill is the most reliable friction method for most people, but it takes practice. Try it at home before you need it.

Wood

Use dead, dry, medium-soft, non-resinous wood. Sources list cedar, cottonwood, willow, basswood, aspen, poplar, juniper, buckeye, spruce, and yucca (especially a dry flower or seed stalk for a spindle). A cottonwood fireboard is praised as one of the easiest. Red oak and other hard woods are very hard to use. Pine pitch lubricates and makes it harder, so beginners should skip pine.

Sizes vary by source. One teacher uses a fireboard about 12 in (30 cm) long and 1 in (2.5 cm) thick, and a spindle about 8 in (20 cm) long and 1 in (2.5 cm) wide. The Army manual gives a spindle about 3/4 in (2 cm) by 10 in (25 cm), and it pairs a harder spindle with a softwood board. Treat all of these as starting points, not exact sizes. Use what fits your hand and set a straight spindle with a dull, rounded tip on the bottom and a tapered tip on top.

Technique

  1. Make a tinder bundle about the size of a softball, then lay a bark or leaf ember catch on flat ground.
  2. Carve a small starter hole in the fireboard about an inch (2.5 cm) from its edge.
  3. Wrap the bowstring once around the spindle and seat the spindle in the hole.
  4. Kneel with one foot on the fireboard. Brace the wrist of your pressing hand against your shin to keep the spindle straight.
  5. Press the bearing block onto the top of the spindle. Wax or soap in the socket helps the spindle spin without heat there.
  6. Bow with slow, smooth, full strokes until smoke appears.
  7. Stop. Cut a pie-slice notch from the edge into the hole, with the point just short of the center. Set the ember catch under it.
  8. Bow again, faster, with more downward pressure. Dark dust and steady smoke mean you are close. Keep going even after the smoke rises.
  9. Stop. Let the dust pile glow. Tip the coal into the center of the tinder bundle and blow gently until it flames.

Other friction methods

  • Hand drill. The same spindle and board, but you spin the spindle between your palms. It is harder than a bow drill and needs very dry, straight stalks and lots of practice.
  • Fire plough. Rub a hardwood shaft back and forth along a groove in a softwood base until dust ignites.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Rubbing two sticks together will quickly make fire. Fact: Friction fire needs the right dry wood, good technique, and often many tries. Learn it before an emergency. Treat it as a backup, not your first plan.

Star fire

Dakota fire hole

Dig a hole about 1 ft (30 cm) deep and wide. Dig a second, slanted tunnel from the upwind side down to the bottom of the pit. Air flows in through the tunnel, so the fire burns hot and small and hides light and smoke. The Army manual warns that it needs a large vent on the upwind side.

Warning

Check fire rules before you dig. Many places ban digging fire pits. Leave No Trace suggests a mound fire on mineral soil where there is no ring.

Fire in Rain, Snow and Wind

Rain and wet wood

  • Look for dead twigs under evergreens. They stay drier, and their pitch burns well.
  • Peel off wet bark. Dry wood is often just underneath.
  • Split kindling and fuel lengthwise to reach the dry center. Half-sticks light faster than whole ones.
  • Build a tall, narrow lay. A cone about a foot (30 cm) tall lets heat rise and dry the wood above. Teepees do well here.
  • Use extra tinder and a backup bundle. Add jelly-soaked cotton, fatwood, or birch bark.
  • Build a slight mound, not a pit. Pits fill with water.
  • Dry damp wood next to the fire before you feed it in.

Feather sticks

A feather stick is a dry stick with thin curls shaved up one side but left attached. The curls catch a flame like tinder, even when the outside of the stick is wet. Make several from dead, dry softwood.

Snow and soggy ground

Build a platform so the fire does not sink and melt into the ground. The Army manual says to lay several layers of green logs, about wrist size, with each layer crossing the one below. Use a top-down lay on snow or wet ground.

Wind

  • Pick a spot sheltered from wind and well away from open fields and gusty ridges.
  • Use a lean-to lay, a Dakota hole, or a trench-style fire.
  • Build a windbreak with rocks (dry, safe ones), logs, a pack, or snow blocks. Keep it a safe distance from the flames.
  • Light from the upwind side.

Warning

Strong wind can carry sparks into dry brush. If the wind is strong or the ground is dry, do not light a fire.

Carrying Fire

If you have a good fire and must move, you can carry it. Pair a glowing coal with something that smolders slowly, such as punky wood, dried tinder fungus, or a pine cone. Wrap it in a damp (not dripping) material like wet moss, then check it often. It can last for hours, but you must blow on it and adjust the wrapping from time to time. Another route is to light the edge of a dry bracket fungus from a coal and carry it smoldering in a fireproof container, such as a metal tin. Popular Science describes this lasting an hour or two. Practice while you still have a fire, so you learn how long yours lasts.

At the next camp, set the ember in dry tinder and blow it into flame.

Warning

Never put a live coal inside a pack or pocket, or take it into a vehicle. Carry it in your hand in a container that can't burn through, away from your clothes.

Using Your Fire

Cooking

Cook over coals, not flames. Flames burn the outside and leave the middle raw. Let a log-cabin or long fire burn down to a bed of glowing coals, then cook on a rack or green sticks. Never use oleander or other poisonous plants for skewers or fuel. Dakota holes and long fires are good for cooking pots.

Warmth and reflector walls

A fire warms the side facing it. A reflector wall bounces heat back. Stack logs or green sticks on the far side of the fire so the heat comes back toward you and your shelter.

Build the wall from green logs, stones that are dry and safe (see below), or stacked wood. Make it as wide as your shelter opening.

Drying gear

Hang wet clothes on a rack of sticks near the fire, not over it. Watch them. Synthetic fabrics melt, and wool and cotton can scorch. Dry damp boots slowly and keep them back from the heat.

Signaling

Fire gives off light at night and smoke by day, and both can be seen from far away. Keep extra fuel ready so you can build the fire up when you hear or see a search craft. Plan signals before you need them (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

Safety First

Before you light

  1. Check for burn bans and fire restrictions with the land agency, park office, or local fire department. Do not build a fire where it is banned.
  2. Do not build in dry, hazardous, or windy conditions.
  3. Choose a flat, open spot with nothing hanging overhead. Smokey Bear's guidance asks for overhead clearance of at least 3 times the flame height.
  4. Use an existing fire ring if there is one. Otherwise clear a circle at least 10 ft (3 m) across, down to bare dirt.
  5. Keep the fire at least 15 ft (4.5 m) from tents, plants, and low branches.
  6. Have water and a shovel or digging tool within reach.
  7. Keep the fire small. Never leave it alone. Keep children and pets back.

Legal note

Rules differ by land agency and change with fire danger. The Forest Service warns that an escaped campfire can bring a citation and fine, and you can be billed for the cost of fighting the fire, which can run to hundreds of thousands of dollars. In a real emergency you may need a fire to survive, but check local rules whenever you can.

Putting it out: drown, stir, feel

  1. Drown. Pour plenty of water on the fire until the hissing stops. Pour slowly. Steam can scald and can blow ash and smoke up at you. Dirt alone is not enough.
  2. Stir. Mix the water into the ash, embers, and soil with a stick or shovel. Scrape logs and sticks so you can reach hidden embers.
  3. Drown again. Add more water, until everything is cool.
  4. Feel. Hold the back of your hand just above the ashes. If you feel any heat, add more water, stir, and check again. Do not leave until it is cool to the touch.

Gray or white coals can hold heat for hours and flare in a gust. If there is no water, spread the coals with a tool and stir sand or dirt through them until the heat is gone. Don't just kick dirt over the pile. That can bank the coals so they stay hot. The California State Parks guide adds that covered embers can keep burning underground for hours.

If the fire escapes the ring or embers land outside it, call 911 right away if you can.

Carbon monoxide in tents and shelters

Burning wood, charcoal, and fuel gives off carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you can't see or smell. It can kill you in your sleep. The CDC says burning charcoal of any color gives off CO, and that you should never use a portable gas camp stove indoors. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says never to use fuel-burning heaters or lanterns while sleeping in tents, campers, or vehicles. The California Poison Control System says not to use fuel-burning camping gear inside a tent. The Army survival manual also warns that fires in shelters can cause CO poisoning.

Signs: headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion. It can feel like the flu. People who are asleep or drunk can die before they notice any signs. If someone has these signs, get everyone out into fresh air and call 911.

Do not

  • Do not bring a hot fire, coals, a stove, or a lantern into a closed tent or snow cave to sleep.
  • Do not run a fire in a shelter without a large vent for smoke.
  • Do not sleep with a fire burning in a small, sealed space.

What never to burn

  • Poison ivy, poison oak, and poison sumac. The oil in these plants (urushiol) rides in the smoke. The CDC's National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) warns that breathing this smoke can irritate the lungs and cause severe allergic breathing problems. The Alabama Extension adds that it can swell the airways, make breathing hard, and can even kill. Never burn them or a pile with them in it. If you get a cough, wheeze, or trouble breathing from smoke, get medical help at once.
  • Oleander (Nerium oleander). All parts are poisonous. The Arizona and Missouri poison centers both list smoke from burning oleander as a way to be poisoned, and NC State Extension says not to burn it. Do not burn it or cook on sticks of it. Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222 (U.S.).
  • Treated, painted, or pressure-treated wood. Older pressure-treated lumber (CCA, used in U.S. homes before 2004) contains arsenic. California State Parks says never to burn pressure-treated wood in open fires. The National Pesticide Information Center says no treated wood should ever be burned, because the smoke and ash can be toxic. Weathered boards are hard to identify, so skip scrap wood when in doubt.
  • Plastics, batteries, foil, cans, plywood, and coated cardboard. They give off toxic gas or leave toxic residue.

Rocks around a fire

Do not use wet or porous rocks around or in a fire. The Army survival manual warns that they may explode when heated. A U.S. Air Force safety article explains why: water soaked into a rock turns to steam, and the pressure can crack the rock apart and throw pieces at people nearby. Stones taken from rivers, creeks, or wet ground are the most likely to be waterlogged. If you are unsure about a rock, leave it out. An existing metal fire ring or a ring of bare mineral soil is safer.
