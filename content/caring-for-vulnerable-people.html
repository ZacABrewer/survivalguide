Babies, children, older adults, people with disabilities, people who depend on medicines or powered equipment, and pets all need more than a basic kit. They need earlier plans, special supplies, and people who know what to do. This chapter shows how to prepare for each group, using guidance from CDC, FEMA, FDA, and other agencies. Make these plans before trouble starts, because most of them cannot be improvised.

Plan now, with your care team

This chapter is general planning guidance, not medical advice. For any medicine, device, or condition, talk to your doctor, pharmacist, or equipment supplier now. Ask them what to do in a long outage or evacuation. Call 911 for a life-threatening emergency if phones work.

Infants

A baby cannot ask for water, tell you it is too hot, or wait for help. The main dangers in a disaster are unsafe feeding, dirty water, and unsafe sleep or temperature. Plan for all three.

Feeding: breast milk first, then ready-to-feed formula

The CDC calls breastfeeding the safest way to feed a baby in an emergency. Plan to keep nursing through a disaster. Stress and travel do not make it unsafe. If power is out, hand expression or a manual pump still works, so practice hand expression before you need it. Wash pump parts with soap and clean water between uses. Ask responders or shelter staff for a clean place to pump and to store milk.

If you use formula, ready-to-feed liquid is the safest choice. It is sterile and needs no water. Unopened containers need no refrigeration, so store them in a cool, dry place and check dates. The CDC says powdered formula is not sterile. That matters most for babies under 2 months, premature babies, and babies with weak immune systems. For those babies, use ready-to-feed if you can.

Mixing powdered formula safely

Use this order every time. If you have no safe way to clean bottles, skip them and feed from a disposable cup.

The CDC adds three rules. Use prepared formula within 2 hours of mixing and within 1 hour of starting the feeding. If the power is out and you cannot refrigerate, make fresh formula for each feeding and throw away leftovers. Throw away cans that are dented, rusted, puffy, or have a broken seal, or that touched unsafe water. For ways to make water safe, see Part 2 › Water.

Do not

  • Do not make homemade formula.
  • Do not add extra water or extra powder. Either can harm the baby.
  • Do not put formula in a container that ever held chemicals or toxic products.
  • Do not microwave a bottle. It makes hot spots that can burn the mouth.
  • Do not reuse a bottle you cannot clean. Use a disposable cup and throw it away.

After a flood, throw away nipples, pacifiers, and other hard-to-clean items that touched flood water. Keep a stock of disposable cups, plates, and spoons for feeding.

If the power is out, formula that was opened or prepared and kept in the fridge should be thrown out after 4 hours, or if the fridge went above 40°F (4°C). Expressed breast milk in a cooler with frozen ice packs lasts up to 24 hours. Frozen milk that still has ice crystals can be refrozen. Milk that has fully thawed cannot be refrozen and should be used within 24 hours. For fridge and freezer rules, see Part 5 › Food Safety.

Diapers, sleep, and temperature

  • Diapers. Keep several days of diapers and wipes in the go-bag and check the size every six months, as the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) kit advises. Hygiene tips are in Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation.
  • Safe sleep. The CDC, citing the AAP, says to put a baby on the back for every sleep, including naps. Use a firm, flat surface with a fitted sheet, and keep soft items such as pillows, blankets, and bumpers out of the sleep space. Keep the baby's sleep space in your room, ideally for at least the first 6 months. Keep these rules even in a crowded shelter, and ask staff for a crib or a quiet corner.
  • Overheating. Do not cover the baby's head or let them get too hot. Sweating or a hot chest suggests overheating, so check the chest, not just the hands. Remove a layer if so. For cold and heat dangers in general, see Part 1 › Clothing & Layering and Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Stretching formula with extra water saves supplies in a crisis. Fact: The CDC lists too much or too little water as a danger. A diluted bottle can make a baby sick. If you are running low, get help from shelter staff, a hospital, or local relief groups.

Children

Children need their own supplies and a plan for when they are away from you. For how children react to a crisis, and how to help them cope, see Part 1 › Survival Mindset & Psychology.

Kits and comfort

  • Pack a go-bag a child can carry. Include extra clothes, a favorite toy or blanket, and a night light. The AAP notes children may be very upset if they lose a comfort item.
  • Pack overnight supplies, as the CDC suggests: diapers if needed, special food or formula, and extra essential medicines such as inhalers.
  • For a child with special health needs, keep a go-bag by the family kit. Fill out the AAP Emergency Information Form and keep a copy on your phone. Send extra medicine and battery packs for medical equipment with the child, if the school agrees.

ID cards and reunification

  1. Make a card for each child's backpack, and a copy for your wallet. List the child's name, birth date, medical conditions, and allergies. Add your phone number and two emergency contacts. The CDC offers a Backpack Emergency Card.
  2. Teach children their full name, your names and phone numbers, and an out-of-state contact. Teach them when to call 911.
  3. Pick two meeting places: one near home, and one farther away. Tell children what to do if you are separated.
  4. Set a family password. Tell children to leave only with an adult who knows it.
  5. Keep a recent photo of each child on your phone and in your wallet. If a child is lost, tell police age, height, weight, hair and eye color, and clothes.
  6. In a crowd, write your phone number on the child's clothes or on a bracelet. The AAP also suggests writing it on a forearm in marker if you fear separation.

Schools and child care

Ask the school or child-care center, in writing if you can: How will you tell parents about an emergency? Where do parents pick up children? Who may collect my child? How are medicines given, and by whom? Review the plan every year. Schools have their own reunification plans, so follow the school's pickup instructions.

If you are separated, the CDC points to the Red Cross Reconnecting Families program. To report a missing or found child, it lists the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children hotline, 1-800-THE-LOST.

Pregnant people

If you are pregnant, add your prenatal records, due date, and your doctor's and hospital's numbers to your go-bag. Plan your route to the hospital and a backup route. Tell your support network your due date. Talk to your prenatal provider about what to do if you go into labor and cannot reach care. For the steps of an emergency birth and newborn care, see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care.

Older Adults

Many older adults live alone, take several medicines, or have trouble moving, hearing, or seeing. Each of these can turn a short outage into a danger. For emotional care, see Part 1 › Survival Mindset & Psychology.

  • Mobility. Practice getting out by every exit. Keep a spare cane or walker. If stairs are the only way out, ask your building's manager or local emergency office about plans for residents who cannot use them. Leave early, because slow exits are the hardest in a rush. See Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions.
  • Heat and cold. Older bodies can have a harder time with temperature. Check on older people often in heat waves and cold snaps. Ask the pharmacist whether any of your medicines make heat or cold harder to handle. Signs and treatment are in Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.
  • Medication list. Keep a current list (Figure 4) and extra copies. Pack the medicines themselves, in labeled bottles.
  • Hearing and vision aids. Keep spare hearing-aid batteries in the go-bag. Store aids and glasses in the same place every night so you can grab them. Keep a spare pair of glasses if you can.
  • Check-ins. Agree on a daily check-in with a neighbor or relative, by phone or text, and on a signal such as a card in the window.

Dementia and wandering

The Alzheimer's Association says about 60% of people with dementia will wander at some point. Changes in routine and new places raise the risk, and disasters bring both. Keep the person with you or with other trusted people. Do not leave them alone.

  • Pack an ID bracelet and clothing tags, and a recent photo.
  • Pack a comfort item, easy-to-change clothes, and slip-on or Velcro shoes. Add incontinence supplies if needed.
  • Pack medicines with a list, doctor contacts, and copies of key papers such as power of attorney and insurance cards.
  • Tell shelter or hotel staff about the diagnosis so they can help. The Association's helpline is 800-272-3900.
  • Stay calm yourself. The Association notes this may ease the person's anxiety.

If the person goes missing

Search close to home first, since most wanderers are found within about half a mile (0.8 km). The Association says to call 911 if the person is not found after 15 minutes. Call sooner in cold, heat, or storms, or near water or roads. Tell the police the person has Alzheimer's or dementia and is a vulnerable adult.

People with Disabilities

Disabilities differ, so plans must too. FEMA's core advice is to build a personal support network, plan for your equipment, and tell people how to help you. A plan that works for most people may fail for you if it ignores your needs.

Support network

FEMA advises you to name people who can help, and keep a contact list in a watertight container in your kit. Tell them where your supplies are. Give at least one person a key. Show them how to use and move your equipment, such as how to fold your wheelchair or work your lift.

Mobility devices

  • If you use a power wheelchair, keep a lightweight manual chair as a backup. Teach others how to run yours.
  • Know your chair's size and weight and whether it folds, so transport can be arranged.
  • Keep a spare cane or walker. If you must leave your chair, bring your seat cushion.
  • Write down model details for each device and where it came from (Medicaid, Medicare, or private insurance). This helps you replace it if it is lost or destroyed.

Communication access

  • Deaf or hard of hearing. FEMA lists a weather radio with a text display and flashing alert, spare hearing-aid batteries, a TTY, and pen and paper.
  • Blind or low vision. Label supplies in Braille or large print. Keep a supply list as an audio file or on a flash drive. Include a Braille or Deaf-Blind communication device if you use one.
  • Speech or communication disability. Write down the best way for others to communicate with you.
  • Wear medical alert tags. A phone's medical ID screen can also hold your access needs.

Shelters, transport, and registries

  • The CDC says accessible transport may not be available in an emergency. Ask your paratransit provider or local government ahead of time who provides emergency transport. FEMA suggests planning accessible rides to clinics.
  • When you reach a shelter, tell the staff your needs and how others can help. Shelters must be accessible under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA).
  • Some states and cities run voluntary registries for people who need help in disasters. Florida's is one example. Ask your local emergency management office or health department what exists near you. A public-health brief warns that registries can give a false sense of security, so your own plan must come first.
  • Ask your electric utility whether it keeps a list of customers who rely on powered medical equipment, and sign up if so.

Legal note

FEMA says disaster aid is available regardless of disability. To report discrimination, call FEMA at 800-621-3362 (TTY 800-462-7585).

Planning Around Essential Medicines and Equipment

If you or someone you care for cannot go a few days without a medicine or a machine, that need is your first priority. Most fixes cost little if you plan ahead.

Your medicine list and extra supply

Keep one list of every medicine, in several places: your wallet, your go-bag, a photo on your phone, and with a trusted contact. Update it at every doctor visit. Talk to your doctor and pharmacist now about getting an extra supply for emergencies and how to handle refills. Ask about your insurer's rules too. For general medicine storage and expiration, see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines.

Insulin and power outages

Insulin can lose strength with heat and freezing. The FDA's emergency guidance says that in an emergency, insulin vials or cartridges, opened or unopened, can be kept at 59°F to 86°F (15°C to 30°C) for up to 28 days and still work. Normal storage in the fridge is about 36°F to 46°F (2°C to 8°C). Check the exact limits for your own product and pen on its label or ask your pharmacist.

  • Keep insulin as cool as you can, but never frozen. The FDA says not to use insulin that has been frozen. Do not set it against ice or a frozen pack.
  • Keep it out of direct sun and heat, such as a closed car in summer.
  • If you have no choice, insulin that got hotter than 86°F (30°C) may still work, but less well the longer it stays hot. Replace it as soon as you can.
  • Check your blood sugar often. The CDC says to tell shelter staff if it runs too high or too low, and to see your doctor as soon as the emergency ends.
  • If you cannot get your usual insulin, do not switch types or amounts on your own. Work with a doctor or pharmacist. The FDA has emergency switching guidance for them. Seek medical help promptly.

Dialysis and other life-sustaining treatment

FEMA advises that if you rely on dialysis or similar treatment, you should know the location and hours of more than one facility. Ask your care team now for an emergency diet plan and what to do if you miss treatment. This chapter does not give diet or treatment details, because they depend on your condition. Tell your support network your schedule.

Power-dependent equipment and oxygen

Oxygen concentrators, ventilators, suction, and similar devices stop when the power does. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services runs the emPOWER program to help communities find at-risk people. Its data goes only to health officials, so you still need your own plan. Work through three levels (Figure 5), and ask your supplier for each.

Oxygen and fire

Oxygen makes fires burn faster and hotter. Ask your supplier for the exact safe distance from flames, heat, and sparks for your equipment, and follow it. Use battery lights, not candles, during an outage. Ask in advance whether you can get backup cylinders and how long they last. This chapter did not verify specific distances or run times, so use your supplier's figures.

Do not

  • Do not run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage to power equipment or heat the room.
  • Do not wait until the battery is nearly dead to leave.
  • Do not change your medicines or equipment settings on your own because you are stressed.

Pets and Service Animals

Pets are family, and people who will not leave them behind often delay leaving. FEMA's advice is simple: if officials order an evacuation, your pets leave too. Plan for that now. After Hurricane Katrina, Congress passed the Pets Evacuation and Transportation Standards (PETS) Act, and FEMA carries out its work.

Kits and carriers

FEMA says to keep two kits: a larger one for staying put, and a light one for leaving.

  • Keep copies of registration and vaccine records in a waterproof container and on your phone.
  • Get pets used to their carriers and to car trips before bad weather.
  • Bring pets indoors at the first sign of a storm. Set up a safe room at home without tools, debris, or toxic items.
  • Name a neighbor, friend, or relative who can care for or evacuate your pets if you cannot.

Where pets can go

FEMA warns that many public shelters and hotels do not take pets. Before a disaster, find pet-friendly places and list their addresses and phone numbers. Also line up a friend or relative outside the danger zone who can take your pets.

Service animals in shelters

Service animals are not pets under the law. The CDC says shelters must allow them. Under the ADA, service animals are dogs, and in some cases miniature horses. The ADA National Network says government-run shelters must change "no pets" rules for service animals and must not separate a person from the animal. Staff may ask only two questions: whether you need the animal because of a disability, and what tasks it performs.

  • Bring food, water, ID tags, and vet records for the animal. Do not count on the shelter to supply them.
  • Tell responders you have a service animal and the legal right to evacuate with it.
  • Plan time outdoors for the animal. Shelters should adjust security screening so you are not delayed each time you go out.

Legal note

The ADA does not cover pets, so a shelter may turn away ordinary pets. The CDC also says that under the Fair Housing Amendments Act, disaster shelters must allow emotional support animals. Only one of the sources we opened says this, so check with your local shelter operator and the ADA National Network before you rely on it.

Putting It Together

  1. List who in your home is vulnerable and what each person needs for 3 days away and 2 weeks at home (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear and Part 1 › Planning).
  2. Write the medication card for each person (Figure 4). Ask each doctor about an extra supply.
  3. Build your support network (Figure 3). Give one person a key.
  4. Call your equipment supplier and electric utility. Build a three-level power plan (Figure 5).
  5. Pack kits for infants, children, and pets (Figures 1 and 6). Store comfort items, ID cards, and photos.
  6. Ask schools, workplaces, and care facilities about their plans.
  7. Practice an evacuation, including the slowest person and every pet. Fix what fails.

Get hands-on training too. Take infant and child CPR and first aid from the Red Cross or American Heart Association, and a general first-aid course (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines). For disasters that strike, see Part 9 › Natural Disasters.
