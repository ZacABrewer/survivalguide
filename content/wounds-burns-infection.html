Most wounds in the field are small, but a few kill fast (bleeding) and many go bad slowly (infection). This chapter goes deeper than the Red Pages card: how to stop bleeding, clean and cover a wound, decide whether to close it, spot infection early, treat burns, and keep a daily log when no hospital is near. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course teaches these skills far better than any page.

Order of care

Life threats come first. Stop heavy bleeding, then check breathing and signs of shock (see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Clean and close wounds only after that. Call for help early (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

Bleeding Control in Depth

Severe bleeding can kill in minutes. Work in steps, and go to the next step if the one before it fails. Use gloves or a plastic bag over your hand, but do not wait for them.

1. Direct pressure

Put a dressing on the wound and press straight down with steady, firm force. The Red Cross says to keep pressing until the bleeding stops, someone takes over, you are too tired to go on, or the scene becomes unsafe. If blood soaks through, add a pad on top. Do not lift the first one. Swap that top pad as it soaks instead of piling up many (Red Cross). If the bleeding stops, wrap a roller bandage firmly over the dressing and check the skin beyond it (see Pressure bandage below). If it starts again, take off the bandage, leave the first dressing in place, and press again by hand.

2. Wound packing

If pressure alone fails, and the wound is deep, pack it. Packing is for life-threatening bleeding from places a tourniquet cannot reach, such as the scalp, neck, shoulder, groin, or back, or from a limb when you have no tourniquet. The Red Cross lists it as a skill for trained people, so practice it in a Stop the Bleed class.

  1. Pull away clothing so you can see the wound. Wipe away pooled blood to find where the blood is coming from.
  2. Take hemostatic gauze if you have it, or plain gauze, a clean cloth, or clothing.
  3. Push the end of the gauze into the wound with your fingers. Aim for the bleeding point at the bottom first.
  4. Keep feeding gauze in until the wound is full and the gauze is firm.
  5. Put a pad over the top. Press hard with both hands, straight down (Figure 1). With a hemostatic dressing, military guidelines call for at least 3 minutes of direct pressure.
  6. Wrap a pressure bandage over it and check the limb beyond it.

3. Hemostatic dressings

These are gauze dressings treated with a clotting agent. Military guidelines name Combat Gauze as the first choice, with Celox Gauze and ChitoGauze as alternatives. They are used like plain gauze, with firm pressure on top. The agent loses strength with age, but an old dressing still works as ordinary gauze (Stop the Bleed). If one fails, take it out and pack a fresh one (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).

4. Pressure bandage

Once bleeding stops, hold the dressing in place with a roller bandage wrapped firmly over it. Then check the skin farthest from the heart: its color, its warmth, and whether the person feels numbness or tingling. If the fingers or toes go pale, cool, or numb, the wrap is too tight. Loosen it (Figure 2).

5. Tourniquets

Use a tourniquet for life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg when firm pressure is not stopping it, or when you cannot keep pressing (see Part 0 › Red Pages for placement). On a limb, if you have a tourniquet, do not lose time packing first; the Red Cross keeps limb packing for when no tourniquet is available. Put it 2 to 3 inches (5 to 8 cm) above the wound, between the wound and the heart, and never on a joint. If that spot lands on a knee or elbow, go just above the joint. Pull the strap tight, then twist the rod until the bleeding stops. Lock the rod and write the time on it. If bleeding continues, add a second tourniquet above the first (Red Cross, Stop the Bleed).

Training matters. A tourniquet tight enough to work hurts. That is normal, so do not loosen it. A Stop the Bleed class takes about one hour and gives hands-on practice. Take one.

What is known about leaving one on a long time

  • Military guidelines (the 2016 TCCC text) say to try to replace a tourniquet with a hemostatic or pressure dressing within 2 hours if other methods can control the bleeding.
  • That same text says to convert only if the person is not in shock, the wound can be watched closely, and the tourniquet is not controlling bleeding from an amputated limb.
  • It says not to remove a tourniquet that has been on more than 6 hours unless close monitoring and lab testing are available.
  • A 2026 TCCC change proposal (Journal of Special Operations Medicine) would tighten this. Trained non-medics would recheck a tourniquet within 2 hours and convert it only in those first 2 hours. After 2 hours, conversion would be for medical personnel only. After 6 hours, conversion in the field is discouraged. A failed attempt does not reset the clock.
  • A wilderness course handout calls under 2 hours safe, and links more than 6 hours with nerve and tissue damage and with dangerous chemical changes in the blood when blood flow returns.

I could not find a verified, exact "safe time" for a lay rescuer. So the rule for untrained people is simple: leave it on, mark the time, and get the person to care. Only someone trained in conversion should try it, within the first 2 hours, under the conditions above, with the wound packed and a pressure dressing ready. Watch for bleeding to return, and tighten the tourniquet again at once if it does. Do not experiment. Use a telemedicine or medical control line if you have one.

Do not

  • Do not remove or loosen a tourniquet because the person is in pain.
  • Do not put a tourniquet on a joint.
  • Do not use a tourniquet for bleeding that a few minutes of firm pressure will stop.

Cleaning a Wound

Once the bleeding has stopped, cleaning is the most important thing you can do to prevent infection. The goal is to wash out bacteria, dirt, and dead tissue. The Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) wound guideline (2014, still its newest on wounds) says to start as early as practical. Small, shallow wounds under about 0.4 inch (1 cm) that are not dirty need only a gentle wash.

Irrigation

  1. Wash your hands. Clip long hair around the wound with scissors. Do not shave, because shaving raises infection risk.
  2. Use water you would be willing to drink. WMS calls drinkable (potable) water the preferred fluid. If your water is not safe, treat it first (see Part 2 › Water).
  3. Use at least 1 quart (1 L). Dirty or deep wounds need more. A wilderness course handout suggests about 3 to 10 quarts (3 to 10 L), depending on pressure.
  4. Use a firm stream. WMS recommends high pressure, about 6 to 12 psi, because it lowers infection rates compared with a gentle pour. (Its own definitions stretch "high" to 15 psi. Above 15 psi, the stream can damage tissue and drive dirt deeper.) A 35 mL syringe with a 19-gauge needle makes about 8 psi. A bottle or bag with a pinhole poked in it also works, though its pressure varies.
  5. Aim the stream into the wound so it lifts dirt out, and let the run-off fall away from you (Figure 3).
  6. Pick out loose grit, wood, or plant material with clean tweezers. Do not dig for deep objects.

Soap and antiseptics

Wash the skin around the wound with mild soap and water, and keep soap out of the wound itself. WMS advises against adding soap, antibiotics, or antiseptics such as povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, or benzalkonium chloride to the irrigation water. They can harm healthy tissue and slow healing, and bacteria counts can rebound later. The exception is a possible rabies exposure (below). I could not verify any benefit for hydrogen peroxide or rubbing alcohol in wounds, so this guide leaves them out.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A wound must be burned or stung with strong antiseptic to be clean. Fact: Plenty of clean water at good pressure does the cleaning. Strong antiseptics in an open wound can damage tissue.

To Close or Not to Close

Closing a dirty wound traps bacteria inside. In the field, leave a wound open and covered unless it is clean, fresh, and gaping.

Leave open (clean, pack loosely, cover)May close (if clean, fresh, and low tension)
Animal and human bitesClean, straight cut (laceration) that gapes open
Punctures (deep, narrow, can hold germs)Cleaned and irrigated well
Heavily dirty or contaminated woundsWithin about 6 hours of injury (up to about 10 hours on the face and scalp)
Wounds with dead or crushed tissueEdges come together easily with no pull
Wounds older than about 6 hours (10 hours on the face and scalp)No bite or rabies concern

These time limits come from the WMS guidelines. They are not exact. The guidelines themselves disagree with each other in one table, which treats wounds seen after 8 to 12 hours (24 hours on the face) as contaminated. So use the shorter times in the field. For wounds with possible rabies, WMS says to delay closure by 4 days. Trained people can close dirty wounds later (delayed closure), or leave them to heal from the bottom up.

WMS also says to evacuate if the wound is complex or if you suspect damage to a tendon, joint, nerve, or blood vessel.

Closure strips and tape

Tape is the mildest way to close a wound, and it is the only one lay people should use. It has the lowest strength of all closures, so use it only on clean, straight, low-tension cuts. Use wound-closure strips or surgical tape. Lay them on without pulling the skin sideways. WMS warns that this sideways pull can blister the skin, especially over sticky aids such as tincture of benzoin.

  1. Clean the wound and let the skin dry. Strips do not stick to wet or oily skin.
  2. Squeeze the two edges together so they just meet. They should not overlap.
  3. Place the first strip across the middle of the wound (Figure 4).
  4. Add strips on each side, working outward from the middle. Leave small gaps between strips so fluid can drain.
  5. Cover with a clean dressing. Check daily. If it swells, turns red, or leaks pus, remove the strips and open the wound.

Sutures and staples are for trained people

WMS saves sutures and staples for complex wounds that must be closed under tension. That takes training. Closing a wound with needle and thread without training risks trapping germs inside and injuring nerves or vessels. If a wound must be closed and tape will not hold, pack it, dress it, and get care. Skin glue is acceptable for simple, low-tension wounds only.

Dressings

Wounds heal better moist and clean. WMS prefers low-adherent dressings or thin plastic films. Wet-to-dry gauze is an accepted fallback. Topical antibiotic ointment may help and has little downside if the person is not allergic. Cover the wound with a clean bandage, and change the dressing when it gets wet, dirty, or soaked, and look at the wound at least once a day. WMS gives no fixed change interval, so the daily check is this guide's practical rule.

Infection, Sepsis, and Tetanus

Even well-cared-for field wounds get infected. WMS puts the rate at about 1% to 12%.

Signs of infection

  • Redness that spreads, warmth, swelling, and pain that grows after the first day or two.
  • Pus, a bad smell, or a wound that opens up.
  • Fever, chills, or sweating.
  • Skin that looks tight and shiny, or a sore that grows fast within 24 hours.
  • Red streaks that spread out from the wound. This is a sign the infection is moving along lymph channels (lymphangitis). The Red Cross lists red streaks as a reason to contact a health provider.
  • A crackling feel under the skin. WMS lists gas in the tissue as an evacuation trigger.

What to do

  1. Mark the edge of the redness with a pen and write the time. Check it every few hours. If redness spreads past the line, the infection is winning.
  2. If a taped wound looks infected, remove the strips, open it, and irrigate.
  3. Raise the limb above the level of the heart, and rest it. This helps reduce swelling.
  4. Seek medical care for any spreading infection. Cellulitis usually needs prescription antibiotics, given by a clinician (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).
  5. A walled-off pocket of pus is an abscess. Draining one is a procedure. WMS says to open and drain an abscess only if equipment and training allow. Do not squeeze it or cut it open yourself; MedlinePlus warns this can spread the infection. Warm, moist compresses several times a day can help a small boil drain on its own. Get care.

Sepsis red flags: evacuate now

The CDC lists these warning signs: clammy or sweaty skin, confusion, extreme pain, fever or shivering, a fast heart rate or weak pulse, and shortness of breath. Any of these with a known infection is a medical emergency. Also evacuate for infection that worsens on antibiotics, infection near the eyes, or a very sick person.

Tetanus

Tetanus bacteria live in soil and enter through broken skin, most often through injuries, especially deep or dirty wounds. Symptoms start 3 to 21 days after the injury, on average about 8 days. The first sign is usually spasm of the jaw muscles ("lockjaw"). About 1 in 10 cases in the United States is fatal. You cannot treat it in the field, so check your vaccination dates before you go. The CDC says to see a health care provider about a shot for any deep or puncture wound, or one with dirt, soil, feces, saliva, or dead tissue in it.

Clean every wound well. The CDC says not to use antibiotics to prevent tetanus; watch for infection and treat it. WMS notes that oral antibiotics can delay the onset of tetanus if evacuation is late. Get the booster on the way out.

Special Wounds

Punctures

A puncture is a deep, narrow wound that can hold germs and a piece of the object. MedlinePlus says it may go deeper than it looks. Rinse it under running water for 5 minutes and wash the skin with soap. Do not poke around for pieces of the object. Treat it as a dirty wound for tetanus and leave it open. MedlinePlus says to get care if part of the object is missing, the wound is deeper than 1/4 inch (0.6 cm), it is on the face, or it came from a nail, a fishhook, a rusty object, or a bite.

Impaled objects

If an object is stuck in the body, do not pull it out. Pulling can start heavy bleeding. Pad around it so it cannot move. Control bleeding with pressure on the sides, and put any tourniquet above it. A wilderness course handout says large objects, objects near vital structures, and objects in a body cavity or the eye stay in. It allows removing small, shallow wood or plant material, and objects pressing on vessels or nerves, by trained people. I could not verify any other exceptions. Evacuate.

Amputated parts

  1. Control the bleeding from the stump first, using pressure, packing, or a tourniquet.
  2. Wrap the part in sterile gauze or a clean, damp cloth to keep it moist.
  3. Seal it in a dry plastic bag.
  4. Put that bag in a second bag or container with ice and water. Do not let the part touch ice directly. Do not use dry ice. Do not soak it.
  5. Send the part with the person. A part that is kept cool can sometimes be reattached. MedlinePlus says an uncooled part may stay usable for about 4 to 6 hours, and cooling stretches that. Move fast anyway.

Abrasions and road rash

A scrape removes the top skin and often leaves grit. Irrigate it well with plenty of clean water, wash around it with soap, and remove dirt that you can see. Then cover it with a non-stick, moist dressing. These wounds get infected easily, so watch them daily.

Splinters

Wood and plant material irritate tissue and cause infection, so remove them if they are small and near the surface. Use clean tweezers, then wash. Leave deep or large pieces and get care.

Fishhooks

The simplest method that works in the field is the string-yank. A 2001 American Family Physician article and an emergency-medicine review both describe it as low-damage and usable without anesthesia on small and medium hooks. Neither is a wilderness guideline.

  1. Everyone nearby wears eye protection and stands out of the hook's path. The hook can fly out fast.
  2. Tape or cut any treble hook points not in the skin. Remove the line, bait, and lure.
  3. Rest the body part on a firm, flat surface so the skin stays steady.
  4. Wrap strong string or fishing line around the middle of the hook's bend. Hold the ends tight.
  5. Press down on the eye of the hook so the shank lies flat against the skin. This frees the barb (Figure 6).
  6. Keep pressing. Give the string a quick, firm jerk parallel to the shank, back the way the hook went in.
  7. Wash and treat it as a puncture. Check your tetanus date.

Simply backing the hook out (retrograde) fails more often. Pushing the point through and cutting off the barb nearly always works, but it adds a second wound and usually needs numbing. Leave that to a clinician.

Do not

  • Do not try to remove a hook in or near the eye or eyelid. Tape a rigid cup over the eye without pressing on it. Evacuate.
  • Do not pull a hook that may reach bone, a joint, a tendon, a nerve, or a large blood vessel (numbness, weakness, or heavy bleeding). AAFP says these need careful medical evaluation first. Pad it so it cannot move, and get care.
  • Do not use the string-yank on a loose body part such as an earlobe. It cannot be held steady.

Animal bites and rabies risk

Wash a bite or scratch with soap and water for 15 minutes right away (see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning). Get care for any bite that breaks the skin. It may need antibiotics, a rabies vaccine, or a tetanus shot (Red Cross). If the animal might have rabies, the CDC says to seek medical care urgently and to contact the local health department. Any mammal can carry rabies. In the United States the common carriers are raccoons, skunks, bats, and foxes. The CDC says treatment after exposure is highly effective if you get it as soon as possible. Those who need it and do not get it before symptoms start will most likely die. WMS says bites with any chance of rabies need evacuation, and it treats most mammal bites as contaminated, so leave them open. Human bites and bites to the hand are the ones WMS says warrant antibiotics. WMS also lists a virucidal rinse such as povidone-iodine for rabies-risk wounds.

Blisters

Prevent them: wear boots that fit and two pairs of socks, keep socks clean and dry, and check your feet at every rest. At the first hot spot, stop. Put a round piece of moleskin or athletic tape over it before it forms a blister (NOLS). Small intact blisters: leave them alone and pad with a doughnut-shaped piece of foam, so the hole keeps the pad off the blister. Large blisters (nickel-size or bigger) can be drained by a trained person with a sterilized needle at its base, then covered. NOLS describes that method. I found only that one wilderness source. Check a drained blister every day for infection.

Burns

Burn depth

Depth tells you how serious a burn is. Look again over the next few days, because burns can deepen.

How big is the burn? The rule of palms

Size matters because large burns cause fluid loss and shock. A quick field method uses the patient's own hand, fingers together. StatPearls says the palm alone is about 0.5% of body surface, and the whole hand with fingers is about 1%. Minnesota Department of Health agrees. Count only partial-thickness and full-thickness burns, not simple redness. The 2025 WMS burn guideline notes that the palm method tends to overestimate, especially for burns over 10% of the body. For an adult, the rule of nines (head 9%, each arm 9%, chest and belly 18%, back 18%, each leg 18%, groin 1%) works for larger burns, and WMS recommends a checked method like it for fluid and evacuation decisions.

Cool it

Sources differ on how long. The Red Cross says 5 to 20 minutes of clean, running water (lukewarm is fine if cool is not available). MedlinePlus says at least 5 to 30 minutes. The American Academy of Dermatology says about 10 minutes for a minor first-degree burn. The 2025 WMS burn guideline recommends 20 minutes of cool running water at about 53 to 64°F (12 to 18°C) for burns under 10% of the body, started as soon as possible (its text also mentions 20 to 30 minutes). Cooling still helps if started within an hour. If there is no running water, soaking in cool water is acceptable. Drinkable water is preferred over untreated stream water. This guide follows WMS: cool a small burn for about 20 minutes with cool water. Stop early if the person starts to shiver.

Large burns are different. The WMS cooling advice covers burns under 10% of the body (about 10 of the patient's hands). WMS suggests not cooling at all in the field if there is a risk of hypothermia, and MedlinePlus warns against putting a severe burn in cold water. The Red Cross says to watch for hypothermia when cooling a large area, especially in children. Cover a large burn and keep the person warm (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

What not to put on a burn

  • No ice or snow on the wound. Snow can cool the water, but not the skin directly.
  • No butter, oil, mayonnaise, toothpaste, or other greasy home remedies. The Red Cross says greasy products can trap heat. (One dermatology source allows plain petroleum jelly after cooling a minor first-degree burn, but the Red Cross does not.)
  • Do not pop blisters or peel dead skin.
  • Do not pull off clothing that is stuck to the burn. Remove jewelry and rings near the burn before swelling starts.
  • Do not use sticky adhesive bandages on the burn.

Dressing and pain

After cooling, loosely cover the burn with a clean, dry, non-stick dressing. If you have no proper dressing, any clean, dry cloth will do (WMS). Do not wrap tightly, because burns swell. WMS suggests leaving most burn blisters intact. For pain, ibuprofen or acetaminophen may help; use as directed on the label. WMS advises against antibiotics by mouth to prevent burn infection. The 2025 WMS guideline recommends antibiotic ointment or honey on deep partial-thickness burns when care will be delayed, and says no ointment or special dressing is needed if care is less than 12 to 24 hours away. Change the dressing if it is wet or dirty, and check the burn daily for spreading depth or signs of infection (see above).

Fluids

Burns leak fluid. Large partial or full-thickness burns can cause shock. If an ambulance is on its way, MedlinePlus says to give nothing by mouth for a severe burn. When care is hours or days away, WMS recommends drinking instead: have the person sip often if they are awake and can swallow. Oral rehydration solution is a good choice. The simple recipe in the WMS burn guideline is 6 teaspoons of sugar and half a teaspoon of salt in 1 liter (about 1 quart) of clean water. The 2025 WMS guideline recommends drinking for burns under 20% of the body, and IV fluids, if available, for 20% or more. A burn that large means evacuation. Dark or scant urine means they need more.

Which burns need evacuation

WMS says most burn patients will need evacuation. Only minor burns are suited to care in the field.

  • Burns of the face, hands, feet, genitals, or joints, or burns that go all the way around a limb, chest, or neck.
  • Any full-thickness burn. The 2025 WMS guideline lists full-thickness burns over 5% and partial-thickness burns over 10 to 20% of the body as evacuation triggers.
  • Smoke inhaled, burns in the mouth or nose, or trouble breathing.
  • Chemical burns, electrical burns, and lightning injuries.
  • Burns in infants, older adults, or people with other illness or injury.
  • A burn that is infected, or has pain that cannot be controlled.
  • Any burn you are unsure about. MedlinePlus says to treat it as major.

Chemical burns

  1. Protect yourself. Wear gloves. Brush off any dry powder first.
  2. Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry.
  3. Flush with plenty of water for at least 20 minutes (Mayo Clinic; the Red Cross says at least 15, so use the longer time). Rinse longer if it still hurts.
  4. Cover loosely with a clean cloth.
  5. If a toxic chemical is possible, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 (United States) and keep the container or name. Eye burns need 15 to 20 minutes of eye rinsing (see Part 0 › Red Pages).

Electrical burns

Make sure the power is off before you touch the person. An electrical burn can injure deep tissue even when the skin looks fine. The Red Cross says all electrical burns need medical evaluation, even small ones. Be ready for CPR and an AED. Use the guidance above for the skin burn.
