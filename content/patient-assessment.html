When someone is hurt or sick far from a hospital, the first job is to find what will kill them soonest, fix that, and then work out the rest in a calm, repeatable order. This chapter teaches that order: size up the scene, run the primary survey, measure vital signs with no equipment, take a history, write it down, and keep re-checking so you can spot a patient who is getting worse. It is written at wilderness first responder level. Take a hands-on WFA or WFR course; reading cannot replace practice.

Scene Safety and Mechanism of Injury

You cannot help anyone if you become a second patient. Before you touch the person, stop and look. EMS assessment guidelines start with the same step: confirm the scene is safe for you, the patient, and bystanders, and move the patient away from any hazard before you start care. They also ask you to count the patients, work out what happened, and decide what help or gear you need.

Quick size-up

  1. Look for threats: rockfall, moving water, thin ice, fire, traffic, lightning, unstable slopes, wildlife, a violent person, or fumes.
  2. Make the scene safer if you can do it without risk. If you cannot, wait, or move the patient only as far as needed to reach safety.
  3. Count the patients. Look around for people you have not seen yet.
  4. Put on gloves if you have them. The CDC says to use protective equipment whenever you might be exposed to infectious material, and blood and other body fluids count. Wash your hands as soon as you can after you finish.
  5. Read the mechanism of injury (see below).
  6. Take stock of people, gear, light, weather, and time. Decide who will help and who will keep watch.

Mechanism of injury

The mechanism is how the injury happened. A long fall, a crash, a blow to the head, or being thrown from an animal or vehicle can cause deep injuries that look small on the surface. Ask the patient and any witnesses what happened, and look at the scene: a bent helmet, a broken branch, a dented hood. This tells you where to look hard later, and whether to protect the spine.

Warning

Do not rush in to a scene with a downed power line, a gas smell, a rising flood, or an active threat. A rescuer who dies helps no one. Move to safety, call for help if you can, and plan before you act.

The Primary Survey: Life Threats First

The primary survey is a fast check that looks only for things that can kill within minutes. You fix each problem as you find it, then move on. Do not start the head-to-toe exam until this is done.

Why the letters differ

Courses teach slightly different letters. Older first aid classes teach ABC. In 2025 the American College of Surgeons put an "x" in front of its trauma course letters, making them xABCDE, to stress fast control of heavy bleeding. A California EMS guide uses ABC by default, X-ABC for trauma with life-threatening bleeding, and CAB (compressions first) for cardiac arrest. A wilderness medicine group adds scene safety and shelter first, then catastrophic bleeding, then ABCDE. Stop the Bleed, also run by the American College of Surgeons, teaches three bleeding actions: direct pressure, wound packing, and a tourniquet. The letters change. The priorities do not: make the scene safe, stop massive bleeding, then airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure. Learn the version your course uses, and know that the others agree on what matters most.

Cardiac arrest is different

If a patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally, skip the long survey and start CPR (see Part 0 › Red Pages). Bleeding first applies to trauma where blood is spurting or pooling.

X: Massive bleeding

Look over the patient for blood that is spurting, pooling on the ground, or soaking through clothes. Stop it first. Stop the Bleed calls bleeding the top cause of preventable death after injury. Use firm direct pressure, pack the wound, or apply a tourniquet. Full steps are in Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection, and in Part 0 › Red Pages.

A: Airway

If the patient is talking, the airway is open. If they are unresponsive, open it with a head tilt and chin lift. If you suspect a spine injury, use a jaw thrust without tilting the head. If the jaw thrust does not open the airway, use the head tilt. Clear visible blockages from the mouth. When the airway and the neck conflict, the airway wins. The American Heart Association (for cardiac arrest) and the Wilderness Medical Society both put an open airway ahead of keeping the spine still.

B: Breathing

Watch the chest rise. In an unresponsive patient, take no more than 10 seconds to decide whether they are breathing normally. Gasping is not normal breathing. Count the rate and note the effort (see the vital signs section). Look for wounds that suck air, a chest that moves unevenly, or blue lips. Treat what you find using Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine and the Red Pages.

C: Circulation

Check the pulse at the wrist, then judge skin color, warmth, and moisture. Re-check for bleeding you missed. Blood can also hide inside the chest, belly, or pelvis, so a fast weak pulse with pale, cool skin after a hard fall or crash may mean bleeding you cannot see.

D: Disability

Check responsiveness using AVPU. Ask the patient to wiggle fingers and toes and tell you if they can feel your touch. Low blood sugar can look like a head or brain problem, so ask about diabetes and look for a medical alert tag.

E: Exposure and environment

Cut or remove clothing as needed to see injuries, respecting modesty and weather. Then cover the patient and insulate them from the ground. A cold patient bleeds more easily and recovers poorly (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Common deadly mistakes

  • Walking past a bleeding wound to check the airway of a patient who is already talking.
  • Getting drawn to a dramatic but minor injury (a bloody scalp, a twisted ankle) and missing a silent one.
  • Skipping the re-check. Patients change.
  • Leaving the patient bare and cold after you finish looking.

Spine Precautions

Think about a spine injury when the mechanism is serious: a fall from height, a crash, being thrown, a diving or sliding impact, or a hard blow to the head or neck. Treat the patient as possibly injured if they have any of these:

  • Pain or tenderness over the middle of the spine
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in an arm or leg
  • A changed level of alertness, or alcohol or drug use
  • Another painful injury that could distract them from neck pain

These are the same checks used by the NEXUS rule, which the Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) names as one tool for trained rescuers. North Carolina's EMS protocol uses them too, plus the mechanism, and is more cautious with older adults. Under that protocol, a patient who passes every check, with no major mechanism, does not need spine motion limits. Making that call well takes training. If you are not trained, or you are unsure, protect the spine.

The WMS 2024 guideline sets the goal as protecting the spinal cord and spine while keeping you and the patient safe. It aims to limit motion, not to lock the patient down. It advises against rigid neck collars in wilderness care. It says backboards are only for brief moves, not for holding a patient still. In practice:

  • Tell the patient to keep still and to avoid moving the neck, especially if it hurts.
  • Gently bring the head in line with the body and hold it there. Stop and support it where it is if moving it meets resistance, raises pain, or causes new or worse numbness or weakness.
  • An alert, reliable patient may move themselves out of danger. WMS supports this.
  • Find and fix life threats first. Never let spine care block the airway.

Details, and how to move a patient with a possible spine injury, are in Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine.

Vital Signs Without Equipment

Vital signs are the body's basic measurements. With no cuff, no monitor, and no thermometer, you can still collect most of them with your eyes, hands, and a watch. Take a first set early, write it down with the time, and then repeat. A single number matters less than the change.

Level of responsiveness (AVPU)

AVPU is a four-step scale. Start at the top and stop when you get a response.

  • Alert: awake and responding on their own. Alert does not always mean oriented. Also ask their name, where they are, and what happened. Confusion matters.
  • Voice: they respond only when you speak to them. The response may be a moan, a grunt, or a small movement.
  • Pain: they respond only to a painful stimulus, such as pinching the earlobe or pressing a pen on a fingernail.
  • Unresponsive: no response to voice or pain.

AVPU is crude. It has only four steps. Still, a drop from A to V is one of the most important changes you can catch. Always try voice before pain.

Heart rate and pulse

Use your index and middle fingers, not your thumb, because the thumb has its own pulse. Press gently. MedlinePlus says you can count for a full minute, or count for 30 seconds and double it, 15 seconds and multiply by 4, or 10 seconds and multiply by 6. Also note whether the pulse is strong or weak, and regular or irregular.

  1. Find the wrist pulse on the thumb side of the inner wrist, below the base of the thumb.
  2. Rest two fingers lightly on the spot and wait. Do not press hard.
  3. Count the beats against a watch (see above for timing choices).
  4. If you cannot find a wrist pulse, try the neck. Sit or lay the patient down first, and press on one side only, never both at once. Pressing on the neck can cause fainting or a slowed heartbeat in some people.
  5. For an infant, use the brachial pulse, as the Red Cross teaches. Move the arm a little away from the body and feel the inside of the upper arm, between the shoulder and elbow.

Pulse checks are not always reliable

The American Heart Association says lay rescuers cannot reliably tell whether a pulse is there or not. The AHA and Red Cross both say lay rescuers should not check a pulse before starting CPR on an unresponsive child or infant who is not breathing normally. If someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR (see Part 0 › Red Pages).

Breathing rate and effort

Count breaths by watching the chest rise. One rise and fall is one breath. If the patient knows you are counting, they may change how they breathe, so keep your fingers on the wrist as if still taking the pulse. Also watch the effort:

  • Fast, slow, or gasping breaths
  • A chest that moves in an odd way, or skin pulling in between the ribs or above the collarbones
  • Nostrils flaring, grunting, or trouble speaking a full sentence
  • Blue or gray lips or fingernails

Skin: color, temperature, moisture

Look at the lips, nail beds, and inside of the eyelids, and feel the forehead and hands. Note pale, flushed, gray, or blue skin, hot or cold skin, and dry or sweaty skin. Cool, pale, damp skin with a fast pulse is a warning that circulation may be failing. MedlinePlus lists pale, cool, clammy skin and a rapid, weak pulse among the signs of shock. In cold weather, cool hands alone mean little, so compare with the chest and the patient's trend.

Capillary refill

Capillary refill tests how fast blood returns to the small vessels under a fingernail. Squeeze the nail bed until it turns pale, let go, and time how long the color takes to come back. MedlinePlus says pink should return in less than 2 seconds, and that slower refill can point to dehydration, hypothermia, low blood pressure, or shock.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A refill time over 2 seconds proves shock. Fact: The 2-second rule is a rough teaching tool. In a 2008 study of 1,000 healthy adults, a 2-second cutoff would have wrongly flagged 45% of them. Refill was slower in older people, in women, and in the cold. Earlier work found cool rooms slowed refill in healthy children, and dim light can make it hard to see at all. Use it as one clue among many, and trust the trend and the other signs more.

Pupils

Look at the pupils (the black centers of the eyes) in the secondary survey and again on every re-check after a head injury. Note the size, whether both match, and whether they shrink when light hits them. Shade the eyes with your hand for a few seconds, then uncover them or shine a light in from the side. They should shrink.

Up to 1 in 5 healthy people have pupils of slightly different size. Sources differ on how big a normal difference can be: MedlinePlus says usually under 0.5 mm and at most about 1 mm (0.04 in), while an Australian doctors' guide allows up to 2 mm. You cannot measure this well in the field, so ask the patient or companions whether it is usual for them. MedlinePlus says unequal pupils after an eye or head injury need medical help right away. A pupil that is widely dilated and does not react to light, or any new change after an injury, is an emergency. Eye drops and some medicines can also change pupil size, but treat it as an emergency until proven otherwise (see Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine).

Temperature

With no thermometer, you can only get a rough sense by touch. Feel the forehead and chest, and note if skin feels hot, cool, or cold compared with yours. Shivering and sweating matter too. If you do have a thermometer, MedlinePlus lists a normal adult temperature of 97.7 to 99.1°F (36.5 to 37.3°C). An adult probably has a fever above 99 to 99.5°F (37.2 to 37.5°C) by mouth, depending on the time of day. In children, a fever is 100.4°F (38°C) or higher by rectum, 99.5°F (37.5°C) by mouth, or 99°F (37.2°C) under the arm. A body temperature below 95°F (35°C) is a medical emergency (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Know the patient's own baseline

A trained runner with a pulse of 55 is normal. A frail adult at 55 might not be. Ask what is usual, and compare every new set of numbers to the first set you took.

The Secondary Survey: Head to Toe and History

Once life threats are handled, take a slower look. If the patient is critical, do not delay evacuation to finish this exam. Do what you can on the way.

Head-to-toe exam

EMS training texts give this order for the rapid trauma exam: head, neck, chest, belly, pelvis, arms and legs, then the back. At each area, look and feel for these signs, remembered as DCAP-BTLS: deformities, contusions (bruises), abrasions (scrapes), punctures, burns, tenderness, lacerations (cuts), and swelling. Check arms and legs for movement, feeling, and a pulse beyond any injury. Check the back last. If a spine injury is possible, keep the spine in line while you do it. The WMS prefers a team lift-and-slide over a log roll when spine motion must be limited (see Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine).

Pelvis

Do not rock or squeeze the pelvis again and again. If pressure causes pain, stop.

SAMPLE history

Ask the patient, or companions if the patient cannot answer. SAMPLE stands for:

  • Signs and symptoms: what is wrong, what the patient feels, what you see
  • Allergies: medicines, foods, stings
  • Medications: prescription, over the counter, supplements
  • Pertinent past history: heart, lung, diabetes, seizures, pregnancy, past surgery
  • Last oral intake: when they last ate or drank
  • Events leading up to it: what they were doing, what happened

In the field it also helps to ask about output: when they last passed urine and stool, and whether it looked normal. This tells you about hydration and illness. Write the answers down in the patient's words.

OPQRST for pain

  • Onset: when and how it started
  • Provocation or palliation: what makes it better or worse
  • Quality: sharp, dull, tearing, pressure, burning
  • Region and radiation: where it is, and whether it spreads
  • Severity: 0 to 10, with 10 the worst
  • Time: how long, and whether constant or on and off

Writing It Down: The SOAP Note

Memory fails under stress and over long hours. A written record lets a second rescuer take over, and lets a doctor later see what happened. SOAP is a simple four-part note:

  • Subjective: what the patient and witnesses tell you (SAMPLE, OPQRST)
  • Objective: what you see, feel, and measure (vital signs, exam findings)
  • Assessment: your best idea of what is wrong, with a list of problems
  • Plan: what you will do, what you will watch, and when you will re-check or evacuate

Write the time on every entry. Use plain words, not guesses presented as facts. Copy this form onto paper and keep it in your kit.

Monitoring Over Hours and Days

Your first set of numbers is a baseline. The point of taking more sets is to see which way the patient is going. One EMT training text (Montgomery County, Maryland) suggests re-checking every 15 minutes for a stable patient, and every 5 minutes for one who is unstable or could become unstable. If something may have changed, repeat at least the primary survey. In a long field stay, courses vary on how far to stretch these gaps. A fair rule: the sicker the patient, the shorter the gap, and any change in the patient is a reason to check again right away. Wake a sleeping patient at planned times to check responsiveness and breathing, if the injury or illness calls for it.

The same idea works for pulse and breathing. A pulse that is getting faster and weaker, breathing that is getting faster or more labored, or skin that is getting colder, pale, and damp usually means the patient is getting worse.

Red flags: start the evacuation now

Any one of these means the patient needs more care than you can give. Begin evacuation or a rescue call (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions and Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

  • Responsiveness falls (A to V, V to P) or confusion grows
  • Breathing is hard, noisy, or too fast, or lips turn blue or gray
  • Bleeding that you cannot control, or signs of bleeding inside the body
  • A pulse that keeps getting faster and weaker
  • After a head injury: unequal or non-reacting pupils, or a patient who becomes harder to wake
  • New numbness or weakness in the arms or legs
  • Body temperature below 95°F (35°C)
  • Any patient who is getting worse despite your care

If help is not coming, you may have to care for the patient for days (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming, and Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care). Keep the log going. Eat, drink, and rest yourself too.

Several Patients at Once

When there are more patients than rescuers, you must decide who gets help first. Triage means sorting by need. In the US the most commonly used method for mass-casualty incidents is called START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment). It was developed in California in 1983. It uses five quick checks: can the person walk, are they breathing, is the breathing rate above or below 30 per minute, is circulation adequate (radial pulse, or capillary refill), and can they follow simple commands.

CategoryMeaning
Minor (green)Fairly minor injuries; unlikely to worsen over days; may be able to help with their own care ("walking wounded")
Delayed (yellow)May be serious, even life-threatening, but not expected to get much worse over several hours
Immediate (red)Needs care within minutes (up to about an hour) to survive; airway, breathing, or circulation problem
Expectant (black)Unlikely to survive given the injuries and the care available; give comfort and ease pain

The START checks sort each patient like this (from CHEMM and a peer-reviewed training paper):

  • Can walk: Minor.
  • Not breathing: open the airway once. Still not breathing: Expectant. Breathes only after you open the airway: Immediate.
  • Breathing faster than 30 a minute: Immediate.
  • No wrist (radial) pulse, or capillary refill slower than 2 seconds: Immediate. In the cold, rely on the wrist pulse. A 1996 revision of START swapped it in for capillary refill and reported better accuracy, especially in cold weather.
  • Cannot follow a simple command, such as "squeeze my hand": Immediate.
  • Passes every check: Delayed.
  1. Call out to everyone who can walk to move to a safe spot. Treat them as minor for now.
  2. Go to the rest, one at a time, as fast as you can. Stop only for massive bleeding or an airway you can open at once.
  3. Tag each patient with a category (a note or colored cloth works).
  4. Treat the red patients first, then yellow.
  5. Go back and re-triage. People move between categories.

Triage has limits

Triage is a fast sort, not a diagnosis. START was built for responders with trained help arriving. If you have only a few patients and several helpers, skip triage and treat everyone by the primary survey. Changing a patient to expectant is a heavy decision. Reconsider as resources change, and keep giving comfort. Practice triage in a class with an instructor.
