Cold, heat, altitude, lightning, water, and sun hurt more people outdoors than any animal does. This chapter goes deeper than the Red Pages cards: how to tell the stages of each injury apart, what to do when help is hours or days away, and which popular tricks make things worse. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course teaches these skills far better than any page.

Order of care

Check the scene is safe, then airway, breathing, and bleeding (see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Many of these injuries are fixed by changing the surroundings: get out of the cold, the heat, the water, or the storm. Plan to call for help early (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). For shelter methods see Part 2 › Shelter.

Hypothermia

Hypothermia means the body core is losing heat faster than it makes it. It does not need freezing weather. Wet clothes, wind, and tiredness make it worse. Core temperature is hard to measure in the field, so rescuers stage the injury by what they see.

Staging by signs

Two systems are in common use. The Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) calls the stages mild, moderate, and severe. The Swiss system numbers them HT I to HT IV. Both use the same clues: is the person shivering, are they alert, and are there signs of life. The temperatures are only rough guides, and the sources say they do not always match the signs.

The old rule "nobody is dead until warm and dead" still matters. Cold stiffness, fixed pupils, and a pulse too slow to feel do not rule out saving someone. Check for breathing and a pulse for a full 60 seconds, then start CPR unless the body is frozen solid.

Handling: be gentle

A cold heart is irritable. Rough handling, rolling, or making a drowsy person walk can trigger a deadly heart rhythm. Keep moderate and severe cases lying flat. Move them as little as you can. Lift them as one unit.

Afterdrop

Afterdrop is a further fall in core temperature after rescue. It can happen when cold blood from the arms and legs returns to the core, for example if you rub the limbs, make the person move, or dunk them in warm water. Warm the trunk first and leave the limbs alone.

The hypothermia wrap ("burrito")

The goal is a sealed, insulated cocoon that holds in whatever heat the person has and any you add. Get everything ready before you open the wrap, because every minute of exposure counts.

  1. Pick the spot. Get out of wind and rain, and off snow or wet ground. Work fast, but stay gentle.
  2. Lay down a thick insulating pad under the person: a sleeping pad, pack, rope coil, or a deep bed of branches. The ground steals more heat than the air.
  3. Lay a waterproof sheet (tarp, plastic sheet, or emergency blanket) on the pad. This will be the inner vapor barrier.
  4. Ease the person onto it. Dry clothing is better than none. For wet clothing, see "Wet clothes" below.
  5. Put warm (not hot) bottles or chemical heat packs against the clothing at the chest, armpits, and back. Wrap them in cloth. Mark where they are.
  6. Wrap the inner barrier around the feet first, then the sides, then the body.
  7. Add insulation over it: a sleeping bag or blankets, with the pad outside or under it. Keep the insulation fluffy; crushed insulation does not warm.
  8. Wrap everything in an outer wind and waterproof layer. Cover the head and neck, leaving the face clear for breathing.
  9. Check the skin under heat sources every 20 to 30 minutes for burns. Check breathing often.

Wet clothes

Sources differ. The Red Cross and CDC say to remove wet clothing. WMS says to leave it on until the person is somewhere warm. A British military guideline gives a middle path: if wet and shelter is under 30 minutes away, leave wet clothes on; if shelter is farther, build a shelter and take them off. The safe rule is this: do not strip someone in the wind. Take wet clothes off only inside a sheltered spot with dry insulation ready, cutting them off so you do not move the limbs much.

Rewarming by stage

  • Mild: Insulate, then add heat to the armpits, chest, and back. If the person is alert, shivering well, and can swallow, give warm, high-calorie food and drink. Keep them resting for at least 30 minutes, then watch closely. If they are not improving after 30 minutes, evacuate. If they are getting better, let them add activity slowly to make their own heat.
  • Moderate and severe: Keep them flat and still. Nothing by mouth. Wrap, add heat to the trunk, and evacuate with great care. Small chemical packs do not give enough heat to rewarm a core; they are for hands and feet. A big warm pack or skin-to-skin contact under the wrap spreads more heat.
  • No signs of life: Start CPR. Hypothermic hearts may not respond to shocks below about 86°F (30°C). WMS says to give one shock and then focus on rewarming. Do not stop CPR just because the person is cold and stiff.

A well-insulated person who is still shivering hard can slowly rewarm on their own, as long as they have food for fuel, which is why food matters in mild cases. Shivering stops as the core falls below about 86 to 90°F (30 to 32°C). A person who stops shivering without warming up is getting worse, not better.

Do not

  • Do not give alcohol. Do not give anything by mouth to a drowsy person.
  • Do not rub the arms and legs, or put the person in a hot bath.
  • Do not put heat packs or hot bottles straight on bare skin.
  • Do not let a moderate or severe case walk, even if they say they feel fine.
  • Do not treat "stopped shivering" as "recovered."

Cold-water rescue collapse

People pulled from cold water can collapse during or soon after rescue, from falling blood pressure or a sudden heart rhythm problem. Keep them horizontal, handle them gently, and do not stand them up to walk, even if they seem fine.

Cold-Water Immersion: the 1-10-1 Rule

Falling into cold water is deadly in stages, and drowning usually comes first, not hypothermia. The "1-10-1" rule is a plan for those stages while you wear a life jacket.

  • Cold shock (first minutes): gasping and fast breathing. Without a life jacket, people can inhale water and drown.
  • Swim failure (about the next 10 minutes): cold arms and legs weaken fast and stop working well, no matter how good a swimmer you are. Use this time to get out or get help.
  • Hypothermia (about 30 to 60 minutes and on): the core slowly cools until the person passes out. Even in icy water this often takes around an hour, so a life jacket buys time.

What helps: wear a life jacket. Stay with a floating boat, since it is easier to spot than a head in the water. Do not swim unless a boat or shore is very near. Keep your clothes on, because they trap some warmth and float for a while. Float on your back. To slow heat loss, use the HELP position (arms crossed over the chest, knees drawn up, ankles crossed, leaning back). If you are with others, huddle with chests touching, with children or smaller people in the middle. Once out of the water, treat for hypothermia as above.

Frostbite

Frostbite is frozen tissue, usually fingers, toes, nose, ears, and cheeks. Frostnip is the mild, early version: skin goes white and numb, and it comes back with warming and no lasting harm. Real frostbite stays numb and hard after you warm it a bit.

Field care

  1. Get out of the cold and treat hypothermia first. Take off tight or wet clothing, rings, and watches before swelling starts.
  2. Ask the key question: can it freeze again? Thawing and refreezing does more damage than staying frozen. If there is any chance of refreezing, or you cannot keep the person warm, leave the part frozen and protect it.
  3. WMS advises rewarming in the field only when definitive care is more than about 2 hours away and refreezing can be prevented. Avoid walking on a frozen foot. But if walking out is the only way to safety, walk on it still frozen rather than thawing it first: walking on thawed feet causes severe damage, so thaw only when you can stop walking.
  4. Rewarm in a warm water bath at 98.6 to 102.2°F (37 to 39°C); summaries of the 2024 WMS update (AAFP, MSD Manual) keep this range. Use a thermometer. If you have none, test the water with an uninjured hand or foot for 30 seconds; it should feel warm and tolerable, not hot.
  5. Keep the part in the bath until it turns red or purple and feels soft, often about 30 minutes. Add warm water as it cools. It will hurt a lot as feeling returns.
  6. Air dry or blot gently. Pad loosely with dry, bulky dressings (aloe vera gel underneath is fine if you have it). Lift the limb above the heart if you can, to ease swelling.
  7. The WMS lists ibuprofen for field use. Use as directed on the label, if you can safely take it.
  8. Leave blood-filled blisters alone. Evidence for draining clear ones is thin, so do not drain them in the field.
  9. Get medical care. Deep frostbite has hospital treatments that work best soon after the injury.

Do not

  • Do not rub or massage frostbitten skin, and do not rub it with snow.
  • Do not warm it by a fire, stove, or heating pad. Numb skin burns without pain.
  • Do not walk on feet that have been thawed. Do not thaw if you will have to refreeze.

Trench Foot (Non-Freezing Cold Injury)

Trench foot, also called immersion foot, is a nerve and blood-vessel injury from feet staying wet and cool for hours to days. It does not need freezing temperatures. It happens at temperatures up to about 59°F (15°C). Wet boots, tight laces, and not moving make it worse. It can hit hands too.

  • Signs: pain, swelling, and a heavy feeling in feet that have been wet and cold for a long time.
  • Prevention: change into dry socks often. Keep feet clean and dry. Take wet boots off at night and air the feet. Raise bare feet when you sleep. Loosen boots. Wiggle your toes through the day.
  • Care: get out of wet footwear, pat the feet dry, and raise them. The CDC lists a brief warm soak (about 5 minutes), while one emergency medicine source advises slow warming at room temperature and avoiding warm-water immersion. Sources differ, so use the gentler choice: let the feet warm slowly in dry socks and do not use heat. Get medical care soon. Watch for infection (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If it is not freezing, your feet are safe. Fact: Days of cold wetness can cause lasting nerve damage at temperatures well above freezing.

Heat Illness

Heat illness is a ladder. Cramps and exhaustion are warnings. Heat stroke is a life threat: the core temperature is above about 104°F (40°C) and the brain stops working normally. Heavy work in heat can cause heat stroke even in fit young people.

Heat cramps and heat exhaustion

Cramps are painful muscle spasms in the legs, arms, or belly during or after hard work in the heat. Stop and rest somewhere cool. Sip water plus a sports drink, a salty snack, or a lightly salted drink (MedlinePlus suggests 1 teaspoon of salt per quart or liter of water). Gently stretch or firmly massage the cramped muscle. Do not use salt tablets. Get help if cramps last more than an hour, or if the person has heart problems or is on a low-salt diet. Heat exhaustion adds weakness, headache, nausea, and heavy sweating, with a core temperature usually under 104°F (40°C). Stop work, get into shade, loosen or remove clothing, and cool the skin with wet cloths. Give sips if the person is awake and able to swallow. If they get worse, vomit repeatedly, or do not improve in about an hour, treat them as heat stroke and get help.

Heat stroke: cool first, transport second

Heat stroke is a medical emergency, and the damage depends on how long the core stays hot. WMS and other guidelines call whole-body cold-water immersion the fastest way to cool, and say to start when you suspect heat stroke, even if you cannot measure temperature. The Korey Stringer Institute, a heat-stroke research group, teaches "cool first, transport second": begin cooling where you are, and move the person afterward, because ambulances usually cannot do immersion. Experts aim to bring the core down within about 30 minutes of collapse, since damage rises the longer it stays hot.

  1. Get the person out of the sun. Take off extra clothing and gear. Keep their airway safe, since they may vomit or have a seizure.
  2. Put as much of the body as you can in cold water, about 35 to 60°F (2 to 15°C), and stir it. Ice water in a tarp, stream, or lake works. Keep the head above water and hold it the whole time.
  3. How long: with a rectal thermometer, cool until the core reaches about 102°F (39°C), then take them out so they do not get too cold. With no thermometer, the Korey Stringer Institute says to immerse for about 10 to 15 minutes, and the Red Cross says about 15 minutes or until the person is no longer confused. Expect roughly 1°F of cooling every 3 minutes in cold, stirred water.
  4. Take them out, dry them, and keep watching. If confusion or high heat comes back, cool again.
  5. No water body? Use cold, wet towels or sheets over as much skin as you can, change them often, and fan. Put cold packs on large blood-vessel areas such as the armpits and groin. One WMS summary also lists the face, palms, and soles for chemical packs. Misting and fanning cool much more slowly than immersion.
  6. Have someone else call for rescue while you cool; do not stop cooling to make the call yourself. Keep watching the airway, and keep cooling on the way if you must move.

Do not

  • Do not give acetaminophen or aspirin to treat heat stroke. WMS says they do not lower the core temperature in heat illness.
  • Do not give salt tablets. Use salty food instead.
  • Do not give drinks to a confused or drowsy person.
  • Do not wait for a thermometer or a rescuer before you start cooling.

Hyponatremia: too much water

Exercise-associated hyponatremia is low blood sodium, usually from drinking too much plain water or sports drink over hours of work. It looks a lot like heat illness: headache, nausea, vomiting, weakness, confusion, collapse, seizure. Clues for overdrinking are bloating, weight gain, and no thirst. Symptoms can appear up to a day after activity.

  • Prevention: drink to thirst. Do not force fluids on a schedule. Eat salty food on long, hot days.
  • Mild: stop drinking, rest in the shade, and eat salty food or sip a salty broth. Cool the skin if hot.
  • Severe (confused, seizing, collapsed): this is an emergency. Treatment is salt solution given into a vein by trained medical staff. Get help and evacuate. If the person is hot and confused, treat for heat stroke and cool them; delaying cooling is deadly.

Telling the two apart in the field is hard. The cautious rule: a hot, confused person gets cooled. Do not pour water into a confused person either way.

Altitude Illness

Altitude illness can start above about 8,000 feet (2,450 m), and the risk rises as you climb faster and sleep higher. It has three forms.

AMS (acute mountain sickness)
Headache plus at least one of: poor appetite, nausea, dizziness, or unusual tiredness. Usually starts 2 to 12 hours after arrival, often the first night. It often fades in 1 to 2 days if you do not go higher.
HACE (cerebral edema)
Brain swelling, an extreme form of AMS. Look for stumbling and poor balance (ataxia), confusion, odd behavior, or drowsiness. Coma can follow within a day. Rare below about 14,000 ft (4,300 m).
HAPE (pulmonary edema)
Fluid in the lungs. Early: unusual breathlessness, weak on the climb, dry cough. Later: breathless at rest, wet cough, pink or bloody froth. It can also cause drowsiness or confusion.

The key treatment: go down

Descent is the single best treatment for all three. Descend until symptoms clear. For AMS, the CDC says even 1,000 feet (300 m) often brings quick relief, and a medical reference lists 1,650 to 3,300 feet (500 to 1,000 m) for severe cases. HACE and HAPE need prompt descent, as far as is practical; keep the person's effort low. Never send a person with HACE down alone. If you cannot descend, oxygen, if you have it, helps, and a portable pressure bag can buy time, but neither should delay a needed descent.

Prevention: how fast to climb

  • Go slowly. Your sleeping height matters more than the highest point you reach in a day. "Climb high, sleep low" helps.
  • Above about 9,800 feet (3,000 m), raise your sleeping altitude by no more than about 1,650 feet (500 m) per day, and take a rest day (sleep at the same height) every 3 to 4 days (WMS 2024 guideline, as reported by the CDC and the MSD Manual). The CDC adds one extra night for every 3,300 feet (1,000 m) gained. Do not climb higher to sleep while you have symptoms, however mild.
  • Avoid jumping from low ground straight to sleeping above about 9,000 feet (2,750 m).
  • Take it easy the first 48 hours and avoid alcohol. Acclimatization takes several days.
  • Forcing extra fluids has not been shown to prevent altitude illness (see hyponatremia above).
  • If you have had altitude illness before, plan a slower climb.

Medicines. Doctors prescribe drugs for prevention and treatment, including acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, and sildenafil or tadalafil. Talk to a doctor before a high trip about whether any are right for you. This guide gives no doses. They are not a substitute for slow ascent and descent.

Rule out other causes

Headache, nausea, and confusion at altitude can also come from carbon monoxide in a tent or cabin, dehydration, low blood sugar, hypothermia, or hyponatremia. Check for those too (see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning). Never light a stove inside a closed tent.

Lightning

Prevention: current guidance

The old "30-30 rule" (go inside if the flash-to-bang is under 30 seconds) is out of date as the main plan. The National Weather Service (NWS) teaches a simpler rule: when thunder roars, go indoors. If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Lightning can strike 10 miles (16 km) or more from the storm. Wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder before you go back out.

  • Safe shelter is a substantial building with plumbing and wiring, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows up. Tents, sheds, picnic shelters, and covered porches do not protect you.
  • If you are caught outdoors, get off ridges, summits, and open ground, and away from water. Stay away from isolated tall trees, cliffs, poles, power lines, and metal fences. Do not be the tallest thing around, and do not lie flat on the ground. In woods, keep some distance from any single tree.
  • In a group, spread out so one strike does not hurt everyone and someone can help.

The "lightning position"

Older guides taught a "lightning crouch": squat low, feet together, head tucked. The NWS stopped recommending it in 2008, because it does not meaningfully protect you; if lightning comes down on or near you, you are likely to be hit whether you stand or crouch. WMS also treats it as a last resort at most, not a safety plan. Do not waste time crouching when you could be moving toward a building, a hard-topped vehicle, or lower, less exposed ground. The real protection is planning: check the forecast, turn back early, and know where you will shelter.

Caring for a person struck by lightning

A lightning victim carries no electric charge and is safe to touch. Cardiac arrest is the main cause of death. About 1 in 10 victims dies, and many survivors have lasting pain or nerve problems.

  1. Check the sky. If the storm is still overhead, move yourself and the person to safer ground when you can do so without more harm.
  2. Call for help (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).
  3. Check breathing and pulse. If neither is present, start CPR and use an AED if you have one (see Part 0 › Red Pages).
  4. If several people were struck, use reverse triage: treat first the ones who are not breathing and have no pulse. In most disasters, a person with no signs of life is passed over. With lightning, they are the ones most likely to come back with quick breathing support.
  5. Check for burns, broken bones from a fall, and hearing or eye injuries. Treat what you find (see Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine and Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  6. Get every survivor evaluated, even those who feel fine. Many survivors have lasting problems.

WMS notes that a second cardiac arrest can follow if breathing is not supported after the heart restarts. Keep supporting breaths.

Drowning

Drowning kills by cutting off oxygen. Your first rule is that you must not become the second victim.

Reach, throw, row, don't go

WMS says untrained people should reach, throw, or row from a safe position. Only trained people should enter the water.

After the person is out

  1. Check for response and breathing. Send someone for help.
  2. Open the airway. If they are not breathing normally, give rescue breaths. WMS stresses that getting oxygen to the brain is the priority in drowning, so breaths matter more than in many other arrests. If they have no pulse, give CPR with compressions and breaths (see Part 0 › Red Pages for the CPR steps).
  3. Only trained rescuers with the right gear should try breaths in the water, and never chest compressions. Get the person out first.
  4. Spine injury is very rare in drowning. Take spine precautions only when there is a strong clue, such as a dive into shallow water or a fall.
  5. Treat for hypothermia if cold and wet (see above).

Watch afterward. People who coughed or struggled to breathe can worsen over hours as lungs fill. WMS says anyone with abnormal breathing, heavy cough, frothy spit, or a changed mind needs urgent evacuation. A person with no symptoms and normal lung sounds can be watched for about 6 hours. In the cold, WMS says stopping resuscitation may be reasonable after submersion longer than 30 minutes in water warmer than 43°F (6°C), or longer than 90 minutes in colder water, or after 25 minutes of continuous CPR. Context matters; if you have help or hope, keep going and ask a medical professional.

Do not

  • Do not swim out to a struggling person without a float. They may climb on you.
  • Do not do chest compressions in the water.

Sunburn and Snow Blindness

Sunburn

Sunburn is a UV burn to the skin. Snow and water reflect UV back at you. Cover up, use shade, and use sunscreen as directed on the label.

  1. Get out of the sun.
  2. Cool the skin with a damp cool cloth or a cool bath for about 10 minutes at a time. Pat dry.
  3. Use a moisturizer such as aloe or soy while the skin is damp. Avoid products with alcohol.
  4. Take over-the-counter pain relief, as directed on the label.
  5. Drink extra water.
  6. Leave blisters alone. If one breaks, wash it gently with mild soap and water and cover it with a non-stick bandage.

Seek medical care for large or facial blisters, fever with chills or vomiting, confusion, dizziness or fainting, signs of infection (pus, red streaks), or eye pain.

Snow blindness (photokeratitis)

Snow blindness is a sunburn of the clear front of the eye (the cornea). It comes from UV light off snow, ice, water, or a bright haze, even on a cloudy day. Symptoms usually start 30 minutes to 12 hours after exposure, often at night: severe pain in both eyes, tearing, a gritty feeling, light sensitivity, and blurred vision.

  • Care: get out of the light and rest out of bright light. Use a cool damp cloth over closed eyes. Remove contact lenses and do not wear them until the eyes heal. Do not rub your eyes. Use over-the-counter pain relief as directed on the label. Artificial tears can ease the gritty feeling.
  • Outlook: the surface layer usually heals in 24 to 72 hours. See an eye doctor if pain worsens or vision does not clear.
  • Prevention: wear sunglasses or goggles that block UV, with side shields on snow. Carry a spare pair.

Improvised slit goggles

Traditional Inuit snow goggles used narrow slits to cut glare. US military survival manuals teach the same trick: if you lose your sunglasses, cut small horizontal slits in a strip of lightproof material such as cardboard, thin leather, bark, or webbing, and tie it on behind your head. Slits let in far less light than open eyes. No tested data exists on how much UV an improvised pair blocks, so treat them as better than nothing, not equal to real UV-rated lenses.

  1. Cut a strip of material long enough to wrap the eyes and reach behind the head. Dark material cuts glare best.
  2. Cut one small horizontal slit over each eye, just wide enough to see through. Narrower lets in less light but shows you less.
  3. Shape a nose notch so the strip sits comfortably. Smooth the edges so they do not scratch.
  4. Tie it on with cord or tape. Line the inside with cloth, and keep light from leaking in around the sides.
  5. Look around by turning your head. Move slowly on rough ground, because the field of view is narrow.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: You cannot get a bad sunburn or snow blindness on a cloudy or cold day. Fact: UV passes through cloud, and snow reflects it back up at you. Cold hides the heat of the sun, not the UV.

When Help Is Days Away

  • Make a safe shelter and stay put for hypothermia, heat stroke, and altitude problems that are mild. Walking out an injured, cold, or sick person adds risk. Descent is the exception for altitude illness.
  • Keep a log every few hours: time, temperature, alertness, breathing rate, and what you gave. A trend tells you whether to stay or go (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).
  • Feed and water the patient once they are awake and swallowing. Warm sweet drinks for cold; salty snacks and sips for heat.
  • Protect the rescuers. Do not push the team into the same danger. Weigh the cost of a dangerous evacuation against waiting (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).
  • Signal early (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).
  • Train. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course practices wraps, cooling, and rescue breathing under supervision.
