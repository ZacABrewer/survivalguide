Most deaths while traveling in the wild come from moving water, thin ice, heat, cold, and steep ground. This chapter helps you decide whether to move at all, and how to move with the least risk when you must. Moving is a risk. If you are lost and nothing forces you out, staying put is usually safer (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming and Part 9 › Navigation Without Instruments).

Warning

Moving water and ice kill fast. Cold water weakens your arms and legs within minutes. If a crossing looks doubtful, treat it as unsafe. Wait, or find another place.

River Crossings

Ask three questions first. Must you cross at all? Could you wait for the water to drop? Is there a better spot? Snow-fed and glacier-fed streams run lowest in the early morning and rise through the afternoon, so a crossing that is easy at dawn may be impossible on the way back.

Scout first

  1. Walk the bank both ways. The widest stretch is usually the shallowest and slowest.
  2. Look downstream of every possible crossing. Waterfalls, boulders, fallen trees, and debris piles can trap a person who falls. If you see one, pick another spot.
  3. Throw in a stick. If it races away, or you hear rocks knocking along the bottom, do not cross.
  4. Judge depth against your own body. Two feet (60 cm) is knee-deep on a tall person and mid-thigh on a short one. One source says to keep slow water below mid-thigh and fast water below the knee. Treat those as upper limits.
  5. Avoid log jams. They shift without warning and can pin you.

Cross with a plan

  1. Keep your shoes on. Barefoot and flip-flops are poor choices. With no spare footwear, cross in your boots and change into dry socks on the far side.
  2. Pack spare clothing in a waterproof bag, such as a heavy trash bag.
  3. Loosen the pack so you can drop it fast (see the hip-belt note below).
  4. Face upstream. Hold a pole or branch with both hands, planted upstream, so your legs and the pole form a tripod.
  5. Shuffle sideways, one point of contact at a time. Keep two planted.
  6. Angle downstream. Do not fight the current by heading straight across. The Army manual suggests about 45 degrees to the current.
  7. On the far side, get dry, put on warm layers, and eat (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Group crossings

A linked group is steadier than one person. In a line, everyone faces upstream in a column parallel to the current and holds the shoulders or pack of the person ahead. In a linked group, people face the far bank and link arms through each other's packs, each keeping the hip belt buckled.

Sources disagree about who goes upstream. The National Park Service and a mountaineering club say the strongest person; another Park Service page says the strongest and heaviest. For a group holding one shared pole, the Army manual puts the heaviest person on the downstream end and the lightest upstream. This guide suggests the strongest, steadiest person in front. Agree on the order before anyone steps in, and stay linked until everyone is out.

Do not

  • Never tie a rope to a person in moving water. If they fall, the current can pull the rope tight and hold them under. A university swiftwater rescue text says never to tie a rope around a rescuer, because a tied-in person loses control of themselves. American Whitewater calls tethered rescue high-risk and for trained people only, using a harness with a quick release. Anyone who holds a rope must be able to let go at once.
  • Do not try to swim a wide, fast river without training. Go around instead.

The hip belt debate

Common advice, including from the National Park Service, is to unbuckle the hip belt and sternum strap before entering fast water, and loosen the shoulder straps. A pack full of water is heavy and can hold you under. As one guide puts it, a lost pack beats a lost life. Not everyone agrees. One hiker notes that with a heavy load an unbuckled belt makes you less steady, and in a linked group the belt is part of how you hold each other.

A conservative rule: unbuckle when a fall would mean being swept away or pinned (fast, deep, cold, or rocky water). Keep the belt on only when the water is slow and shallow and the runout is safe. If unsure, unbuckle.

If you are swept away

If you feel your feet go, drop forward onto your hands and knees. If the current takes you, roll onto your back and use the defensive swimming position.

  1. Lie on your back with your feet high and pointed downstream.
  2. Use your feet to push off rocks and your arms to steer.
  3. Do not stand in fast water. A foot trapped between rocks can hold you under. American Whitewater says to stand only in slow or very shallow water, below your knee.
  4. When the water is slow or deep, switch to aggressive swimming: roll onto your stomach and swim hard for shore or an eddy (a slack patch behind an obstacle). Alternate the two methods as the river changes.
  5. Use the pack as a float if it helps. Let go of it if it drags you.

Falling Into Cold Water

A cold plunge is a medical emergency as well as a travel problem. The danger comes in stages. In the first minute, cold shock makes you gasp. Over about ten minutes, your arms and legs lose strength. Hypothermia usually comes after about an hour. This is the 1-10-1 rule, explained in Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies. Use the first ten minutes to get out, not to think.

  1. Cover your mouth and nose with a gloved hand if you can. Breathe slowly until the gasping stops.
  2. Keep your clothes on. Trapped air helps you float, and the layers insulate.
  3. Head for the nearest safe exit.
  4. Once out, get warm at once (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies and Part 2 › Fire).

Wear a life jacket on any boat or ice trip. It buys the time that the rule promises.

Ice Thickness and Ice Safety

No ice is guaranteed. Thickness can vary greatly across one lake. Currents, springs, narrow channels, snow, sun, and rotting plants all change it. The numbers below are minimums for new, clear ice and average loads.

When sources disagree, use the thicker number. For walking, that is 6 inches (15 cm). The Red Cross says 8 inches (20 cm) for skating or group games. Loads that stay in one place need thicker ice than moving loads: the Minnesota DNR table for loads parked more than two hours starts at 6 inches (15 cm) for one person standing.

Reading and testing the ice

  • White ice forms when snow melts and refreezes on the lake. It is about half as strong as clear ice, so double the thickness numbers on it.
  • Our sources give no thickness rule for slush or rotten spring ice. Stay off it. Old spring ice can look fine and still hide thin spots.
  • Thin spots often come from underwater springs. Drill a test hole and measure. Repeat often.
  • Under 4 inches (10 cm), stay off.
  • Carry two ice picks on your body, where you can reach them with wet hands. They can be homemade, with floating handles.

If you fall through

  1. Stop the gasping. Cover your nose and mouth, and breathe slowly.
  2. Face the way you came. That ice held you. (The Red Cross adds: do not try to climb up on the weak, broken edge. Reach forward onto firmer ice without pushing down.)
  3. Put your arms on the ice. Let the water drain from your clothes.
  4. Kick hard until your body is level in the water. With picks, drive both in and use short, choppy pulls. Do not extend your arms fully.
  5. Slide out onto your belly. Keep kicking as you work your chest and hips onto the ice.
  6. Crawl or roll away with your arms and legs spread. Do not stand until you are well clear.
  7. Get warm at once and treat for hypothermia (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Helping someone else

  1. Call for help first.
  2. From shore or thick ice, reach with a pole or branch, or throw a rope.
  3. If you must go onto weak ice, lie flat, wear a life jacket, and push a pole ahead of you.
  4. Have the person kick while you pull. Move both of you off thin ice before anything else.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If the ice is thick where you tested it, the whole lake is the same. Fact: Thickness can change greatly within one body of water. Test again and again.

Snowshoe and Ski Travel

The Army manual says snow 12 inches (30 cm) deep or more makes walking hard, and almost impossible without snowshoes or skis. Both spread your weight over more area. Learn the technique in person. These points matter most.

  • No snowshoes? The Army says to make a pair from willow, cloth strips, leather, or similar material. It gives no pattern, so expect to adjust.
  • Do not sweat. Wet clothing loses warmth. Open your jacket, shed a layer, or push back your hood before you overheat.
  • Do not travel in a blizzard or whiteout. You cannot judge slope or drops. Distances in clear cold air are usually underestimated.
  • Make camp early so you have daylight to build a shelter (see Part 2 › Shelter).
  • Avoid snow-covered streams. Snow can keep ice from forming. Some frozen rivers have soft, open spots.
  • Snow bridges: cross at a right angle, probe ahead, and spread your weight by crawling or using snowshoes or skis.
  • Slippery slopes: where a slip could hurt you, use an ice axe or traction devices (microspikes or crampons).

Avalanche awareness

This is a pointer, not a course. Take a hands-on class before traveling in snowy mountains (see Part 9 › Natural Disasters).

  • Most avalanches start on slopes of about 30 to 45 degrees. A snowshoe route can wander onto them, so check the ground beside your trail. Low-angle ground can still be hit by slides from steeper slopes above.
  • Look above you. Steep terrain above a flat valley can slide onto you. Do not rest in a runout zone.
  • Terrain traps make a slide deadlier: gullies, creek beds, cliffs, flats below steep slopes, and trees or rocks in the path.
  • Red flags: recent avalanches, cracks shooting out from your feet, a loud "whumpf" of collapsing snow, heavy new snow, wind drifting snow, and rapid warming or rain.
  • Check the regional avalanche forecast before every trip. Seeing no red flags does not mean an avalanche cannot happen.
  • If there is any chance of avalanche terrain, everyone needs a transceiver, shovel, and probe, and must have practiced with them. Avalanche Canada says one or two of the three is not enough. Your companions must be able to dig you out, because minutes count.
  • If in doubt, stay on low-angle ground away from steep slopes.

The Army manual suggests traveling in early morning in avalanche-prone hills. Treat that as one rule of thumb. The forecast and the terrain matter more.

Desert Travel

The Army manual says desert air can reach 140°F (60°C), and sand can run 29 to 40°F (16 to 22°C) hotter than the air. Shade is 20 to 31°F (11 to 17°C) cooler than sunlit air. Nights can turn cold.

Move when it is cool

Rest in shade through the heat of the day. Travel in the evening, at night, and in the early morning. The National Park Service tells Grand Canyon hikers to avoid strenuous hiking between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m. in summer. Big Bend National Park says to avoid hiking in the low desert from about 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. on hot days. The Army agrees that night travel saves water.

Night has its own risks. The Army calls dark, moonless desert nights extremely hazardous. Distances are often underestimated, sometimes by a factor of three. Mirages are least likely at dawn, at dusk, and by moonlight.

Ration sweat, not water

Water is safer in your body than in your canteen. The Army manual says to conserve sweat, not water, and says plainly not to ration water. Big Bend National Park agrees: drink the water you have, carry at least 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day, and eat a salty snack when you drink (see Part 2 › Water). It says a person working hard in the sun at 110°F (43°C) can need about 5 gallons (19 L) a day. It advises about a pint (0.5 L) an hour below 100°F (38°C) and about a quart (1 L) an hour above it. The Army says people who drink only by thirst take in about two-thirds of what they need. Do not overdo it, either. Forcing far more water than you lose, without salt, can cause dangerous low blood sodium (hyponatremia). If you feel bloated or sick, stop forcing fluids (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If you are short of water, make it last with tiny sips. Fact: Rationing raises the risk of heat illness. Cut the water you lose: rest in shade, limit work, and move only when it is cool.

  • Rest in shade, off the ground. Hot sand radiates heat.
  • Keep clothes on, with sleeves down and head and neck covered.
  • Eat little if water is short. Digestion uses water. Salty snacks, or lightly salted water (the Army says about 1/4 teaspoon of salt per quart or liter), replace salt lost in sweat.
  • Check your urine. Pale means enough. Dark means drink more.
  • Sandstorms: wear goggles and cover your nose and mouth. With no shelter, mark your heading, lie down, and wait it out.

Do not

  • Do not strip down. The Army says to keep your clothes on.

Learn the signs of heat illness first (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Mountain Travel

Pace

Be the tortoise. Start the day at a speed you can hold at the end of it. Surges followed by long rests waste energy, so take short, regular breaks instead. On steep climbs, use the rest step: with each stride, lock the back leg straight so your bones, not your muscles, hold your weight for a moment.

Switchbacks

A switchback is a zigzag that trades distance for a gentler slope. Stay on it. Cutting corners wears gullies in the slope, and in high, cold places the damage can take decades to heal. Off-trail on a steep climb, zigzag across the slope instead of going straight up.

Descending

  • Keep your weight low and over your feet, with the downhill knee slightly bent as you land.
  • Take shorter steps on steep ground.
  • Tighten your hip belt so the pack does not swing.
  • Stay alert after a long climb. Many slips come when people relax.

Scree and talus

Scree is small loose rock. Talus is larger blocks. Both can move underfoot.

  • Assume each step will slide. Place your foot on purpose, in short steps, with your weight over your legs.
  • Test big rocks before trusting them. Talus can trigger a rockslide.
  • Do not "surf" down loose scree. A pack makes a slip harder to recover from.
  • Spread out. Never stand directly below someone who might dislodge rock.
  • If the slope is steeper or looser than expected, turn back.

Exposure and altitude

Mountains add cold, wind, lightning, and drops. Altitude illness can begin above about 8,000 feet (2,450 m). Climb gradually, and descend if symptoms worsen (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Jungle and Swamp Travel

In rain forest the Army says you may see only about 50 yards (50 m) ahead. Its advice is to look through the jungle, not at it. Look for natural gaps and game trails, and follow them. In the tropics, travel only in daylight. Darkness comes quickly, and so does dawn.

Using a machete

  • Cut only what you must. Chopping is tiring and slows you in the heat.
  • Cut vines with an upward stroke. The Army says this is quieter. Keep your free hand and legs clear of the blade.
  • Part growth with a stick before reaching in. It knocks off ants and spiders and warns you of snakes.

Leeches

The Army manual lists leeches among the hazards of tropical swamps. Tuck your trousers into your boots and check yourself often. Filter and treat your water: the Army warns that a leech swallowed in water can attach inside the throat or nose, and its bite wounds can become infected (see Part 2 › Water and Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning).

Swamps and bogs

  • Avoid saltwater (mangrove) swamps if you can. Cross at low tide, and watch for crocodiles.
  • Freshwater swamps often have islands where you can leave the water.
  • If channels run through a swamp, a raft may beat wading.
  • Do not walk across bogs or muskeg if you can bypass them. If you must, lie flat, spread your limbs, and move slowly. Laid logs or branches help.

Quicksand facts

Quicksand is water-soaked sand that turns fluid when disturbed. It is about twice as dense as your body, so a person cannot be pulled fully under. Sinking should stop near the waist. The real danger is being stuck as a tide rises.

  1. Do not thrash.
  2. Wriggle your legs slowly, a little at a time, so water seeps in and loosens the sand.
  3. Do not let others yank you. Freeing a stuck foot takes force like lifting a medium-size car, and a hard pull can injure you.
  4. Test doubtful ground first. The Army says to toss a small stone on it. If it sinks, go around. If you must cross, lie flat with arms and legs spread and move slowly, as on a bog.

Moving at Night

Travel at night only when waiting is worse: heat, flood, or a rescue point you can reach. Tropical manuals say to move only when it is light. If you do go, protect your eyes and slow down.

  • Dark adaptation takes time. Eyes need about 20 to 30 minutes to adjust. After bright sun on snow or sand, it can take hours. Wear sunglasses by day and avoid staring at firelight.
  • Use red light. The cells that work in dim light are largely insensitive to red, so a red filter or red lamp keeps your night vision. Use white light only briefly.
  • Close one eye when you must use white light, then open it afterward. It will still be adapted to the dark.
  • Look beside, not at. Dim-light cells sit away from the center of your vision. To see a faint object, look a little to one side of it.
  • Slow down. Moonless, cloudy nights are the worst. If you cannot see the ground, stop and wait for light.
  • Hold your heading with a luminous compass, a star, or stakes (see Part 9 › Maps & Compass).

Carrying Loads

How much?

A common outdoor-retailer guide says a loaded backpacking pack should weigh no more than about 20% of your body weight, and a day pack about 10%. For a 150-pound (68 kg) person, that is about 30 pounds (14 kg) and 15 pounds (7 kg). These are rough upper limits, and experienced hikers treat them as flexible. In an emergency, carry the lightest load that still keeps you warm, dry, hydrated, and able to signal.

Fit a pack

  1. Measure your torso from the bony bump at the base of your neck (C7) down your spine to the top of your hip bones. Pick the pack size from that number, not your height. Size ranges vary by brand.
  2. Seat the hip belt on top of your hip bones, not your soft waist. Make it snug, not pinching. It should carry most of the load.
  3. Check the shoulder straps. If they sit too high or low, the pack is the wrong size for your torso.
  4. Snug the load lifters (the straps from pack top to shoulder straps) to about 45 degrees.
  5. Clip the sternum strap about 1 inch (2.5 cm) below your collarbones. Do not cinch it so tight that it limits breathing.

Pack it

  • Put everything inside. Gear strapped outside swings and feels unstable.
  • Put the heaviest items close to your back, centered between shoulder blades and hips.
  • Keep daytime items, such as rain gear and snacks, within reach.
  • Wear your heaviest clothes and boots instead of carrying them.

Improvised carries

Source note

No agency manual we checked covers these two methods. The horseshoe pack steps follow a backpacking magazine; the travois is described only in general terms. Try both at home before you need them.

Horseshoe (bedroll) pack. Lay a tarp, poncho, or blanket flat, at least about 2.5 by 5 feet (75 by 150 cm). Put heavier items toward the two ends and soft items in the middle, then roll it into a tight sausage. Tie it in several places, then tie the ends together. Wear it like a sash: the soft middle on one shoulder, the ends across the opposite hip. Use knots you can untie quickly. It carries little and can be hard on the shoulder over long distances.

Travois. A traditional drag frame: two long poles joined at the front, spread at the back, with a platform lashed across (see Part 3 › Cordage, Knots & Lashings). You pull the joined end, and the rear ends drag. It suits smooth ground or snow better than rocks and roots. Moving an injured person is a separate problem (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).
