Most survival situations are won or lost in the head before they are won or lost in the body. Fear, panic, bad decisions, and quarrels kill people who had the gear and the skills. This chapter explains what stress does to you, then gives drills: a first-five-minutes routine, breathing, a decision loop, a daily morale plan, group roles, and ways to help children, older adults, and anyone panicking.

Not a substitute for care

This chapter is basic field guidance, not mental health treatment. If anyone talks about ending their life or hurting themselves, do not leave them alone. Stay with them, keep anything dangerous out of reach, and get professional help as soon as you can. In the US, if phones work, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or chat at 988lifeline.org, any time of day; it is also for people worried about someone else. If there is immediate danger, call 911. For care when no help is coming, see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care (mental health in crisis section) and Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming. Odd behavior can also be physical: cold, heat, thirst, low blood sugar, and head injury can all cause confusion, so check the body too (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies and Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).

What Stress Does to You

Stress is a normal response to pressure. The US Army survival manual (FM 21-76) says some stress is useful, because it pushes you to act. Too much turns into distress. Its list of stressors includes injury, illness, uncertainty, harsh weather, hunger, thirst, tiredness, and isolation. Expect several at once.

Figure 1 shows the effects people report most. If you notice these signs, you are not weak or broken. You are a human under load.

Narrowed attention

Kenneth Hill, a researcher who interviewed many lost people, found that moderate arousal can sharpen thinking, but intense fear narrows attention. Familiar landmarks may go unrecognized, and fear pushes people to keep moving. Hill calls the near-total loss of rational thought "woods shock," and notes that experienced outdoor people can get it too. (We read a secondary summary of Hill, not his original chapter.)

A 2025 study measured something similar. Fifty-seven police officers wore heart monitors during simulated mental-health crisis calls. Afterward they remembered the main threat in the scene almost every time (for the knife, 100%). They remembered a backpack in the scene only about a third of the time (31.7%). The authors link this to tunnel vision, but they did not measure vision directly, and the study was small. It cites earlier work on losses of motor skill under stress but did not test them. The practical message is modest: when scared, expect to miss things at the edges, and expect clumsy hands.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: In a disaster, crowds panic and trample each other. Fact: Disaster research summarized by E. L. Quarantelli says panic flight is rare. It needs special conditions: a confined space, immediate high risk, a belief that escape is still possible, and no one to turn to. Most people act in a calm, purposeful way, and many move toward the danger to help others. Brief shock affects very few and passes quickly.

The First Five Minutes: STOP and SURVIVAL

The first moment is the worst. You feel the urge to move, fix, or run. Hill found that fear pushes people into random travel. Search teams advise the opposite. Figure 2 shows the STOP routine a mountain rescue team teaches. STOP comes from search-and-rescue teaching; it is not in FM 21-76, which uses the longer SURVIVAL list below. The extra details in the figure, such as sitting down and taking five breaths, are this guide's additions.

Do each letter in order. If you are not confident of a route, stay put. Stay put too if you are hurt, near exhaustion, or it is getting dark (see Part 9 › Maps & Compass).

Why not just push on? Hill found that lost people rarely stay put on purpose; most who stop are exhausted, asleep, or unconscious. Many, even experienced ones, wrongly believe they are heading out. Backtracking works for people with the skill and patience, but most are reluctant to turn around. Folk rules such as "follow a stream" can mislead; in his Nova Scotia example, a stream more likely ended in a swamp than a town. (See Part 9 › Travel & Movement.)

Calm the Body: Breathing and Grounding

You cannot think your way out of a racing pulse. Slow breathing is the fastest tool you carry, and it is free. A 2023 randomized trial (Balban and others) gave 108 adults one 5-minute daily practice for 28 days: cyclic sighing, box breathing, fast "hyperventilation" breathing, or mindfulness meditation. Anxiety and bad mood eased in all groups, by similar amounts. Breathwork as a whole raised positive mood and slowed resting breathing more than meditation did. But slow "cyclic sighing" was the only technique that clearly beat meditation. Box breathing did not differ significantly from meditation. The study was small, remote, and short, and done in daily life, not a crisis. Treat it as promising, not proven. Box breathing is still widely taught and is easy to remember under stress; the double sigh has the stronger evidence from this one trial.

Drill 1: Box breathing

  1. Sit or stand still. Relax your shoulders.
  2. Breathe in slowly through your nose while you count to 4.
  3. Hold gently while you count to 4.
  4. Breathe out slowly while you count to 4.
  5. Hold gently while you count to 4.
  6. Repeat for four rounds. Then ask yourself: what is the next small job?

The trial set each person's box length by a breath-hold test. People with the shortest test times used 3 to 4 seconds per side; longer tiers used 5 to 6 or 8 to 10. It told people to breathe through the nose if possible and to shorten the counts if they had to strain. Start with 4 and adjust. This guide's rule: if you feel lightheaded, stop, breathe normally, and sit.

Drill 2: Double sigh

In the study, cyclic sighing meant a slow inhale, a second short inhale to fill the lungs, then a long, slow exhale, repeated for 5 minutes. Breathing in through the nose and out through the mouth was suggested. A group can do it together.

Drill 3: Five senses grounding

The Mental Health Foundation suggests this for panic. Name five things you can see, four you can feel, three you can hear, two you can smell, and one you can taste.

Panic: In You and In Others

Signs in yourself

The Mental Health Foundation lists these signs of a panic attack: breathlessness or a choking feeling, a racing or pounding heart, nausea, dizziness, hot or cold flashes, chest or stomach pain, trembling, sweating, tingling in the fingers, and feeling cut off from your body. Most attacks last 5 to 20 minutes and peak within about 10.

Behavior gives away panic too. This guide's list, drawing on Hill: moving without a plan, being unable to make a simple choice, freezing, ignoring advice, or repeating a useless action.

Warning: chest pain

Panic can cause chest pain, but so can a heart attack. The American Heart Association lists these heart attack signs: chest pain or pressure; pain spreading to an arm or shoulder; jaw, neck, or back pain; shortness of breath; and lightheadedness, nausea, or vomiting. Women are somewhat more likely to have shortness of breath, nausea, and back or jaw pain. If these signs are present, or the person is older or ill, or you are unsure, treat it as a heart attack: call 911 if you can (see Part 0 › Red Pages). Do not assume it is "just panic."

If it is you

  1. Tell yourself: this is temporary and it will pass. (Rule out real danger first; see the warning above.)
  2. Slow your breathing (Figure 3).
  3. Stay where you are if it is safe. Fleeing can make you fear that place later.
  4. Use the five-senses exercise or describe an object in detail.
  5. When it eases, drink water, sit, and pick one small job.

If it is someone else

The Mental Health Foundation says a companion can help by telling the person what is happening and reassuring them that it will pass and the symptoms will not harm them. A survivor guide hosted by the Minnesota Department of Health adds: shield people from onlookers and reminders of the event, and let them talk if they wish, but do not probe.

Do not

  • Do not shout "calm down." It dismisses the feeling.
  • Do not leave the person alone if they are confused or talking about self-harm.
  • Do not let a panicking person run off into the dark, water, or rough ground.

Deciding Under Stress

Beat the first mistake: disbelief

Quarantelli's research summary says that the first response to a warning is usually disbelief, not action. People explain away early signs: a loud noise is a truck backfiring, a shaking house is passing traffic. He calls this the "normalcy bias." People read vague or mixed messages in the most hopeful way. They are more likely to act when the threat seems lasting, personal, near, and certain, and when others confirm it. So check, then move early. If you feel the urge to wait and see, ask a hard question: what would I do if this were real? Do the cheap, safe part of that now, such as packing, filling water, and telling someone your plan (see Part 0 › Golden Rules).

The decision loop

Big plans break when conditions change. Use short loops instead (Figure 5). Each loop is one small step, so a bad call costs little.

A checklist for any big choice

This is the guide's own list. Run it when you feel pushed to hurry.

  1. Is anyone in danger right now? If yes, deal with that first (see Part 0 › Red Pages).
  2. Separate facts from guesses. "The creek is rising" is a fact. "It will flood" is a guess.
  3. List two or three options, including "wait and watch."
  4. Ask for each: What is the worst case? Can I undo it? What does it cost in water, daylight, and energy?
  5. Choose and say it out loud. NOLS advises stating and confirming decisions and inviting questions.
  6. Set a time to check. "If the water has not dropped by noon, we move."

Task lists

When your head is full, paper holds what it cannot. List everything that needs doing. Mark the top three: what could kill you soonest, what helps most, what is easy to finish. Do the first. Cross it off. This turns a fog of worries into a line of jobs.

Mental shortcuts that trap groups

Ian McCammon studied recreational avalanche accidents and found six shortcuts that pull groups toward risk, remembered as FACETS. They apply to any group.

Familiarity: groups take more risk on ground they know. Acceptance: people take risks to win approval. Consistency: groups committed to a goal push on as conditions worsen, most of all in groups of four or more. Expert halo: novices were more likely to follow an older or more skilled leader into danger than novice groups that decided together. Tracks: time pressure or a rare chance pushes groups to act. Social facilitation: seeing other groups nearby makes people feel safer than they should.

Beat them by asking, out loud: "What if we are wrong?" and by giving every member, even the newest, permission to say "stop."

Tired, hungry brains decide badly

FM 21-76 counts hunger, thirst, and fatigue as stressors. Before a big choice you cannot undo, drink, eat if you can, and rest. If it can wait until morning, let it wait.

Morale and the Will to Survive

FM 21-76 says that skills matter, but the will to survive is essential, and describes it as the refusal to give up. It recommends preparing your mind ahead of time: know your fears, stay realistic, keep a positive outlook, and train. It also lists stress tools: relaxation, time management, and reframing a problem.

Will is built from routines, small wins, and people. The manual notes that depression often grows from a cycle of frustration and anger, until a person feels nothing can be done. Short sadness about loved ones can renew your drive, but long hopelessness drains the will to live.

Build a daily routine

A routine gives a hard day a shape. Figure 6 is a model day. Adjust it to your light, weather, and tasks. A steady rhythm also helps children and older adults, as later sections explain.

Morale habits that work

  • Keep your hands and mind busy. The manual says loneliness and boredom can deepen low mood, but they can also bring out creativity. Make things, fix things, plan things.
  • Count small wins. At the evening meeting, each person names one thing that went right. (This guide's habit, not a research finding.)
  • Be realistic. Do not promise rescue by Tuesday. A broken promise hurts morale more than an honest "we do not know."
  • Talk. Let people grieve and say the names of those they lost.

Watch for slipping morale

This guide's signs, not a clinical test: someone stops eating, drinking, or caring for themselves, withdraws, sleeps all day, or says there is no point. Respond early. Give a job, sit with them, and make sure they eat and drink. If they talk of death or self-harm, do not leave them alone. Call or text 988 if you can reach it, and see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care (mental health in crisis section).

Groups and Leadership

Hill found that being in a group tends to keep people calmer and more rational, and children especially. Quarantelli's summary adds that people confirm warnings with those close to them, and that evacuations are mostly made by intact families and friend groups, who often wait until everyone is accounted for. Use that pull, but give the group structure.

Leadership that fits the moment

The Wilderness Medicine Institute of NOLS leadership chapter describes directive, consultative, and consensus styles. A directive style is common in a crisis, but a good leader still draws on the team's knowledge. Every style needs competence, self-awareness, tolerance for uncertainty, and clear communication. A leader need not be expert at everything and should hand specialized jobs to those who are.

NOLS tells leaders to know their own habits, such as rushing or taking on too much, to plan rest and shift changes, and to admit when overloaded. A NOLS article on the Andes crash survivors (a general-audience, secondary source) makes a similar point. Their formal captain did not always lead, and others stepped up as conditions changed. Roberto Canessa led through judgment, not title.

Habits that hold a group together

  • Give every person a job. NOLS says each person should know their task and how it fits the big picture. Finish one task before starting the next.
  • Main jobs first: care for the injured, feed the team, make camp.
  • Hold short briefings at each change of phase: plan, reason, what to watch for, who does what.
  • Handle conflict by timing. NOLS advises setting personal differences aside during the emergency and talking them through afterward.
  • Debrief after a big task: what we planned, what happened, why, and what to do next time.
  • Make it safe to speak. Invite concerns, and watch for FACETS traps, especially expert halo.

Children in a Crisis

Children read adults closely. The table below combines CDC and Child Mind Institute guidance; the age bands differ slightly between sources. The Child Mind Institute advises you to stay calm, keep your own worries out of earshot, and give plenty of hugs. Keep routines for meals, sleep, and rules, or make new ones if you are displaced. Share information briefly and honestly, let children ask questions, and listen without lecturing. "I don't know" is a fine answer.

Younger children

The Psychological First Aid field guide (with the National Child Traumatic Stress Network and the National Center for PTSD) gives parents these tips for preschoolers:

  • Fear of being alone: stay calm and hide your own fear. If you must leave, say when you will be back, then be back on time.
  • Step-backs such as thumb-sucking: respond calmly and never shame the child.
  • Death: young children may think it is not permanent, or that their thoughts caused it. Give simple, honest answers.

Teach children now to stay put if lost; Hill's summary mentions Hug-a-Tree training.

Who needs extra watching

The Minnesota-hosted guide says youth react more strongly if they saw the event, lost someone, were forced from home, had earlier trauma, or have an adult nearby who is struggling. Limit disaster news. The CDC, the Child Mind Institute, and the PFA parent tips all advise this; coverage can make the event seem ongoing or close by, and it can stir fears that it will happen again.

When to worry

The Child Mind Institute says children may look fine for the first month and show symptoms later. The CDC advises talking with a pediatrician, school counselor, or child psychologist if a child stays anxious, fearful, sad, or angry for more than two to four weeks, if problems get worse instead of better, or if reactions keep disrupting school, friends, or family life. Get help promptly if anxiety does not ease, or if a child hears voices, sees things that are not there, becomes paranoid, has panic attacks, or has thoughts of hurting themselves or others. If a child talks of self-harm, do not leave them alone, and call or text 988 if you can. With no clinician available, keep the child close and keep routines (see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care).

Older Adults in a Crisis

The Texas Ready disaster-preparedness site lists reactions that are common in older adults after a disaster: confusion or disorientation, memory trouble, avoiding the full picture of the damage, fear of losing independence or being moved into care, withdrawing, hopelessness about starting over, irritability or distrust, and grief that stirs up older losses. They often recover more slowly, and scammers target them.

Warning signs

  • Worse memory, poor decisions, trouble paying attention.
  • Missed medicines, poor hygiene, declining health.
  • Less movement, sleep trouble, nightmares.

Confusion in an older person may be physical: thirst, heat, cold, infection, or missed medicine. Check these first (see Part 7 › Illness in the Field). This is the guide's judgment, not a point from the Texas page.

How to help

  • Check in often in person, and listen to worries.
  • Keep their routine steady as far as you can.
  • Protect their independence. Give a real role in the group, such as a lookout or a storyteller, and ask their advice. This is this guide's advice.
  • Keep them connected to family, friends, and faith groups.
  • Plan for medicines, glasses, and hearing aids before a crisis (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).

Afterward: Recovery and When to Get Help

Most people recover on their own, with sleep, food, safety, and support from others. Give people time. Do not push anyone to retell the story or "get over it." Gentle listening, a steady routine, and trusted company do the most good.

Seek a doctor or mental health professional if distress does not ease, if sleep, appetite, and daily tasks stay badly disrupted, or if someone has panic attacks, hears or sees things that are not there, or thinks of harming themselves or others. Caregivers wear down too. In the US, call or text 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org for anyone in emotional distress or a suicidal crisis, including when you are worried about someone else. The CDC also lists SAMHSA's Disaster Distress Hotline, 1-800-985-5990 (call or text), for disaster-related distress. Call 911 for immediate danger. When no professional help can be reached, see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care (mental health in crisis section).

Train your mind before the crisis

FM 21-76 says training helps you function despite fear and builds the confidence to control it. Practice box breathing daily, run STOP on every hike, take a first aid class, and rehearse your family plan.
