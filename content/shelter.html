Cold, wind, rain, and heat can disable you long before thirst or hunger do. Shelter is how you stop the weather from taking your body heat, or cooking it. This chapter shows how to pick a safe spot, insulate yourself from the ground, build quick shelters for forest, snow, desert, and wet tropics, and stay warm or cool once you are inside.

Why Shelter Comes First

A common survival rule of thumb says you can last about three hours without shelter in harsh weather. Treat that as a loose guide, not a fact. It is a teaching shortcut and is not scientifically exact. It still makes the point: exposure works fast, and wind, wet clothes, and cold ground speed it up. Hypothermia can start in air that is only 30 to 60°F (-1 to 16°C) when wind or rain is added.

Make a shelter early, while you still have the strength and judgment to do it well. Early signs of hypothermia are shivering, slurred speech, stumbling, and poor judgment. Watch your partners, since people often miss these signs in themselves.

Warning

A wet, shivering, confused person is in danger now. Get them out of the wind and off the ground first. Do not make them hike on to find a "better" site.

How You Lose Heat

Your body loses heat in five ways. (Some guides count four and fold breathing into the others.) Knowing them tells you what a shelter has to fix.

PathWhat happensWhere it hurts most
ConductionHeat moves into a colder thing you touch.Lying on cold ground, snow, or rock. Wilderness medicine guidelines say a large amount of heat can be lost to the ground.
ConvectionMoving air or water carries away the warm layer next to your skin.Wind, and any contact with water.
RadiationYour body gives off heat to colder surroundings.Open, clear nights. Covering skin and sitting inside a shelter cuts it.
EvaporationWater turns to vapor and pulls heat from your skin.Sweat and wet clothes.
RespirationYou breathe out warm, moist air.Cold, dry air. It adds to the total but is hard to reduce.

The sources I checked give no firm percentages for these five. They agree on the pattern: cold ground, wind, and wet clothing are the big, fixable losses in the field. Insulate underneath you, block the wind, and stay dry. Slow down or open layers before you sweat through them.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A warm hat is all you need to stay warm. Fact: Your head and neck do lose significant heat, so cover them. But heat leaves from every uncovered surface, so you must also insulate the ground under you and block the wind.

Choosing a Site

Take a few minutes to read the terrain before you build. A good shelter in a bad spot can still kill you.

Check this list

  • Dead trees and limbs ("widowmakers"). They can fall at any time, even on calm days. If you must camp near a risky tree, stay outside its fall zone, which on flat ground is about 1.5 times the height of the part that could fail. Slopes can make that zone bigger. Do not tie hammocks to dead or unstable trees.
  • Flash-flood paths. Do not sleep in dry washes, gullies, or low ground beside streams. Dry streambeds can rise in minutes with no rain where you are. Most flash flood deaths happen at night.
  • Cold-air pools. On clear, calm nights, air cooled by the ground becomes dense and drains downhill. It collects in valley bottoms like water in a basin. Ravines, narrow valleys, and creek beds are colder and also hold more insects. Camp partway up the slope instead.
  • Exposed ridgetops. Wind drives convection, so look for a spot behind a rise, boulder, or thick stand of live trees.
  • Water's edge. Stay above the high-water mark. Low ground floods and breeds insects.
  • Other hazards. Avoid rockslide chutes, avalanche paths, loose rocks above you, and spots with insect nests, snakes, or poisonous plants.
  • Visibility to rescuers. If you are waiting for rescue, hang something bright near the shelter and stay near an open spot where searchers can see you.

Insulate from the Ground First

Ground, snow, and rock pull heat out of you by conduction. Your body weight also squashes the loft out of clothing and bags, so the layer beneath you does the least insulating. Fix this before you worry about the roof.

The US Army manual says never to sleep directly on the ground in the cold. Use pine boughs, grass, leaves, or similar dry material. For a debris hut, it calls for about 1 foot (30 cm) of insulating material inside. Pile your bed thicker than seems needed, because it packs down under your weight.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: It is a proven fact that you lose 80 percent of your body heat to the ground. Fact: The Army manual says that at rest you may lose as much as 80 percent to the ground, but wilderness medicine guidelines give no such number. They say only that a large amount of heat can be lost to the ground. Use the idea, not the number.

Debris Hut

The debris hut is the classic forest shelter, a big leafy sleeping bag. It is small on purpose, because a tight space is easier to warm with your own body.

Steps

  1. Pick a site with plenty of sticks and leaves, away from dead trees and standing water.
  2. Find a ridgepole a bit longer than you are tall with your arm stretched over your head. A common size is about 8 feet (2.4 m). It must hold your weight.
  3. Prop the high end on a stump, tree crook, or a two-stake tripod, at about hip to waist height. Set the low end on the ground.
  4. Lean ribbing sticks along both sides of the ridgepole, close together. Leave a small door at the high end. Leave room for your body plus insulation, and keep the space snug.
  5. Lay thinner sticks and brush crosswise over the ribs to make a lattice. This holds the debris up.
  6. Pile light, dry leaves, grass, or pine needles over the frame until the layer is thick.
  7. Fill the inside with your dry bedding layer and pack it down where you will lie.
  8. Lay sticks over the pile so wind cannot blow it off. Crawl in feet first and plug the door with a shirt stuffed with leaves.

How thick?

Sources disagree a little. The US Army manual says at least 1 meter (about 3.3 ft) of debris, and says thicker is better. A wilderness school says at least 3 feet (0.9 m). Another school suggests about 2 feet (0.6 m), more if rain is likely. Aim for the thicker figure, 3 feet (1 m) or more, especially in cold or wet weather. Wet leaves still insulate somewhat, but dry material traps more air.

Warning

Do not make the hut bigger than you need. Extra air space is extra cold air. Also check for ants, and for snakes and spiders in the leaves, before you crawl in.

Lean-To

A lean-to goes up faster than a debris hut. It only blocks wind and rain from one side, and it holds in little heat by itself. It works best when you build a fire in front of it and put a reflector behind the fire.

Field-expedient lean-to (Army manual)

  1. Find two trees or upright poles about 6 feet (2 m) apart.
  2. Tie a horizontal pole between them at waist to chest height.
  3. Lean five to eight long poles against the crossbar. Space them close.
  4. Weave saplings or vines across the poles.
  5. Cover with brush, leaves, or grass. Start at the bottom and work up, like roof shingles.
  6. Pile dry bedding under the roof.

Put the back of the lean-to to the wind. The open side then faces away from the wind and toward your fire. A lean-to gives no cover if the wind shifts, so check the sky and the wind often.

Tarp Pitches

If you carry a tarp, you can have a roof in minutes. Four pitches cover most needs.

  • A-frame. Tie a line between two trees at head height, drape the tarp over it, and stake out both sides. Use a steep slope, about 45 degrees or more, so water runs off. Leave 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm) of gap at the ground for air. Good in rain and medium wind.
  • Lean-to. Raise one edge on poles or trees and stake the other to the ground. Face the high side away from wind and rain. It gives no cover from rain blown sideways. A fire in front can reflect heat in.
  • Diamond. Fold the tarp on the diagonal. Stake two corners to the ground and raise the third on a pole or from a tree. It copes with changing wind but only partly covers you from above.
  • Tarp tent. Set up an inner mesh tent first. Drape the tarp over like an A-frame and stake the sides wide to leave an air gap. It gives full cover and bug protection.

A fifth pitch, the plow point, stakes one end to the ground and lifts the back end on a pole or line. Point the low, staked end straight into the wind.

Warning

An enclosed tarp or tent with no airflow gets damp inside from your breath. Frost or drips can soak your bag. Leave a gap for air, and never burn a stove, lantern, or heater in a closed shelter.

Natural Shelters

Fallen trees, thick evergreens, and rock overhangs can save work. Use them as a base and add walls and bedding.

  • Fallen tree. The trunk gives you one wall. Lean branches against it and add debris. Make sure it is stable and will not shift.
  • Rock overhang. It gives a roof but not a safe floor. Look up for loose rock before you sit down. Do not camp under loose rocks. Block the open side with brush or a tarp and put insulation under you.

Caves

Treat caves with great caution. Rangers warn of loose rock, falls, cold, and total darkness if a light fails. Caves can also flood suddenly, and many cave deaths are drownings. Stay in the dry mouth of a cave if you use one at all. Do not explore deeper; rangers say never to go caving alone and to carry several reliable lights each. Old ruins and caves often hold snakes, spiders, and scorpions. Many caves are also closed to slow white-nose syndrome, a disease that is killing bats. Never enter a closed cave, and follow any posted rules about cleaning your gear.

Snow Shelters

Snow is a good insulator because it holds still air. A snow shelter can be much warmer than the air outside. It is also harder and riskier to build than a debris hut. A snow roof can collapse while you dig or later, and a shelter with a blocked vent can run short of fresh air. Learn this in a class if you can.

Snow trench

A trench is the simplest snow shelter. You get below the wind. The Army manual describes the goal as getting below snow and wind level. In firm snow, cut blocks to roof it. Otherwise use branches, a tarp, or a poncho, and pile snow on top.

  1. Choose a spot away from avalanche slopes and from trees that could blow over.
  2. Dig a trench just long and wide enough to lie in.
  3. Cover it with snow blocks, branches, or a tarp.
  4. Put boughs or other dry insulation on the floor.
  5. Make one small entrance. Plug it with a snow block or a pack when you are inside.
  6. Leave a small gap for air. Clear snow from the roof if it keeps snowing, to avoid collapse.

Quinzhee

A quinzhee (say "KWIN-zee") is a mound of piled snow that you hollow out. It works when the snow is too shallow or loose for a cave. Test the snow first: pack a snowball. If it crumbles, the snow will not bond well enough for a quinzhee.

  1. Pile snow into a mound at least 5 feet (1.5 m) tall and 7 to 10 feet (2 to 3 m) across. One club suggests making it 7 to 8 feet (2 to 2.5 m) tall if you can manage it.
  2. Let it sit at least an hour so the snow bonds (sinters). One club says 60 to 90 minutes is usually enough; other instructors wait several hours. When in doubt, wait longer.
  3. Push sticks about a foot (30 cm) into the mound from the outside, all over. You will use them as thickness gauges.
  4. Dig a low entrance tunnel just big enough to crawl through. Sources differ on its placement: one says the downwind side, another says the low side of the mound. Choose a side out of the wind that keeps the entrance lowest.
  5. Hollow the inside slowly, working from the entrance up and in. Scoop. Do not pry, since prying can weaken the walls. Work with a partner so one person stays outside (see the warning below).
  6. Stop hollowing when you reach the stick tips. Light shining through a wall means it is too thin.
  7. Make a raised sleeping bench higher than the entrance (see Figure 7). Leave the lower floor near the door as the "cold sink."
  8. Poke a vent hole through the roof. NOLS sizes it by temperature: about 4 inches (10 cm) at 30°F (-1°C), 2 inches (5 cm) at 0°F (-18°C), and 1 inch (2.5 cm) at -30°F (-34°C). Check it often, especially while snow is falling or blowing, and keep it open.
  9. If you can, let the finished shelter firm up for another hour or more before you sleep in it.

Aim for walls at least 1 foot (30 cm) thick, and 2 feet (60 cm) is better. Make the bottom wall thicker than the top.

Warning

Snow shelters can collapse on you or run short of fresh air while you sleep. Follow every rule below.

  • Never burn a stove, candle, or any flame inside. Cook outside and use LED lights. Flames use up air and can make carbon monoxide, which can poison you without warning. The Army manual allows cooking in the lower entrance area; NOLS and other instructors say never cook inside. Choose the careful advice.
  • Keep the vent hole open, and check it often.
  • Keep your shovel inside, next to you, so you can dig out after a storm or a partial collapse.
  • Dig with a partner, and keep one person outside at all times. If you must build alone, NOLS advises carrying an emergency beacon in case you get trapped.
  • Mark the shelter with a bright flag or tarp so no one walks, sleds, or drives onto the roof.
  • Never build on or below an avalanche slope, in an avalanche path, or under trees that could fall.

Snow cave and igloo

A snow cave is dug into a deep, firm drift, ideally about 10 feet (3 m) deep, per the Army manual. It needs an arched roof, walls and ceiling at least 1 foot (30 cm) thick, a vent shaft, and a sleeping platform higher than the entrance. An igloo is a dome built from cut blocks of firm snow. It takes practice and good snow. Get hands-on training before you count on one.

Tree wells

Warning

Tree wells are deadly. A tree well is a hollow of deep, loose snow around the base of a tree, often hidden by low branches. People usually fall in head-first. The loose snow packs around them, they cannot move, and they can suffocate about as fast as someone drowning. In research tests, 90 percent of people could not rescue themselves. Give trees a wide berth in deep, soft snow, and travel with a partner who keeps you in sight.

  • If you start to go in: yell or blow a whistle. Grab branches or the trunk, roll, and try to keep your feet below your head.
  • If you are buried: do not struggle. Make a space around your face, keep your airway clear, and stay calm to save air.
  • If your partner goes in: do not leave to get help. Call for help with a whistle, shout, or phone, and dig at once. Go for the airway from the side, and take care not to knock more snow into the hole.

Desert Shade Shelter

In the desert, the problem is heat. Sand and rock can run 30 to 40°F (16 to 22°C) hotter than the air. In the Army manual's example, sand can reach 140°F (60°C) when the air is 110°F (43°C). Shade is the first goal. Do not lie directly on hot ground.

  1. Build it before the heat of the day. Digging in midday heat makes you sweat and lose water.
  2. Dig a trench about 18 to 24 inches (45 to 60 cm) deep and long and wide enough to lie in.
  3. Pile the dug sand on three sides.
  4. Stretch one layer of cloth or tarp over the trench. Use light color on top so it reflects sun.
  5. Add a second layer 12 to 18 inches (30 to 45 cm) above the first. This gap is the point of the design.

The Army survival manual says a double layer can lower the inside temperature by about 20 to 40°F (11 to 22°C), and that a below-ground shelter can cut midday heat by as much as 30 to 40°F (16 to 22°C). All of the numbers in this section, including the trench depth and the air gap, come from that one manual. I could not confirm them elsewhere, so treat them as rough guidance: "helps a lot," not a promise.

Jungle and Swamp: Get Off the Ground

In wet, hot country, the ground is damp and full of insects and animals. The answer is a raised bed. The Army manual's swamp bed uses four trees or poles in a rectangle, strong enough to hold you and your gear. Bamboo is ideal. Lash two poles across the width and more along the length. Set the frame high enough to stay above tides and high water. Cover it with broad leaves or grass. You can also put a roof of tarp or leaves over it.

A tarp hammock is another option. One guide ties an 8 by 10 foot tarp, rolled into a bundle, between trees about 10 feet (3 m) apart. Use only sturdy, live trees, never dead ones. Tie high, because the hammock sags once the knots tighten. In the tropics, protect against mosquitoes, which can carry disease. Use netting if you have it.

Staying Warm

  • Keep your layers dry. Wet clothing is a major heat thief. Put on rain gear or warm clothing before you get wet. Open or remove layers before you sweat. Store spare clothing and your sleeping bag in plastic bags.
  • Eat and drink. Your body burns food to make heat. Eat high-calorie food through the day, before you are worn out, and keep drinking. Skip alcohol, which increases heat loss. I found no firm evidence that a particular food just before bed helps, so I do not claim one.
  • Hot water bottles. A sealed bottle of warm water in your bag adds heat. Cold skin burns easily. Wrap the bottle in cloth, and check your skin every 20 to 30 minutes for redness. Wilderness medicine guidelines report burns from lukewarm water applied right on the skin.
  • Hot rocks. Warm rocks can heat a bed, but heated rocks can crack or burst and throw sharp pieces. Never heat rocks from rivers, creeks, or other wet places, and never use porous rocks such as sandstone, shale, limestone, or pumice. Water trapped inside can turn to steam. Use only dry, solid rock gathered from a dry spot (see Part 2 › Fire). Wrap hot rocks in cloth and keep them from touching your skin.
  • Share body heat. Two people in one bag or sleeping close share warmth and can feel better. In a hypothermia case, guidelines say this does not rewarm someone faster than their own shivering. Use it for comfort, not as a cure.

Do not

  • Do not drink alcohol to "warm up."
  • Do not put hot objects directly against numb or cold skin.
  • Do not sleep with a stove, lamp, or fire burning in a closed shelter.
  • Do not rub or heat the arms and legs of a severely cold, confused person. Handle them gently, keep them lying flat, and get help.

Staying Cool

  • Find shade and rest through the hottest hours. Park rangers at Grand Canyon advise against hiking from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. in summer. Rest in shade then and travel in the cooler morning, evening, or night.
  • Wet your clothing. Soaking a shirt, bandana, and hat helps you cool as the water evaporates. Wear loose, light-colored clothing. Do not wet clothes with mineral-laden water, which the Army manual says can cause a rash.
  • Go below the surface. Digging a trench puts you where the ground and sand are cooler than the surface. See the desert shelter above.
  • Drink to thirst and eat. Park advice is to drink when thirsty and stop when satisfied. Do not force fluids. Eat salty snacks along with your water to avoid low blood sodium.
  • Watch for heat illness. Warning signs include headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps, and low urine output. Fainting, confusion, or an altered mental state can mean heat stroke. This is an emergency. Move the person to shade, get them wet and cool, and seek help.
