When phones fail, radios, written messages, and a family plan are how you reach people. This chapter explains which radios need a license, how far they really reach, how to get a ham license, how to send a clear message by voice or by Morse code, and what to do when you have no electronics at all.

Choosing a Radio Service

This chapter builds on the radio comparison in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication. Read that first for the basics. Here we go deeper on what each service is good for.

FRS and GMRS: the family radios

Family Radio Service (FRS) radios need no individual license. The FCC says FRS has 22 channels, and all of them are shared with GMRS. General Mobile Radio Service (GMRS) does need a license. The FCC lists the applicant age as 18 or older, a 10-year term, and coverage for the license holder's family. The FCC gives GMRS range as one to twenty-five miles, depending on equipment and terrain.

Use these for short jobs: keeping two vehicles in touch, scouting a trail, or talking to a neighbor. Most blister-pack radios sold as "FRS" are cheap and fine for that.

Repeaters

A repeater is a radio station placed on a tall hill or tower. It hears your weak signal and sends it out again at higher power from a high spot. Two handheld radios that cannot reach each other can both reach a repeater. The FCC notes that GMRS allows repeaters. Ham radio has many more of them. A repeater needs power. Many have battery or generator backup, but do not count on it.

CB, marine VHF, and NOAA

CB radio needs no individual license. Channel 9 (27.065 MHz) is set aside for emergencies and help for travelers. Marine VHF channel 16 is the distress and calling channel; a recreational boat in US waters that is not required to carry a radio generally needs no station license (US Coast Guard). NOAA Weather Radio is receive-only.

Legal note

In a real emergency, use any radio you have to call for help. For everyday use, get the license the service requires. Rules change, so check the FCC pages in the Sources before you buy a radio for a license-required service.

Range and Line of Sight

These radios use VHF and UHF signals. They travel mostly in straight lines. Hills, dense woods, buildings, and the curve of the Earth all cut them off. That is why the FCC range for GMRS is a wide span (1 to 25 miles) and not one number.

A rule of thumb

Height helps more than power. As a rough guide, the radio horizon in miles is about 1.4 times the square root of the antenna height in feet. This is a widely used approximation from line-of-sight geometry (it allows for radio waves bending slightly over the Earth's curve). It is not a figure from an agency. At 6 feet (1.8 m) that is about 3.5 miles (5.6 km). From a 100-foot (30 m) hill it is about 14 miles (23 km). Add the horizons of both radios to estimate the distance between them. Trees, buildings, and weak batteries will cut it down.

  1. Stand in the open, not in a ravine or against a cliff.
  2. Hold the radio upright, with the antenna straight up and away from your body.
  3. If you get no answer, move uphill or to a ridge and try again.
  4. Keep your call short in case the signal is marginal.

Getting a Ham License

Amateur (ham) radio gives you the widest reach of any hobby radio, including repeaters and long-distance bands. It also gives you a ready-made community of people who practice emergency communication. You need an FCC license. A license is valid for 10 years (47 CFR 97.25).

How the process works

  1. Pick the entry class, called Technician. Its test covers rules, safety, and basic radio theory.
  2. Study the public question pool with a book, class, or free online practice tests.
  3. Find an exam session. Volunteer examiners give the tests, organized by Volunteer Examiner Coordinators (VECs). ARRL is one VEC (ARRL VEC; FCC VEC page).
  4. Ask the session host what ID and fee to bring. Fees and formats vary by group, so this guide does not list them.
  5. Pass the test. Your call sign is issued through the FCC.

No Morse code test is required for any license class (47 CFR 97.501 and 97.503). Learning Morse is still useful (see below).

ARES and RACES

Two groups often come up. The Amateur Radio Emergency Service (ARES) is run by ARRL. The Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service (RACES) is tied to government emergency management. Both use licensed hams to carry messages when other systems fail. Details about joining differ by county, so contact your local emergency management office or ARRL section. This guide did not verify membership rules or how each program is organized; treat this paragraph as an introduction only.

NOAA Weather Radio and SAME

NOAA Weather Radio (NWR) broadcasts on seven VHF frequencies: 162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500, 162.525, and 162.550 MHz. The National Weather Service says there are more than 1,000 transmitters covering all 50 states, nearby coastal waters, Puerto Rico, the US Virgin Islands, and the Pacific territories. You need a special receiver or scanner. NWR carries weather warnings, watches, and forecasts. It also carries other hazards, such as earthquakes, chemical releases, and AMBER alerts.

What SAME does

Specific Area Message Encoding (SAME) is a short digital burst sent before an alert. It names the type of message, the area (usually a county), and when the alert expires. The NWS says the longest expiration is 6 hours. After the burst comes a 10-second 1050 Hz tone. An end-of-alert burst follows the voice message.

  1. Buy a SAME-capable weather radio with battery backup.
  2. Find the SAME code for your county and any nearby counties you travel to. Use the NWS list.
  3. Program the codes. The radio will then ignore alerts for other counties in the transmitter's range.
  4. Test it each month. A radio that cannot hear the transmitter will stay silent, and you will not know.

Warning

A tone-only receiver sounds for every alert the transmitter sends, in any county. A SAME radio is quieter and more useful. Do not turn the alarm off because it wakes you at night. The NWS says alarms are reserved for the most dangerous events.

Morse Code

Morse code needs no voice, no microphone, and little power. A flashlight, a whistle, a tapped rock, or a radio key can carry it. The letters below come from ITU-R Recommendation M.1677, the same table used in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication.

Timing

Morse is all about spacing. A dot is one unit. A dash is three units. Use one unit of silence between the dots and dashes inside a letter, three between letters, and seven between words. The dash length (3 dots) is from ITU-R M.1677. The gap lengths of 1, 3, and 7 are the commonly used standard values. This writer could not re-open the ITU text to check them in this session, so confirm them there if you need an exact reference.

Learning it

  1. Learn the sound, not the picture. Say "di" for a dot and "dah" for a dash.
  2. Start with a few letters (E, T, A, N, S, O). Add more slowly.
  3. Practice SOS (three short, three long, three short) until it is automatic.
  4. Send it with a light, whistle, or tapping, with a clear pause between letters.

Tip

For plain distress, you do not need all 36 characters. Learn S O S, plus Y E S and N O, and keep the card with your kit.

The Phonetic Alphabet

Letters like B, D, and P sound alike on a weak radio. The phonetic alphabet gives each letter a clear word. Say the word, not the letter, when you spell names, places, and codes. The spellings below (including Alfa and Juliett) are the standard international ones. This table was cross-checked against Wikipedia only; it is not safety-critical.

  1. Say the word you are spelling, then spell it. Example: "Smith. Sierra, Mike, India, Tango, Hotel."
  2. Say numbers one digit at a time. "412" is "four, one, two."
  3. Slow down. A slower message with clear words beats a fast one.

Prowords and Radio Etiquette

Prowords are short spoken signals that keep a conversation clear. The Coast Guard's MAYDAY routine uses "OVER" at the end of a call (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). The meanings below are common voice-radio practice. This guide did not check them against an official voice-procedure manual, and different groups use them a little differently. Agree on them with your group in advance.

WordCommon meaning
OverI have finished talking and expect a reply.
OutI have finished and expect no reply.
RogerI heard and understood. It does not mean "yes."
WilcoI will do it. It means I received and will comply.
Say againPlease repeat. Often used in place of "repeat."
BreakA pause between parts of a long message. Some groups use it to mean "interrupt."

Good manners on the air

  1. Listen first. Do not talk over someone.
  2. Say who you are calling, then who you are: "Camp, this is Trail Two."
  3. Keep messages short. Stop after each piece so others can answer.
  4. Use plain words. Do not use slang codes unless your group agreed on them.
  5. Do not send false alarms. A hoax MAYDAY can lead to prosecution (US Coast Guard).
  6. If someone calls an emergency, stop your chat and keep the channel clear.

Do not

  • Say "over and out." The two mean opposite things. Choose one.
  • Give your exact position on an open channel if you fear theft or harm. Anyone with a radio can hear you.
  • Rely on one radio. Bring a second way to reach help.

A Simple Message Format

A good message answers four questions in a fixed order: who, where, what, and what you need. A fixed order helps when you are scared or the signal is weak. It helps the listener write it down. It also matches the order in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication › What to Say When You Reach Help.

The ICS-213 in plain words

Emergency agencies use a standard sheet called the ICS-213 General Message. FEMA lists it as a three-part form that any message originator may prepare. It is not normally part of the written incident plan. As usually printed, it has lines for who the message is to, who it is from, a subject, a date and time, the message itself, a sign-off by the sender or approver, and a space for a reply. The three copies let the sender keep one, the carrier take one, and the receiver answer on the third. The FEMA page confirms only the three-part layout and who prepares it; the field list is the commonly printed layout, so check a current copy if you need an exact one.

Sending a message

  1. Write it down before you speak or send it.
  2. Put the most urgent need first if the signal is poor.
  3. Send it in short chunks. Spell names with the phonetic alphabet.
  4. Ask the receiver to read it back. Fix any errors.
  5. Note the time you sent it and who got it.

The Family Communication Plan

A plan made before a disaster is the most useful communication tool you can have. Ready.gov advises these steps.

  1. Choose one contact person for everyone in the family. Pick someone out of town, since they may be easier to reach in a disaster (Ready.gov).
  2. Put the contact on paper and in each phone. Give each person a copy for a backpack or wallet.
  3. Pick safe meeting places in your home, your neighborhood, and outside your town. Plan for pets too.
  4. Meet, rehearse, and update the plan as your needs change.

Text or call?

Ready.gov says phone lines may be overloaded and that texting is often easier and keeps lines open for emergency workers. FEMA also says texts often get through when calls do not. Send short texts first. Save battery by lowering screen brightness and using low-power or airplane mode between messages (FEMA, as cited in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). Phone coverage in the backcountry is often missing, so a phone plan is not a radio plan.

Tip

Put the out-of-area contact's number in a child's backpack on paper. A child's phone may die or be lost. Rehearse a text message the child can send.

Powering Your Radios

A radio with a dead battery is a brick. This section is general good practice; this guide did not source battery figures, so test your own gear.

  • Buy radios that use common batteries (AA or AAA), or carry a spare pack.
  • Keep radios off until needed. Set a schedule instead: for example, listen for five minutes at the top of each hour.
  • Rechargeable packs need a way to recharge. Common options are a car charger, a solar panel, a power bank, or a hand crank.
  • Store spare batteries dry, in a bag, with the contacts covered so they cannot short.
  • Test everything each season, and rotate old batteries.
  • Choose a weather radio with battery backup.

When There Is No Electronics

Plan for the day every battery fails. For signals made with mirrors, fires, ground symbols, and whistles, see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication.

Runners

  1. Send at least two people when you can. The US Forest Service advises this for sending help for a lost or hurt person.
  2. Give them a written message in the who, where, what, needs format.
  3. Mark the route you will use and the time you expect them back.
  4. Agree on what to do if a runner does not return.

Notes and boards

  • Leave a dated note at camp, a car, or a trail junction: who you are, where you are going, and when you will return.
  • Use a signal board or marked stakes at a shelter to show "in use," "gone to ____," or "need help." Agree on the meanings first.
  • Write in dark ink on light paper, in a waterproof bag.

Common Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A bigger radio always reaches farther. Fact: Terrain and antenna height usually matter more than power.

  • Buying a radio and never testing it with your group.
  • Skipping the group's channel and check-in schedule.
  • Assuming someone is listening on your family radio channel. Arrange who listens and when.
  • Sending messages without writing them down.
  • Keeping the plan only on a phone.
