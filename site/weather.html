Weather

Weather decides whether a bad night in the field is uncomfortable or deadly. You do not need instruments to read it. Clouds, wind, and a falling or rising barometer tell you what is coming hours ahead. This chapter explains how weather works in plain terms, how to read the sky, and which old sayings hold up and which are myths.

How Weather Works, in Plain Terms

Air has weight. The weight pressing down on you is air pressure. It differs from place to place, and the differences drive the wind. Air flows from higher pressure toward lower pressure, crossing the pressure lines at an angle (NOAA JetStream).

A large body of air with fairly even temperature and moisture is an air mass. A front is the edge where two air masses meet. "Warm" and "cold" are relative. The air behind a "cold" front may still feel mild (NOAA JetStream).

Most weather you care about happens along those edges. Warm air is lighter, so it is pushed up where the two meet. Rising air cools, its moisture condenses, and clouds form. With enough moisture and lift you get rain or snow. If the air is also unstable, you can get thunderstorms.

In general, falling pressure points to an approaching storm or front. Rising pressure goes with clearing and fair weather. Rapid changes go with strong storms (University of Wisconsin, Atmospheric Sciences).

Tip

Weather is a trend, not a snapshot. Note the sky, wind direction, wind strength, and pressure now, then again in 1 to 3 hours. The change is the forecast.

Reading Clouds

NOAA's JetStream guide sorts clouds into ten basic types by height and shape. Low clouds sit below about 6,500 feet (2 km). Middle clouds have bases from about 6,500 to 20,000 feet (2 to 6 km). High clouds are above about 20,000 feet (6 km). Heights come from National Weather Service charts. In names, cumulus means heaped, stratus means layered, and nimbus means rain-bearing.

What each cloud tends to signal

CloudWhat it looks likeWhat it tends to mean

NOAA files nimbostratus as a middle cloud. Its base keeps lowering while rain or snow falls, so it is often called a low cloud, and one National Weather Service office lists it that way. It can also reach up into the high band. Do not worry about the box. Know what it brings.

Fronts: The Two Sequences to Know

Much of the bad weather in the mid-latitudes comes with one of two fronts. They look different, and so does the warning you get.

Cold front: a short, hard change

A cold front is where cold air pushes under warmer air and shoves it upward. Friction slows the cold air near the ground, so the front is steep and lifts warm air fast. Cold fronts usually move faster than warm fronts, so you get less warning.

  1. Ahead of the front: warm, humid air. Winds are often from the south or southwest. Pressure is falling. Clouds build, and thunderstorms may form in a line ahead of the front.
  2. At the front: a band of heavy showers or thunderstorms, gusts, and a quick wind shift. Pressure bottoms out and begins to rise.
  3. Behind the front: winds turn west or northwest, temperature drops, pressure rises sharply, and the sky clears. The wind may stay gusty for hours.

These wind directions are typical for North America and the Northern Hemisphere mid-latitudes (NOAA's surface-map guide; Ohio University and LibreTexts university texts). Local terrain can change them.

Warm front: a long, slow warning

At a warm front, warm air slides up and over cooler air that is slow to give way. The slope is gentle, so clouds spread across a huge area. A warm front moves more slowly than a cold front.

  1. Far ahead (often a day or more): cirrus wisps appear. Pressure begins a slow fall.
  2. Next: a thin milky veil (cirrostratus). A halo may ring the sun or moon.
  3. Then: the sky turns gray (altostratus) and the sun looks dim. Clouds lower and thicken.
  4. At the front's arrival: nimbostratus and several hours of light to moderate steady rain or snow. Low scud and fog can form in the cool air.
  5. After the front passes: winds shift from the east or southeast to the south or southwest. Skies partly clear and the air warms.

A warm front can bring freezing rain or ice pellets if the air at ground level is below freezing.

Wind

Estimating wind speed: the Beaufort scale

Admiral Francis Beaufort's scale rates wind from Force 0 (calm) to Force 12 (hurricane). It works without any instrument. You judge the wind by what it does to the land or the sea. The Met Office notes that the scale was built on sea conditions. The land signs below follow the National Weather Service's version.

At Force 7, the National Weather Service says whole trees move and walking into the wind is hard. At Force 8, twigs break off and progress is slow. Do not travel across open ground, ridges, or water in a gale unless you must.

Winds that follow the sun

On calm days, the sun's heat makes local winds that flip between day and night (National Wildfire Coordinating Group).

  • Sea breeze (day): starts around mid-morning, grows through the day, peaks near the hottest time, and fades near sunset. On the Pacific coast it is often 10 to 15 mph (16 to 24 km/h) and can reach 30 to 40 miles (50 to 65 km) inland.
  • Land breeze (night): begins two to three hours after sunset and ends soon after sunrise. It is weaker, often 3 to 5 mph (5 to 8 km/h). A strong onshore wind can block it.
  • Upslope and upvalley winds (day): air climbs sunlit slopes within minutes of the sun hitting them. The wind up a whole valley starts later, mid- to late morning, and peaks in early afternoon.
  • Downslope and downvalley winds (night): cool air drains down slopes once they fall into shadow and flows down the valley floor after dark. It is shallow, slow, and smooth.

Barometric Pressure: Your Early Warning

A barometer measures air pressure. Pressure drops as a low or front approaches and rises as they leave. Two things matter: the level and the rate of change. The rate is the stronger clue.

Pressure comes in hectopascals (hPa, the same as millibars) or inches of mercury (inHg). One hPa is about 0.03 inHg. As a rough guide, sea-level pressure above about 30.20 inHg (1,023 hPa) tends to go with clear, calm weather, and below about 29.80 inHg (1,009 hPa) with unsettled, wet weather. These are loose rules from secondary sources, not hard cutoffs.

Rates of change

The Met Office's marine forecast glossary uses fixed words for the pressure change over the last three hours. The same bands apply to rising pressure ("rising slowly", "rising quickly", and so on). The last column is a rule of thumb from a secondary forecasting reference, not the Met Office. Sources do not agree exactly on cutoffs, so treat the timing as rough.

Change in 3 hoursMet Office wordingWhat it usually means
Falls 0.1 to 1.5 hPa (under 0.05 inHg)"Falling slowly"Weather is slowly getting worse over 12 to 24 hours. Clouds and wind build.
Falls 1.6 to 3.5 hPa (0.05 to 0.10 inHg)"Falling" (no extra word)A system is on its way. Expect worse weather, likely within 12 to 24 hours. Keep watching the trend.
Falls 3.6 to 6.0 hPa (0.11 to 0.18 inHg)"Falling quickly"An active storm is near. Rain or strong wind may arrive within 6 to 12 hours.
Falls over 6.0 hPa (over 0.18 inHg)"Falling very rapidly"An intense system. Heavy wind and rain may come within hours. Take cover and secure gear now.
Little or no change (under 0.1 hPa)No term (below "slowly")Present conditions likely last 12 hours or more.
Rises (same bands as falls)"Rising slowly" to "rising very rapidly"Clearing and calming. A fast rise often follows a cold front, and the wind can stay gusty for a while.

Yachting World adds that large pressure changes bring strong wind whether pressure is falling or rising. A rising barometer after a front does not mean the wind drops right away.

Altimeter caveat

A watch or GPS "altimeter" is a barometer wearing a different scale. It cannot tell a change in height from a change in weather. If you sit in camp and your altimeter slowly climbs, pressure is falling, and weather may be worsening. If you are walking, the two effects mix together. To use it as a barometer, stay at one spot for a few hours and watch the number. If you travel, reset it whenever you reach a place where you know the true elevation, such as a mapped summit or pass. This is this guide's own reasoning from how pressure falls with height.

Pressure shows where weather is heading, not how strong it will be. Check it against the clouds and wind.

Thunderstorms and Lightning

A thunderstorm is built from cells. According to the National Weather Service Spotter Guide, a single cell lasts about 30 to 60 minutes and passes through three stages.

  1. Developing. A rising column of air builds a cumulus cloud into a tall tower. Little rain falls.
  2. Mature. Rain falls and drags a downdraft with it. Rain-cooled air spreads along the ground as a gust front. The cloud can reach 40,000 to 60,000 feet (12 to 18 km). This stage has the highest chance of hail, heavy rain, frequent lightning, strong winds, and tornadoes.
  3. Dissipating. Heavy rain overwhelms the updraft and the downdraft takes over. The gust front runs ahead and cuts off the storm's warm, moist air. Light rain and weak wind linger, then only an anvil remains.

How storms show their hand

Summer storms over land usually build in the early afternoon, so the National Park Service advises starting early. Warning signs are cumulus growing taller and darker, an anvil forming, and rows of altocumulus. If clouds are going up fast, plan for a storm.

Lightning

For lightning first aid, see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies. Here are the weather facts.

  • Count the seconds between a flash and its thunder, then divide by 5 to get the distance in miles (divide by 3 for kilometers). Fifteen seconds is about 3 miles (5 km).
  • If you can hear thunder, you are within striking range. NOAA says lightning can hit more than 10 miles (16 km) from the rain.
  • Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder or lightning before you go back out. Rain easing off does not mean it is safe.
  • The safe places are a large enclosed building with plumbing and wiring, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows up. Tents, small sheds, porches, and open vehicles are not safe.

Do not

  • Do not stay on a ridge, hilltop, open field, or boulder field. Get down and off the high ground early.
  • Do not shelter under a lone tree, or under a cliff or rock overhang.
  • Do not lie flat on the ground.
  • Do not stay in or near water, or near wire fences, power lines, or windmills.
  • Do not group tightly. The Park Service advises spreading out so that, at most, one person is hit and the others can help.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If it is not raining, lightning cannot hit you. Fact: Most people struck each year are in rain-free areas, miles from the storm. Rubber soles and tires do not protect you.

Fog

Fog is a stratus cloud resting on the ground. It is likely when air temperature and dew point are within about 5°F (3°C) of each other (National Weather Service). Fog hides landmarks and trails, so it is a real navigation hazard. If you are caught in it, slow down, stay on ground you know, or stop and wait for it to lift (see Part 9 › Maps & Compass and Part 9 › Travel & Movement).

Warning

Dense fog can cut visibility badly. Steam fog often drops it to about 1,000 feet (300 m) or less. Do not cross water or steep ground when you cannot see the far side.

Reading the Sky Over a Day

On a normal fair day over land, the sky follows a pattern. Learn it so you can spot what breaks it.

  1. Dawn. Calm and clear after a cold night can mean radiation fog in low ground. Note the wind and the sky.
  2. Mid-morning. The sun heats the ground and small cumulus clouds form. NOAA notes that cumulus forms on clear-sky days over land. Slope and valley winds begin.
  3. Afternoon. Fair-weather cumulus stay small and flat. If they keep growing taller and darker, a thunderstorm may follow. Get off high ground and open terrain. Sea breezes peak near the hottest time of day.
  4. Evening. Fair-weather cumulus usually fade as heating stops. Check the sky to the west. In much of the mid-latitudes, weather mostly arrives from the west.
  5. Night. Land breezes and downslope winds flow. A ring around the moon means high cirrus or cirrostratus is overhead.

If the pattern breaks, take notice. Clouds that thicken and lower, wind that turns and strengthens, or a barometer that keeps falling all suggest a front is coming. For floods, tornadoes, and hurricanes, see Part 9 › Natural Disasters. Plan shelter early (see Part 2 › Shelter).

Weather Lore: What Works and What Doesn't

People forecast long before instruments existed. Some sayings tie to real physics. Others are plain folklore. A good test: does the saying link to something that changes before weather arrives, and does it hold where you live?

SayingVerdictWhy
A ring around the moon (or sun) means rain or snow is coming.WorksThe ring is a halo. Light bends through six-sided ice crystals in thin, high cirrus or cirrostratus. The University of Alaska Geophysical Institute says such cloud normally comes a day or two ahead of a front.
Mares' tails (wispy cirrus) mean a change is coming.WorksThe National Weather Service says cirrus can signal an approaching warm front. In this guide's judgment, it is most useful when the wisps thicken and lower over several hours. Cirrus that stays high and unchanged means little.
Red sky at night, shepherd's delight. Red sky in morning, shepherd's warning.PartlyThe Met Office and the Library of Congress both say it holds, within limits, where weather mostly arrives from the west, as in the mid-latitudes. A red sky at sunset points to high pressure moving in from the west, so the next day is usually dry. A red sky at sunrise points to high pressure that has already passed to the east, and a wet, windy low often follows. The reasoning breaks down where weather does not mainly arrive from the west. Treat it as a tendency, not a promise.
You can smell rain coming.PartlyThe smell is real. Rain on dry ground gives off a scent called petrichor, and a storm can carry ozone. In this guide's judgment, it is a late warning. Rain is already close.
Pine cones close up when rain is near.PartlyThe Met Office confirms that cone scales open in dry air and close when it is damp. The cone tracks humidity, not rain. A weather news site calls the saying partly true but not something to count on.
Birds flying low means rain.PartlySwallows hunt flying insects. Before wet weather, insects are said to stay lower and the birds follow. A secondary source calls it a sign, not a guarantee. Wind, cool mornings, or feeding over water can also bring birds low.
Cows lie down before rain.MythThe Met Office says there is no scientific backing. Cows lie down to rest. Its survey found 61 percent of UK adults believed it.
The higher the pine cones, the harsher the winter.MythCone height depends on the tree species and how it spreads its seed. It has nothing to do with the coming winter.

NOAA Weather Radio

In the United States, NOAA Weather Radio carries National Weather Service warnings, watches, and forecasts around the clock.

  • It uses seven frequencies, from 162.400 to 162.550 MHz, in steps of 0.025 MHz. Make sure your receiver can tune all seven. Some older sets pick up only three.
  • Range is about 40 miles (65 km) from the transmitter. Terrain, the receiver, and the antenna change that. Check coverage before you go.
  • Look for SAME, which alerts you only for chosen counties. Get your code at weather.gov/nwr or by calling 1-888-697-7263. Buy a set with battery backup.

For other radios and calling for help, see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication. Compare any forecast with the sky. If they disagree, plan for the worse outcome.
