Time & Seasons

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026

You can keep a surprisingly good sense of time without a clock. The sun, the stars, the moon, and the sea all run on schedules you can learn. This chapter shows how to guess how much daylight is left, how long days and twilight last at different latitudes, how to read the moon, how to keep a calendar with a knife and a stick, and how to predict tides so a beach does not trap you.

What You Can Tell, and How Well

Every method here is a rough guess, not a clock. The hand method and the Big Dipper clock are good to roughly half an hour at best. A shadow stick finds solar noon well, but clock noon is usually a different time. Moon phases and a notched stick keep the day count, and a tide table beats memory every time. Use two methods and compare them.

For direction finding, see Part 9 › Navigation Without Instruments.

How Much Daylight Is Left? The Hand Method

Knowing how long until dark helps you decide when to stop and build camp (see Part 2 › Shelter and Part 2 › Fire). The hand method needs no gear.

  1. Stretch your arm straight out in front of you.
  2. Turn your hand sideways and keep your fingers level and together.
  3. Put the top edge of your top finger just under the sun's lower edge. Stack fingers downward until the bottom finger touches the horizon.
  4. Count the fingers. A common rule gives each finger about 15 minutes of daylight left.

The honest caveat: latitude and season

Two outdoor-skills writers warn that the rule shifts with latitude. Near the poles the sun sinks more slowly, and near the equator it sets faster than the rule suggests. Neither gives numbers, so this guide worked out how fast the sun sinks at sunset from NOAA's published sun equations.

LatitudeMinutes for the sun to sink 1° at sunset, around an equinoxSame, around the summer or winter solstice
0° (equator)about 4.0about 4.4
20°about 4.3about 4.7
40°about 5.2about 6.1
50°about 6.2about 7.9
60°about 8.0about 13

These figures ignore terrain and the bending of light by the air, but they show the trend. At 50° near a solstice the sun takes about twice as long to sink a degree as at the equator. At 60° it takes about three times as long. The same finger stack means more minutes the farther you are from the equator.

Here is why that matters. Astronomy references put three middle fingers at arm's length at about 5°, so one finger covers roughly 1.5° to 2° (timeanddate.com, secondary source). Combined with the table, that is only about 7 minutes per finger at the equator and about 9 minutes at 40° near an equinox. It reaches 15 minutes or more only near 50° in summer or farther toward the poles. At most latitudes, the 15-minute rule overestimates the daylight you have left. Plan on less.

To calibrate, watch one sunset with a watch. Time how long the sun takes to sink one finger-width, and use your own number from then on. When hills or trees block the real horizon, count to the skyline and add a margin.

Warning

Do not stare at the sun, even a low one. It can damage your eyes. Glance for an instant only. The hand method counts time to sunset, not to dark (see Twilight below).

Shadow Sticks, Sundials, and Clock Time

Solar noon is not clock noon

Solar noon is the moment the sun is highest and your shadow is shortest. Outside the tropics it points due north in the northern hemisphere and due south in the southern. Find it with a stick (see Part 9 › Navigation Without Instruments). Solar noon is the shortest shadow, or halfway between the two times the shadow tip touched the arc in the equal-shadow method.

A sundial shows apparent solar time, based on where the sun really is. Clocks show an even, averaged mean solar time. The Royal Museums Greenwich says the two can differ by up to about 15 minutes. Three things separate solar noon from 12:00 on your watch.

  1. Longitude within your time zone. The earth turns 15° each hour, which is 1° every 4 minutes. If you are west of the middle of your time zone, the sun reaches its high point later than clock noon. If you are east, earlier. Each degree moves it about 4 minutes.
  2. The equation of time. Earth's oval orbit and tilted axis make the sun run a little fast or slow through the year (table below).
  3. Daylight saving time. If your clock is moved forward one hour, add one hour to the result.
DateSun compared with clockSize
Mid-FebruarySun is slow. Solar noon comes late.About 14 minutes
Early NovemberSun is fast. Solar noon comes early.About 16 minutes

On the Greenwich meridian, solar noon falls at 12:00 GMT on only about four days a year (around April 15, June 14, September 1, and December 25).

To estimate when solar noon falls on your watch, start at 12:00. Add 4 minutes for each degree you are west of your zone's central meridian (subtract if east). Subtract the equation of time when the sun is fast, or add it when the sun is slow. Add one hour on daylight saving time. This guide's example: a place 2.3° west of its zone's meridian has solar noon near 11:53 in early November (12:00, plus 9 for longitude, minus 16 for the fast sun) and near 12:23 in mid-February.

What a shadow stick can and cannot do

An upright stick is good at one job: finding solar noon. If you also know when the sun rose, double the time from sunrise to noon to guess when it will set. This guide found no source for an hour-by-hour rule using an upright stick, so do not trust one unless you have tested it against a clock. Time zone borders are political and wander, so near the edge of a wide zone expect errors of 10 minutes or more.

Telling Time by the Stars: The Big Dipper Clock

At night in the northern hemisphere the whole sky turns around Polaris. Two stars at the end of the Big Dipper's bowl, Dubhe and Merak, always point toward Polaris, so the line through them works like the hour hand of a clock. (See Part 9 › Navigation Without Instruments for finding Polaris.) Old navigators carved a wooden tool called a nocturnal for this job. The Royal Museums Greenwich says it gave local time to within about 15 minutes.

  1. Find Polaris and the Big Dipper's two pointer stars, Dubhe and Merak.
  2. Picture a 24-hour clock face with Polaris at its center. Midnight (24) is straight up. Noon (12) is straight down.
  3. Imagine the line from Polaris through the two pointer stars. This is your hour hand.
  4. Read the hand's angle from the midnight line. Count 15° for each hour. The hand moves counterclockwise.
  5. On March 6, the reading is the clock time. A hand 30° to the right of straight up means about 10 p.m.
  6. On any other date, subtract 2 hours for each month since March 6. If the answer is negative, add 24.
  7. If daylight saving time is in effect, add 1 hour.

The monthly correction matches how the sky behaves. A star rises about 4 minutes earlier each night, which adds up to about 2 hours a month. So on April 6 subtract 2 hours, on May 6 subtract 4, and so on. By December 6 you subtract 18, and by February 6, 22.

Example: on December 6 the hand points right, at 3 o'clock. That reads 18. Subtract 18 and you get 0, which is midnight.

Two sources give the Dipper clock an accuracy of about 30 minutes. They say it works best at about 40°N and farther north. Polaris cannot be seen from the southern hemisphere, so this method does not work there. Your position inside your time zone shifts the answer by a few minutes, just as it does for solar noon.

Daylight Length by Latitude and Season

Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5° relative to its orbit. On the solstices the sun reaches its most northern or southern position. On the equinoxes it crosses the equator, and day and night are nearly equal everywhere. The US Naval Observatory counts sunrise and sunset from the moment the sun's upper edge touches the horizon, which is why an equinox day runs a few minutes past 12 hours.

For survival planning, note that near the equator dark comes at a steady time all year, while at 50° the winter day is only about 8 hours. Travel, firewood, and shelter plans change with that. Also, the shortest day is not the day of the earliest sunset. A timekeeping site notes that the latest sunrise comes after the winter solstice and the earliest sunset comes before it. The equation of time causes this.

Twilight and Usable Light

The sky does not go dark at sunset. The US Naval Observatory defines three stages by how far the center of the sun is below the horizon.

StageSun's center below horizonWhat you can do
Civil twilight0° to 6°The horizon and nearby objects are clear. The brightest stars and planets appear. Outdoor chores are possible, often without a light.
Nautical twilight6° to 12°The horizon is still visible, even on a moonless night. You see outlines, but detailed work needs light.
Astronomical twilight12° to 18°The sky looks dark to most people. The horizon is gone. After 18° the sky is as dark as it gets.

How long does each stage last? The numbers below are this guide's calculation from NOAA's sun equations: minutes after sunset until the sun's center reaches each depth.

40° latitude, summer solsticeabout 33 minabout 75 minabout 123 min
50° latitude, summer solsticeabout 45 minabout 110 minnever (sky never fully dark)

Do not count on twilight to finish camp. At 40° near an equinox you have about 30 minutes of good light after sunset. Finish water, fire, and shelter chores before the sun goes down (see Part 2 › Fire and Part 2 › Shelter). Cloud and tree cover make dusk darker than the numbers suggest.

The Moon's Phases and When It Rises

The sun always lights half of the moon. As the moon circles the earth, you see more or less of that lit half. The cycle from one new moon to the next takes about 29.5 days. The moon orbits the earth in about 27.3 days, and needs a couple of extra days to line up with the sun again.

Why the moon rises later each day

NOAA says a lunar day is about 24 hours 50 minutes, 50 minutes longer than a solar day. The moon orbits in the same direction the earth turns, so the earth must turn a bit extra to catch up. That is why moonrise, moonset, and the tides all slip about 50 minutes later each day on average. The real daily delay varies with latitude and season. Astronomy magazine and the Old Farmer's Almanac (secondary sources) say that at mid-northern latitudes it can shrink to about 20 to 25 minutes around the September full moon (the "Harvest Moon") and grow to about 70 minutes around the March full moon. It shrinks even more farther north. Treat 50 minutes as an average only.

Reading the phase

  • Waxing means growing. The moon is up in the evening. In the northern hemisphere the lit side is on the right.
  • Waning means shrinking. The moon rises late and is up in the morning. In the northern hemisphere the lit side is on the left.
  • The full moon rises near sunset and sets near sunrise, so it lights the whole night.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: The earth's shadow makes the moon's phases. Fact: NASA says phases come from the angle at which you see the moon's lit half.

Keeping a Calendar Without Devices

After days of stress, cold, or fever, people lose track of the date. A day count helps you plan rations, medicine, and rescue windows, and tells you when the moon and tides will repeat. A notched stick is the oldest method. This guide describes it as a practical technique, not one from a published source.

  1. Pick a straight, dry stick about as long as your forearm. Choose one daily moment you will never miss, such as first light.
  2. Cut one small notch on the stick at that moment each day. Never "catch up" later from memory.
  3. Cut a longer notch every seventh day to mark weeks.
  4. Cut a ring around the stick at each new moon and each full moon you see.

The moon corrects your count every two weeks. If your notches say day 12 or day 18 on a full-moon night, you missed or doubled a day. Remember that twelve moon cycles come to about 354 days, so a moon-only calendar drifts about 11 days a year behind the seasons (this guide's arithmetic from NASA's cycle length). The solstices are the yearly anchors: the sun is lowest at noon, and your noon shadow longest, near the winter solstice.

Tides

What causes them

Tides come from the pull of the moon and the sun on the oceans. Most coasts see two high tides and two low tides each lunar day. NOAA gives the timing: about 12 hours 25 minutes from one high tide to the next, and about 6 hours 12 minutes from a high to the next low. (One NOAA page gives 6 hours 12.5 minutes.) Each day, tide times slide about 50 minutes later, just as moonrise does.

NOAA's predictions come from the motions of the sun and moon. They do not directly include weather, so real water levels can differ from the table, especially in storms.

Spring and neap tides

At new and full moon, the sun, earth, and moon line up. Their pulls add, so highs are higher and lows are lower. These are spring tides. At first and third quarter, the pulls act at right angles and partly cancel, so highs are lower and lows are higher. These are neap tides. The cycle repeats about every two weeks, so neap tides come about a week after spring tides. NOAA notes that ranges are also larger when the moon is closest to the earth (perigee) and smaller when it is farthest. The largest tides do not always fall on the exact day of the new or full moon, so check the table rather than the calendar.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Spring tides happen in spring. Fact: The name refers to the water welling up. They happen twice every month, at new and full moon, in every season.

Three tide patterns

PatternWhat happens each dayWhere
SemidiurnalTwo highs and two lows, about equal in height, about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.Most Atlantic coasts of the US
Mixed (semidiurnal)Two highs and two lows, but the heights differ a lot.Most Pacific coasts of North America
DiurnalOne high and one low each lunar day (about 24 hours 50 minutes from high to high).Parts of the Gulf of Mexico

The pattern does not tell you how big the tide is. One tutorial lists a spring range of about 36 feet (11 m) at Anchorage, Alaska, and a neap range of only about 5 inches (13 cm) on a Florida beach.

The rule of twelfths

The rule of twelfths estimates the water level between a known low and high. It treats the 6 hours between them as six one-hour steps. In each step the water moves a fixed share of the total range.

Hour1st2nd3rd4th5th6th
Share of range moved1/122/123/123/122/121/12
Total so far1/123/126/129/1211/1212/12

To use it, find the range (high minus low). Three hours after low tide, half the range is covered. Two hours after, 3/12 is covered. On a coast with one tide a day, make each step about two hours.

Warning

The rule of twelfths is a rough guide only. It assumes a smooth, regular rise and fall. It fails where a tide has a double high or double low water, and where local geography changes the pattern. Use a real tide table for any plan that matters.

Dangers on the shore

A rising tide cuts off routes around headlands, rocks, and points. A beach that was easy to walk at low tide may be underwater at high tide, with a cliff behind you. Rising tides also push waves farther up the beach and make sneaker waves worse. Sources differ on the details, so plan for the worst case. NOAA says a sneaker wave can follow 10 to 20 minutes of small waves and run more than 150 feet (45 m) up a beach; a National Weather Service brochure warns of up to 30 minutes of small waves and run-ups of over 100 yards (90 m). NOAA advises watching the water for about 15 minutes before you go near it. A wave with only a few inches of water can lift and roll a large drift log. If you are trapped or someone is swept in, call 911 (in the US). Do not go into the water after them.

  1. Before you walk any beach, look up the high tide time for that day. Know which way the tide is moving. A beach that was dry at this time yesterday may be underwater today, because tide times shift daily.
  2. Plan to be back at your exit well before the next high tide. Allow extra time for children or anyone slow.
  3. Note where you can reach high ground, and keep watching for it.
  4. Turn back early. The water rises fastest in the middle hours of the tide, so a beach that looks fine can flood quickly.
  5. Stay on established trails. Do not climb loose cliffs to escape. Falling rock and steep, loose slopes can kill.

Do not

  • Walk around a headland on a rising tide hoping to beat it.
  • Sit on or near large logs close to the water.
  • Turn your back on the surf, even on a calm day.
  • Camp where waves have recently reached (wet sand shows it).

Reading a tide table

NOAA publishes tide predictions for stations all along the US coast. A table lists the time and height of each high and low for each day. Heights are measured from a local reference level, commonly mean lower low water in the US. A small gap between a day's high and low points to neap tides, and a large gap points to spring tides. Small ports often use a nearby main station plus a posted time and height correction.

  1. Find the station closest to your beach, and note any listed correction.
  2. Read the high and low times for the date.
  3. Check whether the times are in standard time or daylight saving time. The pages this guide read did not say which, so look at the table's notes.
  4. Add a safety margin. The predictions are based on astronomy, and real water levels can differ in bad weather.

With no table, use the pattern. Note a high tide today. The next high is about 12 hours 25 minutes later, and tomorrow's is about 50 minutes later than today's. This works only where tides are regular. Local geography can bend the pattern, so confirm with a table or a local as soon as you can.
