Maps & Compass

A paper map and a baseplate compass need no battery and no signal. This chapter shows you how to read a topographic map and its contour lines, take and follow a bearing, correct for declination, find your position from landmarks, and read latitude, longitude, UTM, and MGRS grid references. Learn these skills at home. The field is a poor place to learn them for the first time.

Parts of a Topographic Map

A topographic map shows the shape of the land with contour lines, plus water, roads, trails, and woods. Most US hikers use the USGS 7.5-minute "quadrangle." The US Geological Survey (USGS) says the name comes from the four-sided area that latitude and longitude lines enclose. Since 1947, the standard quad covers 7.5 minutes of latitude by 7.5 minutes of longitude. Read the margins before you walk anywhere.

  • Title and date. Check that the map covers your ground and how old it is. Trails move and printed declination goes stale.
  • Scale. A ratio such as 1:24,000, plus a bar scale you can measure with.
  • Contour interval. The elevation gained from one contour line to the next. The Army map-reading manual says its maps put this note in the center of the lower margin.
  • Legend. The symbols for roads, trails, buildings, and other features.
  • Declination diagram. Shows the angles between true north, grid north, and magnetic north.
  • Grid ticks and datum. Tick marks on the edge let you read a grid reference. The datum is the reference system the coordinates use. Match your GPS to it before you compare numbers.
ColorUsually shows

BlueLakes, streams, and other water
GreenVegetation such as woods

Scale and Measuring Distance

The USGS says its 7.5-minute maps of the lower 48 states and Hawaii are 1:24,000, so one inch on the map is 2,000 feet on the ground. Alaska's 7.5-minute maps are 1:25,000. The other numbers in the table follow from that ratio.

On a 1:24,000 mapOn the ground
1 inch2,000 feet
2.64 inches1 mile (5,280 feet)
1.64 inches (4.2 cm)1 kilometer
1 centimeter240 meters

A Sierra Club guide says UTM grid lines on these maps are 1 kilometer apart, so the grid works as a ruler. For a straight line, use the ruler edge of your compass. For a winding trail, lay string along the route, straighten it, and measure it against the bar scale. A map shows flat distance, so climbs make the real walk longer. The Army manual also teaches pace counting: walk a measured 100-meter (328-foot) stretch a few times to learn your paces. Your count changes at night because your steps get shorter.

Reading Contour Lines

A contour line joins points at the same elevation. The USGS says closely spaced lines show steep slopes, and wide spacing or no lines shows level ground. Flat areas may use an interval of 10 feet or less, and mountains 100 feet or more.

  • Contour lines do not cross. The Army manual says on a cliff they can merge into one line.
  • Index contours are thick and labeled. The USGS specification draws one at every fifth line. A USGS-based guide says every fourth or fifth, so check your legend.
  • Supplemental lines are added between two contours to show small features the regular interval would miss.

Count the lines you cross and multiply by the interval to find your climb. Six lines on a 40-foot map is about 240 feet (73 m). To judge slope, divide the interval by the ground distance between two lines. On a 1:24,000 map, lines 0.1 inch apart are 200 feet apart, so a 40-foot interval means a 20 percent slope, about 11 degrees. (This worked example is plain arithmetic, not from a source.) The Army manual gives a similar rule: with a 10-meter interval, one line per 100 meters of ground is about a 10 percent slope.

LandformContour pattern
HillClosed rings, one inside another. The smallest is the top.
Ridge or spurU or V shapes whose closed end points away from higher ground.
Valley or drawU or V shapes whose closed end points toward higher ground. A stream usually runs down the middle.
SaddleAn hourglass shape. High ground on two opposite sides, low ground on the other two.
DepressionClosed rings with short ticks pointing toward the low spot. The Army manual says these are usually drawn only if at least one interval deep.
CliffLines pressed together or merged, with ticks pointing downhill.

The rule of V's

Where a contour crosses a stream, it bends into a V or U. Water runs down the valley, so the point of the V aims upstream, toward higher ground. On a ridge it is the opposite: the point aims downhill, away from higher ground. If you are unsure, read the index-line labels and see which way the numbers grow.

Tip

Study a local hill on a topo map, then walk it. Seeing the real slope beside its contour lines trains your eye faster than reading alone.

The Baseplate Compass

REI calls the needle-style baseplate compass the serious navigation tool. Keyring, zipper-pull, and watch compasses are not. A baseplate compass lets you measure and follow angles, and its clear plate lets you work right on the map.

The bezel (ring marked 0 to 360 degrees) and the orienting lines turn together. The needle floats in fluid, and its red end points to magnetic north.

REI warns that iron and steel, electric fields, and magnets can throw off the needle. Stay away from cars, running engines, metal posts, and magnetic clasps on gear. Do not carry the compass next to a cell phone. A compass built for one region can read badly far from home, so REI recommends a "global needle" for travel.

Bearings: Take, Follow, Reverse

A bearing is a direction measured in degrees clockwise from north. North is 0 (or 360), east is 90, south is 180, and west is 270. A bearing belongs to a place. The same number leads somewhere else from a different start.

Take a bearing from the map

  1. Lay one long edge of the baseplate along the line from where you are to where you want to go. The direction-of-travel arrow must point toward the goal.
  2. Turn the bezel until the orienting lines run parallel to the map's north-south lines (meridians, or lines drawn parallel to the map's side edge), with the bezel's N toward the top of the map.
  3. Read the number at the index line. That is your bearing, before any declination correction.

Follow a bearing

  1. Hold the compass flat, with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing away from you.
  2. Turn your whole body until the red end of the needle sits inside the orienting arrow.
  3. Pick a far object on that line, such as a rock or a dead tree. The Army manual says to choose the most distant feature you can identify.
  4. Walk to it without staring at the compass, then pick the next object.

Take a bearing in the field

Point the direction-of-travel arrow at a landmark you can also find on the map. Turn the bezel until the needle sits in the orienting arrow. Read the bearing at the index line. Take three readings and average them, as a Sierra Club guide suggests, and keep the compass level.

Back bearings

A back bearing points from your goal back to where you started. The Army manual's rule: if the bearing is 180 or less, add 180. If it is more, subtract 180. A bearing of 60 has a back bearing of 240. A bearing of 300 has a back bearing of 120.

Why small errors matter

REI says a one-degree error puts you about 100 feet off course per mile. The Sierra Club guide gives these figures for one degree of error.

Distance walkedSideways error from 1 degree
0.5 mile (0.8 km)about 46 feet (14 m)
1 mile (1.6 km)about 92 feet (28 m)
2 miles (3.2 km)about 185 feet (56 m)
3 miles (4.8 km)about 277 feet (84 m)

A forgotten declination of 10 degrees is ten times worse: about half a mile off after 3 miles.

Declination: Magnetic North and True North

Maps are drawn to true north. A compass needle points to magnetic north. NOAA defines declination as the angle between them, positive when magnetic north lies east of true north and negative when it lies west. Across the lower 48 states it runs from east declination in the Northwest to west declination in the Northeast. REI gives the extremes as about 20 degrees east in parts of Washington and 20 degrees west in parts of Maine. Values have shrunk in many places since older guides were written: a 2025 Mountaineers handout lists about 15 degrees east near Olympia, Washington. Always look up your own value.

Fixing it: adjust or do the math

The simplest fix is an adjustable compass. REI says you set the declination once and reset it only when you move to a new region. Brunton-style compasses adjust by hand at the center hub. Suunto-style compasses use a small key in a screw on the back.

With a compass you cannot adjust, do the math each time. The Mountaineers' navigation course writes it as MB = TB − δ and TB = MB + δ, where MB is magnetic bearing, TB is true bearing, and δ is declination (east positive, west negative).

You are goingEast declinationWest declination
Map to ground (true to magnetic)Subtract itAdd it
Ground to map (magnetic to true)Add itSubtract it

Example from the Mountaineers: with 15 degrees east, a magnetic bearing of 285 is a true bearing of 300. If an answer drops below 0, add 360. If it passes 360, subtract 360.

"East is least, west is best" and its traps

This rhyme matches the map-to-ground row of the table: in east declination, subtract (least). In west declination, add (best). The National Wildfire Coordinating Group (NWCG) teaches the rhyme this way, for going from a true (map) bearing to a magnetic one. It fails in these ways:

  • It works in one direction only. Going from compass to map, the steps flip.
  • It does not apply once you set the declination on the compass. Doing both counts it twice.
  • It does not help if the value is old or wrong.
  • It is about true north. If you steer by UTM grid lines, use the grid-magnetic angle in the map margin. The Sierra Club gives an example where grid north is 0°23' east of true north, so 12°25' east declination means setting the compass to 12°.

If you cannot remember, draw the declination diagram from the map margin and see which way the angle goes.

Declination changes

NOAA says declination drifts because of unpredictable flows in Earth's core. Its World Magnetic Model is normally updated every five years. NOAA issued an out-of-cycle update in 2019 because the Arctic field was changing quickly. The current model, WMM2025, was released in December 2024.

  • Denver's airport declination changed just over 2.5 degrees in 22 years (NOAA).
  • A Mountaineers handout lists about 15 degrees east near Olympia, Washington, shifting about 0.12 degrees west each year.
  • A Sierra Club guide gives about 5 arcminutes a year near Los Angeles. Rates vary by place and time.

The value printed on an older map can be several degrees off. Look up today's value on NOAA's declination calculator (NCEI geomagnetism pages). NOAA warns that declination is unreliable near the poles.

Orienting the Map and Matching Map to Ground

Orienting a map means turning it until north on the map points north on the ground. These steps follow REI, with declination set first.

  1. Set the compass declination (or plan to do the math).
  2. Place the compass on the map with the direction-of-travel arrow toward the top of the map. Turn the bezel so N lines up with that arrow.
  3. Slide the baseplate so a long edge lines up with the side edge of the map.
  4. Hold both together and turn your body until the needle sits in the orienting arrow.

Map to terrain: pick a feature on the map, predict where it should be and how it looks, then find it. Terrain to map: pick a prominent object, such as a peak or a lake, and find it on the oriented map. Big shapes like ridgelines and water are easier to confirm than small ones. Orienteering Ireland describes "thumbing" the map: keep your thumb on your position and fold the map so the ground ahead is on top.

Warning

Do not force the map to fit what you see. If a landmark does not match, stop and find out why. Check two or three features, not one.

Finding Your Position: Triangulation

You can find your position by taking bearings to landmarks you can identify on the map. Many guides call this triangulation. The Army manual calls it resection. Its related method, intersection, works the other way: you stand at known points and locate an unknown one.

  1. Orient the map. Pick two or three known landmarks in widely different directions.
  2. Point the direction-of-travel arrow at the first landmark, turn the bezel until the needle sits in the orienting arrow, and read the bearing.
  3. Correct for declination, unless the compass is already adjusted.
  4. On the map, put the baseplate edge on the landmark, with the arrow still pointing toward it. Turn the whole compass, not the bezel, until the orienting lines run north-south and the bezel's N points to map north. Draw a line along the edge, back toward your side.
  5. Repeat for the other landmarks. You are where the lines cross.

This follows REI. The Army manual converts to a back bearing and draws from the landmark toward you, which gives the same line. REI says landmarks should ideally be at least 60 degrees apart as seen from you. Three lines usually form a small triangle, and your position is in or near it. A big triangle means a mistake: recheck your declination, your reading, and the landmark you matched.

If you stand on a trail, stream, or ridge you can identify, one bearing is enough. REI says your position is where the line crosses your route.

Route Tools: Handrails, Catching Features, Attack Points, Aiming Off

Handrail
A line feature you follow, such as a trail, stream, fence, or wall. Orienteering Ireland recommends it for beginners.
Collecting feature
A feature you pass that confirms you are on route, such as a creek crossing.
Catching feature
A large feature beyond your goal that tells you that you went too far, such as a wide road or river.
Attack point
A clear feature near your goal. Go fast to it, then move carefully the last stretch. The Army manual says 500 meters (about 1,640 feet) or less is most desirable.
Aiming off
Aiming to one side of your goal on purpose, so you know which way to turn when you hit a line feature.

The Army manual suggests an offset of about 10 degrees. Walk it until you reach a line feature that crosses your route, then turn the right way. It adds that nobody follows a bearing perfectly, so plan a few steps past the feature instead of searching at random. If your goal sits on a creek, aim a little downstream and turn upstream at the water.

Latitude, Longitude, UTM, and MGRS

You may need to read your location from a map or GPS and give it to rescuers (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

Latitude and longitude

Latitude lines run east-west, from 0 degrees at the equator to 90 degrees north and south at the poles. Longitude lines run north-south, from 0 degrees at the prime meridian (Greenwich, England) to 180 degrees east and west. A degree has 60 minutes (') and a minute has 60 seconds ("). Say latitude first.

FormatExample (same spot)
Degrees, minutes, seconds32° 18' 23.1" N, 122° 36' 52.5" W
Degrees and decimal minutes32° 18.385' N, 122° 36.875' W
Decimal degrees32.30642° N, 122.61458° W

The MapTools example gives the first and last lines. The middle line is the same spot with seconds converted to minutes. In decimal form, a minus sign can mean south or west, but MapTools warns some programs flip the convention, so check. The USGS says that at 38°N, one degree of latitude is about 69 miles, a minute is about 1.15 miles, and a second is about 101 feet. Longitude lines close in toward the poles: at the same latitude, a degree is about 54.6 miles.

UTM and MGRS

The Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) grid covers the world in 60 zones. The US National Grid standard says each zone is 6 degrees of longitude wide and numbered eastward from 180 degrees. Latitude bands are 8 degrees tall. The lower 48 fall in zones 10 through 19 and bands R, S, T, and U. The Military Grid Reference System (MGRS) writes UTM positions as one code. MapTools says MGRS and the US National Grid use the same grid lines and differ only in how you write the position. A full reference has four parts:

  1. Zone and band, such as 10S.
  2. Two letters naming a 100-kilometer square, such as GJ.
  3. Easting digits: how far east you are in that square.
  4. Northing digits: how far north you are in that square.

MapTools' example is 10S GJ 06832 44683: 6,832 meters from the square's west edge and 44,683 meters from its south edge. The Army manual's rule for reading is "always read to the RIGHT and then UP." Find the grid line to the left of your point and read its label. Estimate the tenths toward the next line. Do the same for northing, using the line below you. Write easting first, then northing.

Digits set precision, and the total is always even. Do not add digits you do not have.

DigitsExampleLocates you to about
458 381 km (0.6 mile)
6584 387100 m (330 ft)
85843 387110 m (33 ft)
1058432 387141 m (3 ft)

The US National Grid standard gives the same steps. Its sample is the Washington Monument, 18SUJ23480647. MapTools says to cut extra digits, not round them, because a reference points to the southwest corner of its square. Map labels are often shortened, such as 706 for 706,000 meters. Use the large digits. Also check the datum: the Sierra Club guide notes maps on NAD27 and GPS units on WGS84, and the national grid standard stores values in NAD83. Set your GPS to match the map.

Practice Drills

These are suggestions, not tested courses. Use a safe place with known boundaries, carry water, and tell someone where you will be.

  1. Look up your declination and write it on your compass case and map.
  2. Find a hill, ridge, valley, saddle, and cliff on a map, then go see them.
  3. Walk a box: 100 paces on a bearing, turn 90 degrees, repeat for four legs. How far you miss your start is your error.
  4. Stand on a known spot, take bearings to two landmarks, plot them, and check the crossing.
  5. Read the six-digit reference of five map features aloud, then check each against a GPS.

Consider a navigation course from an outdoor school, a search-and-rescue group, or an orienteering club. An instructor can watch your technique.

Common Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: The needle points to the North Pole. Fact: It points to magnetic north. The angle between that and true north is declination, and it changes by place and over time.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: The declination printed on a map is always right. Fact: It was right when the map was made. Check NOAA for today's value.

Do not

  • Do not take a bearing next to a car, a metal post, or a phone.
  • Do not add declination by hand if you already set it on the compass.
  • Do not follow a bearing blindly into a cliff, river, or bog. Look at the ground.
  • Do not trust one landmark or one bearing. Cross-check with a second.
  • Do not rely on a phone or GPS alone. A map and compass still work when batteries and signals fail.

If you become lost, stop first and decide whether to stay or move (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication and Part 9 › Travel & Movement).
