When Help Isn't Coming

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 11, 2026

This chapter covers two bad situations. In the first, help exists but you cannot reach it, so you work on getting a message out. In the second, there is no help to reach at all, and you must give the care yourself and keep a patient alive for days. Read it with the Red Pages beside you. It does not repeat those cards. It explains what changes when the ambulance is not coming.

Training comes first

Everything here is wilderness first responder level at best. Reading cannot replace a hands-on Wilderness First Aid (WFA) or Wilderness First Responder (WFR) course. This chapter gives no prescription-drug doses. For over-the-counter medicines, use them as directed on the label.

The Big Picture: Which Situation Are You In?

Ask first: can anyone come, if they only knew? If yes, get word out and stay findable (Figure 1). If no, because the whole region is in trouble, you must care for people on your own. The second half of this chapter covers that.

How Emergency Calls Work Without Your Carrier

In the United States, the FCC's basic 911 rules say wireless carriers must send every 911 call to a 911 center, whether or not the caller subscribes to that carrier's service. That includes phones with no service contract at all. The FCC notes one catch: if such a call drops, the center has no number to call back, so you must redial yourself.

You may read that a phone will "use any carrier's tower" for 911. The rule is narrower than that. The federal rule (47 CFR 9.10) covers 911 calls made on a phone that uses a radio technology the serving carrier supports. It puts the duty on the carrier to pass the call on. It does not promise that your phone will find and use another company's tower, and the FCC consumer guide does not say either way. So try 911 even when your own carrier shows no service, but treat a connection through another network as a possible bonus, not a promise. If no tower your phone can use is in range, no call will connect.

Calls, texts, and what the dispatcher gets

  • Say your location first. Location data from the phone can be off by 50 to 300 meters (about 160 to 1,000 feet), and carriers are excused from the accuracy rules in some heavily forested areas.
  • Give your phone number in case the call drops.
  • If a call will not connect, try a text: to 911 where your area accepts texts, or to someone who can get help for you. The FCC and FEMA note that a text may go through when a call will not.
  • Text-to-911 is a fallback. The FCC says it works only where the local 911 center has chosen to accept texts, and that a voice call is still the most reliable method. Where texting is not offered, you should get an automatic reply telling you to use another way. If you get that reply, believe it.
  • If you must redial, wait about 10 seconds between tries. Rapid redialing adds to network overload.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A phone with no service plan, or no service from your own carrier, cannot call 911. Fact: FCC rules require carriers to deliver 911 calls from phones with no plan and from callers who are not their customers. Whether your phone can reach another network is not guaranteed. If no network is in range, the call fails, so keep trying from better spots.

Finding a Signal and Saving Battery

  1. Climb. A National Park Service (NPS) backcountry page says a ridge top may get you a signal where the valley does not.
  2. Get a clear view. Canyons, dense forest, and hills block both phone and satellite links.
  3. Move a little. The NPS page says even a foot or two can help a call connect. Once a call connects, or if rescuers say they will call you back, stay in that exact spot.
  4. Keep the phone alive. Dim the screen and close apps you do not need. The FCC and FEMA also suggest switching the phone off when it cannot find a usable signal, because it drains its battery searching. Turn it on only to try again.

Satellite Help and Beacons

If the phone fails, your next tools use satellites. Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication covers them in full. The short version follows.

  • Phone satellite SOS. Apple says iPhone 14 and later can text emergency services by satellite in the countries where Apple offers it. Apple passes your messages to a dispatcher or an emergency relay center. You must be outdoors with a clear view of the sky and horizon. Dense foliage, hills, and canyons may block it. A message takes 30 seconds or more in ideal conditions, and up to several minutes under trees. Some other phones and carriers offer similar services. Check yours before the trip.
  • Personal locator beacon (PLB). NOAA says a PLB works only on 406 MHz, is turned on by hand, and keeps transmitting for at least 24 hours. Some models add GPS, which narrows your position to about 100 meters (about 330 feet). U.S. beacons must be registered with NOAA, and registration is free.
  • Satellite messengers. They can send an SOS and text, but most need a paid subscription. Make sure yours is active before you go.

After you trigger a beacon, stay put with open sky above you.

Radios as Backup

Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication has the table of FRS, GMRS, CB channel 9, ham, and NOAA Weather Radio, with licensing notes. Handheld FRS and GMRS radios reach only short distances. Ham radio reaches farther, and volunteers run emergency nets. NOAA Weather Radio is listen-only, so it cannot carry your call.

Sending Someone for Help

If you cannot reach help, you may need to send a person to find it. This is risky for the messenger and for the patient left behind. Do it only after you have stabilized the patient and made them safe, warm, and sheltered.

Who goes

  • Send at least two people if you can. The NPS emergency plan says the rest stay with the patient.
  • If only you and the patient are there, the NPS plan says to stabilize them first, then go.
  • Leave the patient warm and sheltered, with water, food, and a whistle. Tell them when you will return.

The written note

Write it down. A stressed or exhausted messenger forgets details. NPS guidance lists what rescuers need: what happened and when, the patient's condition and care given, your location, how many people and what gear, the patient's name, age and who to notify, and the colors of tents, packs, and clothing. Figure 2 turns this into a card.

  1. Fill the card in before the messenger leaves. Read it back to check it.
  2. Write coordinates in the format the phone shows, and name the datum if you know it. Add a landmark in plain words too.
  3. Give the messenger a map with your spot marked (Part 9 › Maps & Compass).
  4. Keep a copy at the camp. Rescuers who find an empty camp will read it.

Stay Put or Move? Notes and Markers

The default advice from the NPS, the American Hiking Society, and the NPS emergency plan is the same: stay put. Moving makes you harder to find. The NPS plan says to move only if there is an immediate threat to life, and to keep a seriously injured person still. The American Hiking Society adds one more reason: you may move to get water, but return to the same spot afterward.

When self-evacuation may be right

This part is judgment, not a rule from one source. Consider leaving only if all of these are true. See also Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions.

  • The patient is stable enough to be left or carried.
  • You know the way out and can finish it before dark and bad weather.
  • Nobody knows where you are and nobody will look for days.
  • Staying costs more than leaving: no water, no shelter, or a patient who is getting worse.

If you move, leave a trail

A New Zealand outdoors magazine suggests bold stick arrows, cloth tied on trees, and a note wedged between rocks. Figure 3 shows all three. The pilot's code for "I went this way" is in Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication.

Do not

  • Do not follow creeks or rivers downstream. The NPS page calls them among the most dangerous routes in the Olympic mountains.
  • Do not leave a seriously hurt person alone without shelter, warmth, water, and food.

No 911 at All: You Are the Rescue

After a major disaster, roads, power, phones, and hospitals may fail together. Help may be days away or not coming. You are now the first responder and the hospital. Cut the problem into smaller jobs.

Mindset and priorities

  1. Make the scene safe. A second victim, such as a rescuer who falls, helps nobody.
  2. Fix the killers first: severe bleeding, no breathing, cold, and heat (Red Pages order). Then shelter, water, and food.
  3. Use the whole group. Give each person a job.
  4. Write things down. You will forget times, and the next person who cares for the patient needs them.

The idea of "prolonged field care"

Military medics use the term "prolonged field care" for care that must go on when a patient cannot reach a hospital soon. The idea has also reached wilderness medicine. It means more than a first aid class: you treat the problem, then you watch, re-check, feed, hydrate, clean, warm, turn, and re-treat for hours or days. This chapter stays at wilderness first responder level. It does not teach surgery or injected drugs.

What Changes for Each Red Pages Emergency

The Red Pages tell you what to do in the first minutes. Here is what is different when no one arrives to take over.

Severe bleeding

  • Use direct pressure, packing, and a tourniquet exactly as in the Red Pages. Record the time you put the tourniquet on.
  • Leave the tourniquet on. Loosening it or swapping it for a pressure dressing ("conversion") is a trained medical decision. These numbers are for trained military staff, not for you: a 2026 proposed change to the U.S. military's Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC) guidelines keeps a 2-hour window for non-medical personnel to reassess a tourniquet, and would limit conversion after 2 hours to medical personnel. An Australian Army education article says that from 2 to 6 hours, conversion depends on hospital-level support (continuous heart monitoring, repeated blood tests, and drugs to treat the effects) and is generally unsuitable in the field. Past 6 hours, it calls conversion unwarranted.
  • For you, "re-check" means look, not loosen. Is it still tight? Is the bleeding still stopped? If blood seeps past it, tighten it or add a second tourniquet above the first (Red Pages).
  • Be honest about the cost. The same Australian article links more than 6 hours of tourniquet time with failure to save the limb. A person can survive losing a limb. They cannot survive bleeding out.
  • What you can do: keep the patient warm, keep pressure on any wound the tourniquet did not control, and watch for shock (Red Pages). The Red Pages shock card says no food or drink in the first stage. If no help is coming for a day or more, a person who is fully awake, not vomiting, and able to swallow will need water, so give small sips. That last point is this guide's judgment; ask a trained provider by radio if you can reach one.
  • Learn to apply a tourniquet in a Stop the Bleed or WFA class. Conversion is an advanced skill. Do not try it from a book.

CPR

The honest truth: CPR with no AED, no advanced care, and no relief rescuers has hard limits, and it wears you out fast. There is no single universal rule for when to stop in a remote place. These are the points that sources support:

  • A Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) drowning summary says that, depending on the situation and resources, it might be reasonable to stop if the person was under water more than 30 minutes (water above 43°F, 6°C), or more than 90 minutes in colder water, or after 25 minutes of continuous CPR. It also says drowning victims need rescue breaths. Compression-only CPR is likely to help little, so it is only for bystanders not trained in rescue breathing.
  • For a person buried in snow, a WMS summary says CPR may be stopped if circulation has not returned after at least 30 minutes. The main exception is a victim who may be very cold: buried more than an hour with a clear airway. It also allows withholding CPR when the arrest was clearly caused by injury.
  • For hypothermia, the WMS says the usual signs of death (fixed, wide pupils, stiffness, blood settling in the body) can appear in people who can still be revived, and a slow pulse can be hard to feel through cold skin. Check for a pulse for a full minute. If you cannot do CPR nonstop, for example while moving the person, do 5 minutes of compressions with breaks of no more than 5 minutes. Cold is the main reason to keep going longer than usual.
  • Lightning is different. A strike stops the heart and breathing at the same time. The heart often restarts on its own, but breathing may not, and the heart can stop again if no one breathes for the person. So give rescue breaths as well as compressions. If several people are hit, use "reverse triage": help the ones who look dead first, because they are often revived. A lightning victim carries no electric charge and is safe to touch, but move to safety if the storm is still close.
  • This guide found no general wilderness rule for stopping CPR after major injury, apart from the avalanche point above.

Across these summaries, about 25 to 30 minutes of CPR with no sign of life is where stopping can become reasonable, except for cold victims. If CPR is not working and you are the only rescuer, stopping is a human decision. Make it with another person if you can, write down the time, and give yourself permission to grieve.

Hypothermia and frostbite

  • Follow the Red Pages for rewarming. Without evacuation, rewarming is the whole treatment.
  • For mild cases, the WMS guidance (through the American Academy of Family Physicians) says to insulate from the ground, add a vapor barrier, protect the head and neck, and put heat on the armpits, chest, and back. If the person is alert and can swallow safely, offer warm, high-calorie food or drink. Check cold skin under heat sources every 20 to 30 minutes to prevent burns.
  • For moderate or severe cases, handle the person very gently and keep them lying flat. Rough movement can trigger heart rhythm problems. Small chemical heat packs cannot rewarm the core, though they can help warm hands and feet.
  • Movement, warm-water immersion, or heating the limbs can cause "afterdrop," a further fall in core temperature. Warm the trunk first.
  • Frostbite: the Red Pages cover when to thaw. With no help coming, definitive care is far away, so thaw only if you can keep the part from freezing again, then protect it.

Heat stroke

The Red Pages cooling steps still apply, including how long to keep someone in cold water: about 15 minutes, or until they are no longer confused. The change is that nobody takes over afterward. Keep the person resting in the shade and watch for changes, as the Red Cross advises. If confusion comes back, start cooling again. Follow the Red Pages rule of no drinks while signs of heat stroke are present. Once the person is fully alert and can swallow, offer small sips. You have no other treatment to add, so keep checking.

Snakebite with no antivenom

Be honest with yourself: nothing in the field neutralizes venom. Only antivenom does. What you can do is limit harm and watch. The CDC's NIOSH page says:

  • Have the person lie or sit down, calm and still, with the bite in a neutral, comfortable position (never raised above the heart; see Red Pages).
  • Remove rings and watches before swelling starts.
  • Wash the bite with soap and water and cover it with a clean, dry dressing.
  • Mark the edge of the swelling on the skin and write the time next to it. Do this again at set times to see how fast the swelling moves.

Do not

  • No tourniquet, no cutting, no sucking, no ice, no soaking in water, and no electric shock.
  • No alcohol and no aspirin, ibuprofen, or naproxen.

This guide cannot give you odds for any area. Plan for the worst, and expect to need rest, fluids, and watching for several days.

Severe allergic reaction without epinephrine

The American Red Cross steps center on epinephrine, from an auto-injector or nasal spray. The same page says a second dose can be given if signs continue 5 to 10 minutes after the first and EMS has not arrived. None of the sources checked for this guide (Red Cross, MedlinePlus, NHS) offers any substitute for epinephrine, and this guide knows of no verified one. Do not count on allergy pills or home remedies to stop anaphylaxis. Without epinephrine, a person in anaphylaxis may die.

  • Have the person lie down; raising the legs is fine if they have no head, neck, back, or leg injury. If breathing is hard, let them sit up slowly. The NHS warns: do not let them stand or walk, even if they feel better.
  • Give nothing by mouth if they have trouble breathing (MedlinePlus). Stay with them and watch breathing. Be ready to start CPR.
  • Keep any used auto-injectors and write down the time of each dose.
  • Prevention is the plan. People with known severe allergies should carry more than one auto-injector, store them as the label says, and replace them on time. Ask your doctor about carrying extras.

Poisoning

Use the Red Pages steps. If phones and Poison Control work, call. If not, get the person away from the source (fresh air for fumes or smoke), keep the container, and monitor breathing and alertness. Prevention matters most: store chemicals labeled and away from food, and never run a generator, grill, or camp stove inside a shelter (see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning).

Dehydration and oral rehydration solution

Diarrhea and vomiting kill when the body loses more fluid than it takes in. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) replaces water and the salts needed to hold it. If you have no packets, you can mix the home version in Figure 6. The World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for the Eastern Mediterranean and the Rehydration Project give the same amounts. The Rehydration Project adds that the spoons must be level.

ItemAmount for 1 liter
Clean drinking water (boiled and cooled if unsure)1 liter (a bit over 1 quart; about 5 cups of 200 ml)
Sugar6 level teaspoons
Salt1/2 level teaspoon
  • Give small sips often, from a cup or spoon. WHO's guide for children says to give extra fluid after each loose stool: about 1.5 to 3.5 fl oz (50 to 100 ml) for children under 2, and about 3.5 to 7 fl oz (100 to 200 ml) for children 2 and older. The Rehydration Project gives slightly larger amounts and says adults and large children need at least 3 quarts or liters a day until they are well.
  • If the person vomits, WHO says to wait 10 minutes, then go on more slowly. Keep breastfeeding babies, and give ORS or clean water as well.
  • Keep the solution cool. If it is still needed after 24 hours, make a fresh batch.
  • Too much salt is harmful, and in extreme cases can cause convulsions. Too much sugar can worsen diarrhea. A little extra water is not harmful. Too little salt does no harm but works less well.

Limits of ORS

WHO counts a child as severely dehydrated with two or more of these signs: very drowsy or hard to wake, sunken eyes, unable to drink or drinking poorly, and pinched skin that goes back very slowly. The CDC notes that some severely dehydrated people need IV fluids. If you cannot get them, keep giving small sips whenever the person can swallow, keep them cool or warm as needed, and watch. See Part 7 › Illness in the Field.

Monitoring a Patient for Days

In prolonged care, the log is your most powerful tool. Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs teaches how to take a pulse, count breaths, and check skin and alertness. Here is how to use those numbers over time.

  • Start a log (Figure 4) as soon as you can. Check more often at first, and more often again if the person looks worse. Write the time of every medicine, meal, drink, and dressing change.
  • Learn the person's usual. Take a first set of readings early. Later readings mean more when compared with that baseline.
  • Watch the direction, not a single number (Figure 5). A pulse and breathing rate that keep rising, skin that turns cool and clammy, or growing confusion all point the wrong way.
  • Check the same way each time: same position, same hand, same count length.

Infection warning signs

Wounds and burns are the usual start. Check them at each dressing change (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection). Worry if you see spreading redness, more swelling or heat, pus, a bad smell, or pain that grows instead of fading.

Sepsis red flags

Sepsis is the body's extreme reaction to an infection. The CDC lists these signs: clammy or sweaty skin, confusion, extreme pain, fever or shivering or feeling very cold, a racing heart or weak pulse, and shortness of breath. Infections that start in the lungs, gut, skin, or urinary tract are common sources. The CDC says sepsis needs fast medical treatment, with antibiotics and support for blood flow. Without a hospital, you cannot give that. What you can do: clean wounds early, keep the person drinking and resting, keep records, and ask a clinician for advice if any radio can reach one.

Preventing the Second Disaster

After a disaster, diarrhea from dirty water and poor sanitation can kill more quietly than the disaster itself. Stop it before it starts.

  • Safe water first. Drink only treated water (Part 2 › Water). WHO's cholera advice says to drink boiled or clean water, use clean water for washing food and making ice, and keep washing of people, diapers, and clothes away from drinking-water sources.
  • Wash hands with soap and safe water after the toilet, before food, and after care of the sick (Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation).
  • Cook food thoroughly, keep it covered, and eat it hot (Part 5 › Food Safety).
  • Separate the sick. One person with diarrhea can infect a group. Give them their own bucket or latrine spot and have one caregiver.

Where to put the toilet

Humanitarian sanitation manuals put latrines about 50 to 100 feet (15 to 30 m) or more from water. WEDC's emergency sanitation manual says at least 30 m (100 feet) from surface water. SSWM, citing a WEDC field manual, says trench latrines belong 15 to 30 m (50 to 100 feet) from any groundwater source, such as a well or spring. Both say the bottom of a pit should be at least 5 feet (1.5 m) above the water table, and WEDC means the wet-season level. Leave No Trace asks for more distance: catholes 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) deep, at least 200 feet (about 60 m) from water, trails, and camp, and the same siting for trip latrines. Sources differ, so this guide uses the larger number, 200 feet (60 m), for every water source (Figure 7). Put the latrine downhill and downstream of water, and away from the kitchen. Toss soil on the waste after each use.

For a group staying long-term, dig a shared latrine and put a handwashing station near it. WEDC also says a shared latrine should be no more than about 165 feet (50 m) walk from shelters, so people will use it, even at night. Leave No Trace's 200 feet from camp is a backcountry rule for short trips.

Medicines When the Pharmacies Are Closed

The best time to prepare is now. Talk with your doctor and pharmacist, as they know your health history. This chapter gives no prescription doses.

  • Ask about extra supply of essential long-term medicines such as heart, blood pressure, asthma, diabetes, seizure, and thyroid drugs, and of emergency ones such as epinephrine auto-injectors.
  • Keep a written list of every medicine, the reason for it, and your allergies. Keep a copy with your first aid kit (Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).
  • Store them well. Keep medicines in the original container, cool and dry. Heat, humidity, and light shorten life.
  • Rotate stock and replace before the date.

What expiration dates mean

The FDA explains that an expiration date marks the time a drug is known to keep its strength, quality, and purity when stored as the label says. A drug that breaks down may be weaker than intended, and the FDA warns it may form toxic compounds. The FDA's shelf-life extension program tests only certain federal stockpiles kept in climate-controlled sites. The FDA notes that medicines held by the public are stored in many different ways, which makes such testing hard to apply to them. A Harvard Health Publishing article on a military study says most tested drugs were still good nearly three years past their dates. But only about 100 drugs were tested, and storage matters: cool and dry is better than hot and damp. Studies of liquid antibiotics, aspirin, nitroglycerin, and insulin found signs of decay, and EpiPens also held up poorly. The article also warns that a weakened heart rhythm drug can be dangerous. Treat the date as a reason to replace life-saving medicine, not to stretch it.

Do not

  • Do not stop a long-term medicine suddenly if you can avoid it. Some are dangerous to stop. Ask your doctor.

Building Community Medical Help

No family is strong enough alone. Build a neighborhood web before the crisis.

  • Make a skills list. Ask who is a nurse, medic, EMT, doctor, dentist, vet, pharmacist, or midwife, who has CPR, WFA, or Stop the Bleed training, and who has a first aid kit, a generator, or a vehicle. Ask who needs help: people on oxygen or dialysis, children, and the elderly.
  • Train together. Hold a CPR and first aid class on the block. Practice with a log and a help card.
  • Get a radio link. Ham operators run emergency nets that relay messages when phones fail. Join a local club (Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

When Someone Dies

Even with perfect care, some people will die.

The disease myth

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO, part of WHO) says that dead bodies from natural disasters do not cause epidemics. Most victims die from injury, drowning, or fire, and they were not carrying epidemic diseases such as cholera or typhoid when they died. PAHO says the public risk is small: water fouled by bodies can cause diarrhea, and routine water disinfection prevents it. Spraying bodies with disinfectant or lime does not help. People who handle bodies face a small risk from blood-borne and diarrheal infections and tuberculosis, which rubber gloves, boots, and handwashing reduce.

The picture changes if the person died of an infectious disease. With cholera, take more care and keep the body and its fluids away from food and water. With Ebola, WHO says burial customs that involve touching the body can spread the disease, and outbreaks call for trained "safe and dignified burial" teams. Do not wash or touch such a body without protection.

Handling and burial

  1. Care for the living first. Do not rush.
  2. Wear gloves and wash hands afterward. Wash clothes that touched the body.
  3. Identify the person. PAHO recommends recording descriptions and taking photos, which supports families and later legal needs.
  4. Keep the body cool and covered if you can. Take photos and notes early: UN News, reporting on a WHO and PAHO field manual, says that in hot climates a face may be too decomposed to recognize within 12 to 48 hours.
  5. If authorities exist, tell them. If they do not, bury the body or follow the family's customs, as respectfully and as safely as you can.
  6. Record the name, date and time, cause if known, and the exact location of the grave.

A humanitarian sanitation manual (WEDC) says graves should be at least 5 feet (1.5 m) deep, and that burial must not pollute ground or surface water. It gives no distance from water, so choose a spot downhill from and well away from wells, springs, and streams. PAHO says rapid mass burial is not justified on public health grounds, and its guidance stresses identifying the dead for their families. Where you can, bury people singly and mark the place.

Grief

PAHO notes that individual burial and traditional mourning help families and communities recover. Let people grieve and say the name. Tell children the truth in simple words. Do not leave alone anyone who talks of harming themselves.

Record what happened

Write a short note: who, when, where, what happened, what care you gave, and who was present. It helps families and later authorities.

Honest Limits, and Why Prevention Matters More

Some problems cannot be fixed without a hospital. You can slow some of them and comfort the patient, but not cure them.

ProblemWhat you can doWhat you cannot do
Internal bleeding (belly, chest, pelvis)Keep warm and still, treat for shock, watch the logFind or stop the bleeding
Appendicitis or a perforated gutRest, small sips if no vomiting, record changesOperate; infection can spread through the belly
Severe head injuryKeep head and neck still, protect the airway, watch alertnessRelieve pressure on the brain
SepsisClean wounds, fluids, rest, recordsGive effective antibiotics and organ support
Anaphylaxis with no epinephrinePosition, watch, CPR if neededReverse the reaction
Venomous snakebite with severe symptomsCalm, still, mark swelling, watchNeutralize venom

These rows are this guide's judgment, based on general medical knowledge rather than any one source.

Why prevention matters more

  • Every injury you avoid is one you do not have to treat. Wear shoes and gloves, use sharp tools slowly, and skip risks that would need a rescue.
  • Keep water, food, and waste clean.
  • Stay warm, hydrated, and rested. Tired people make mistakes.
  • Take a hands-on course now.

Remember

You may not save everyone. A calm, clean, well-recorded effort is still the best thing you can offer.
