Natural Disasters

Tornadoes, floods, fires, quakes, and storms can arrive with hours of warning or with none. This chapter covers twelve natural disasters in the same order each time: warning signs, how to prepare, what to do during, and what to do after. Most deaths in disasters come from a few repeat mistakes, such as driving into water, sheltering in the wrong room, or waiting for an official order that never comes. Learn the plan for your region before you need it.

Know your alert words

A watch means the danger is possible, so get ready. A warning means the danger is happening or about to happen, so act now. Turn on phone emergency alerts and keep a battery or hand-crank weather radio. Alerts are your first line of defense, and the natural signs in this chapter are your backup when alerts fail or time is short.

Tornado

Warning signs: Tornadoes can form within minutes, day or night, and night storms are the most dangerous because people are asleep. The sources used here do not list reliable sky signs, so rely on a watch or warning alert. If you feel in danger and have no alert, take shelter anyway.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Pick your safe place now: a storm shelter, a basement, or a windowless interior room on the lowest floor.
  • Get alerts two ways and test them.
  • Keep sturdy shoes, a flashlight, and a charged phone by your bed.
  • Practice the trip to shelter with every person, and plan for anyone who needs help.
  • If you live in a mobile home, choose a sturdy building to run to before any watch.
  • Go to shelter the moment a warning is issued.
  • Put as many walls as you can between you and the outside. Get under something sturdy and cover your head. A bike or motorcycle helmet helps.
  • Mobile homes, sheds, and tents are not safe. Leave them for a sturdy building.
  • In a car, drive to the nearest sturdy building. If you cannot, see the box below.
  • Stay away from windows and large open rooms such as gyms.
  • Check the forecast to be sure the threat has passed.
  • Watch for glass, nails, and downed power lines.
  • Use flashlights, not candles.
  • Let family know you are safe. Text instead of calling.
  • Follow local officials.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Open the windows to even out pressure and save the house. Fact: This does not help and wastes time you need to get to shelter. Also, do not shelter under an overpass.

Caught in a car

A car is not safe in a tornado. Your first choice is to drive to a sturdy building, or, if the tornado is far off and you can see it, drive out of its path at right angles to it. If you are caught and cannot reach shelter, official advice differs on the last resort. The NWS safety page gives two options: stay in the car, buckled, and get down below the windows with your head covered, or leave the car for ground clearly lower than the road, such as a ditch, and lie flat covering your head. The NOAA Storm Prediction Center says to keep your seatbelt on and crouch as low as you can, away from the windows. All sources agree: a building beats either choice, an overpass is not shelter and has proved deadly, and you must get low and protect your head.

Hurricane and Typhoon

These are the same kind of storm. Atlantic and eastern Pacific storms are called hurricanes. In the western Pacific they are called typhoons. The deadliest hazard is usually water, not wind.

Warning signs: You get days of notice. A hurricane watch means winds of 74 mph (119 km/h) or more are possible and is issued about 48 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected. A hurricane warning means those winds are expected, about 36 hours ahead. Storm surge watches and warnings follow the same timing. Impacts can reach hundreds of miles inland and well outside the forecast cone, and storms can strengthen quickly.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Learn whether you are in an evacuation or flood zone.
  • Write down your route, a destination outside the risk area, and pet plans. Not every shelter takes pets.
  • Stock for a power outage of a week or more: food, water, medicine, cash, radio, lights, chargers.
  • Buy flood insurance early. Standard home policies do not cover flood, and a new policy has a 30-day wait.
  • Cover windows, secure loose items, fuel the car, and charge devices once a watch is issued.
  • If ordered to evacuate, go at once and use official routes, not shortcuts.
  • If you stay, shelter in an interior room away from windows and doors.
  • If flooding is possible, move to higher ground before the water arrives.
  • If water traps you, stay inside and go to the highest floor.
  • Mobile homes and RVs are not safe shelter in strong wind.
  • Return only when officials say it is safe. Nearly half of hurricane deaths happen after the storm.
  • Stay far from downed lines and out of floodwater.
  • Run generators outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m) from doors, windows, and garages. Use a battery carbon monoxide alarm.
  • Use flashlights, not candles. Drink bottled or boiled water.
  • Food in a warm fridge can spoil in as little as 4 hours. Pace your cleanup, because straining your body can bring on a heart attack.

Do not

  • Do not stay in a surge evacuation zone because the wind looks mild.
  • Do not run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage.
  • Do not drive or walk through floodwater after the storm.

Flood and Flash Flood

Warning signs: A flood watch means flooding is possible. A flood warning means it is happening or about to. Flash floods can start fast, even when no rain is falling on you, because the rain may be miles upstream. Water level and speed can change in minutes. Heavy rain, and rising streams in your area, are reasons to be ready to move.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Learn your flood risk and pick a route on foot to higher ground.
  • Keep a kit ready: flashlight, batteries, cash, first aid, water.
  • Move valuables and furniture to upper floors during a watch. Unplug electronics.
  • In heavy rain, do not camp or park near streams, rivers, or creeks.
  • On a warning, go to high ground at once and leave if told to.
  • Never walk, swim, or drive through floodwater.
  • Never drive around a barricade.
  • If water rises around your car, get to higher ground at once if you can do it without stepping into moving water. Never try to walk or drive across moving water.
  • Do not touch electrical equipment while wet or standing in water.
  • Go home only when officials say it is safe.
  • Stay away from standing water. Buried or downed lines can electrify it.
  • Floodwater can carry sewage, chemicals, and sharp debris.
  • Ask your health department before using tap water. Throw out food that got wet or warm.
  • Dry the house within 24 to 48 hours if you can, or assume mold is growing. Wear boots, gloves, eye protection, and an N95 respirator to clean.

The NWS says more than half of all flood deaths are vehicle-related. Turn around and find another route, or wait.

Wildfire

Warning signs: Smoke or ash near you, nearby fires reported by local alerts, and weather updates. You may not get a formal evacuation order. Leave if officials advise it or if you feel threatened.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Create defensible space around buildings (Figure 5).
  • Map more than one exit route and practice it.
  • Keep a go-kit with about 3 days of supplies, plus a month of medicine and copies of key records.
  • Sign up for local alerts. Keep a backup charger and a battery radio.
  • Learn how to limit smoke exposure.
  • Watch the weather and nearby fires. Follow local authorities.
  • Be ready to leave at a moment's notice with your go-kit.
  • If you are trapped, call emergency services if you can and follow responders' directions. These sources give no tactics for being trapped, so do not rely on tricks from the internet.
  • Return only when officials say it is safe.
  • Stay away from hot ash, burned trees, smoldering debris, and downed lines. Mark ash pits that may hide embers.
  • Clean up in gloves, long clothes, goggles, and an N95 respirator. Keep children and people with lung or heart disease away from ash.
  • Check with the health department on tap water. Discard food exposed to heat, smoke, or chemicals.

Defensible space

California's CAL FIRE (Ready for Wildfire) describes three zones around a building. The distances are stated for California, and local rules can be stricter. Check with your fire department. The aim is the same everywhere: starve the fire and the flying embers of fuel next to your walls.

  • Zone 0, 0 to 5 feet (1.5 m): remove leaves, needles, mulch, and firewood. Use gravel or pavers. Keep gutters and roofs clear. Use non-combustible fences and sheds. Move vehicles, boats, and trash bins out. CAL FIRE notes that the rules for this zone were approved but not yet in effect when its page was checked.
  • Zone 1, 0 to 30 feet (9 m): clear dead plants and weeds. Keep trees about 10 feet (3 m) apart. Trim branches 10 feet from chimneys. Move wood piles out to Zone 2.
  • Zone 2, 30 to 100 feet (30 m): mow annual grass to 4 inches (10 cm) or less. Space shrubs and trees apart. Clear fallen branches. Keep wood piles 10 feet from other plants.

Smoke days

Wildfire smoke is harmful far from the flames. The sources used here say to learn how to limit smoke exposure and to keep a way to get alerts. Ask your local health department for smoke advice. For heat illness, see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Earthquake

Warning signs: Usually none. Some regions have the ShakeAlert early-warning system, run by the USGS, which can send a phone alert seconds before strong shaking arrives. Make sure your phone can receive it. When you feel shaking or get an alert, drop, cover, and hold on.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Strap or latch TVs, shelves, bookcases, mirrors, and water heaters.
  • Practice drop, cover, and hold on with everyone at home.
  • Keep a bedside kit with sturdy shoes, a flashlight, and a whistle.
  • Learn to shut off your gas, water, and power.
  • Standard home insurance usually does not cover quake damage.
  • Drop, cover, hold on (Figure 6).
  • In bed, stay put and cover your head with a pillow.
  • Outdoors, move to open space away from buildings, trees, signs, and lines.
  • Driving: pull over, set the brake, stay inside. Avoid bridges, overpasses, and wires.
  • Wheelchair: lock the wheels and cover your head and neck.
  • Do not use elevators.
  • Pause, look for hazards, and put on shoes.
  • Expect aftershocks. Drop, cover, hold on each time.
  • If you smell gas, do not use lighters or switches. Leave, then call 911 from outside.
  • If trapped, cover your mouth and signal with a whistle or by tapping.
  • At the coast, go to high ground at once (see Tsunami).
  • Check the outside of the house before going in. Use flashlights.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: The "triangle of life" (lying beside furniture) is safer. Fact: A USGS answer page calls it a misguided idea that does not fit how US buildings are built. It calls Drop, Cover, and Hold On the best advice, citing the Red Cross, and the USGS ShakeOut guidance teaches the same steps. Stay away from exterior walls, windows, hanging objects, mirrors, tall furniture, and cabinets holding heavy items or glass.

Tsunami

Warning signs (natural): Strong or long shaking near the coast, a loud roar from the ocean, or the sea suddenly rising or pulling back to bare the seafloor. These may be your only warning. Do not wait for a siren or text.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Find out if your home, school, or work is in a hazard zone. Beaches, bays, harbors, and river mouths are most exposed.
  • Learn and walk the local evacuation routes.
  • With no local plan, aim for ground at least 100 feet (30 m) above sea level or 1 mile (1.6 km) inland.
  • Be ready to leave at once.
  • After shaking, drop, cover, hold on, then go.
  • Move quickly to high ground or inland, as far as you can.
  • A tsunami is a series of waves, not one. Do not go back to look.
  • In the water, grab something that floats.
  • On a boat in open water, head out to sea. In a harbor, go inland instead.
  • Stay on high ground until officials give the all clear. Waves can keep coming for 8 hours or more, the first wave is often not the largest, and dangerous currents can last for days.
  • Avoid floodwater, wet electrical gear, and downed lines.
  • Stay clear of damaged buildings, roads, and bridges.
  • Text instead of calling. Follow local advice on drinking water.
  • Dry the building in 24 to 48 hours or expect mold.

Volcano

Warning signs: Official unrest notices from the USGS and local agencies, and closed trails and zones. Officials change hazard zones as an eruption grows, so keep checking updates.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Check USGS hazard maps to see if you are in a lahar (volcanic mudflow) zone.
  • Learn evacuation routes. Prepare as you would for a flood.
  • Stock N95 masks, goggles, medicine, plastic sheeting, and heavy tape.
  • Keep extra medication and oxygen for anyone with breathing problems.
  • On the volcano: leave hazard zones at once. Obey closure signs, since lahars can cut off your exit. Avoid caves and hollows where gas can collect.
  • On snow-covered volcanoes, avoid valley floors.
  • Lahar warning: go to high ground away from the valley floor.
  • Ashfall: shelter indoors. Seal door, window, and vent gaps and put damp towels at the base of outside doors. Outdoors, wear a well-fitted N95 mask (cloth masks filter far less) and goggles. Children, older adults, and people with lung or heart disease should stay inside. Avoid driving, and if you must, go slowly and leave extra space.
  • Stay off fresh lava. Burning plants can release methane that collects under cover and can explode.
  • Wait until ashfall stops before cleaning, unless a roof must be protected.
  • Roof falls cause many injuries, so be careful up there.
  • Keep moving parts of machinery sealed from ash.
  • Keep following official updates.

The sources opened for this chapter give no special steps for a pyroclastic flow beyond leaving hazard zones early and obeying closures. These flows are fast, so the only protection is distance.

Blizzard and Ice Storm

Warning signs: A blizzard brings strong wind that drives snow into whiteouts and can close roads. An ice storm brings freezing rain that coats roads, trees, and power lines. As with other storms, a watch means get ready and a warning means act.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Seal and insulate your home. Plan a warm place to go if power fails.
  • Stock food, about 1 gallon (3.8 L) of water per person per day, a month of medicine, warm clothes, and a shovel.
  • Install smoke and carbon monoxide detectors with fresh batteries.
  • Learn to spot frostbite and hypothermia.
  • Keep a kit in the car. Avoid travel in the storm.
  • Keep flammables at least 3 feet (1 m) from space heaters and fireplaces.
  • Never heat with an oven or stove. Use battery lights instead of candles.
  • Run generators, grills, and camp stoves outdoors only, away from windows.
  • Stranded in a car: stay in it unless help is clearly close. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour, keep the tailpipe clear of snow, and crack a window.
  • Signal with bright cloth, and raise the hood once snow stops.
  • Walk and drive carefully on ice.
  • Stay away from downed lines. Watch for falling branches and ice.
  • Shovel in short bursts with a partner, because overexertion kills.
  • If the house is too cold, go to a warming center.
  • Check on older neighbors.

Carbon monoxide kills in cold snaps

If you feel sick, dizzy, or weak while a heater, generator, or engine is running, get to fresh air at once. For frostbite and hypothermia care, see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies. For warm clothing, see Part 2 › Shelter.

Extreme Heat

Warning signs: Weather much hotter or more humid than normal for your area and the time of year, often lasting days, plus heat advisories and warnings. Humidity makes it feel hotter. Older adults, young children, and people with chronic illness are at highest risk, and more than 700 people in the US die from extreme heat each year (CDC).

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Find air-conditioned places nearby, such as libraries and community centers.
  • Block sun with shades or reflective window covers. Seal gaps and add insulation.
  • Learn the signs of heat illness.
  • Ask neighbors to check on you, and check on them.
  • Stay in air conditioning. Drink fluids often, even if not thirsty.
  • Wear loose, light, light-colored clothing and a wide-brimmed hat. Take cool showers.
  • Avoid hard work in the heat. Avoid sunburn.
  • In extreme heat, an electric fan alone will not keep you safe. Get to air conditioning.
  • Never leave a child, adult, or animal in a parked car.
  • Heat illness can still appear when the heat eases. Keep drinking fluids and resting, and know the signs below.
  • Keep checking on older neighbors.
Heat exhaustionHeat stroke (emergency)
Heavy sweating, pale skin, cramps, tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea, fainting. Move to a cool place, lie down, loosen clothes, and cool the skin with wet cloths or a cool bath. Sip water or a sports drink if awake and able to swallow. Get medical help if it worsens, there is vomiting, or it lasts over an hour. Very high body temperature (core above about 104°F, 40°C), hot red skin that may be dry or still sweaty, fast pulse, confusion, or passing out. Confusion in the heat means heat stroke until proven otherwise. Call 911 and start cooling at once, ideally in cold water, without waiting for help.

The full care steps, such as cold-water immersion for heat stroke, are in Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Drought

Warning signs: A drought is a long stretch of unusually dry weather that disrupts water supplies. It builds slowly. Look for low reservoirs, dropping well levels, dry soil, and local water limits. Officials may restrict outdoor watering, even for homes on private wells.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Fix leaks. Test toilets with food coloring in the tank.
  • Plant drought-tolerant plants and use mulch and drip watering.
  • Store drinking water for emergencies (see Part 2 › Water).
  • Learn your local water rules.
  • Follow local restrictions.
  • Take shorter showers. Run only full loads of dishes and laundry.
  • Keep a bucket in the shower and use the water for plants.
  • Water lawns less often, in short sessions. Cover pools.
  • Keep conserving until officials lift limits. Recovery can take a long time.
  • Ask your health department about the safety of well or tap water.
  • Repair irrigation leaks you found.

Landslide and Debris Flow

Warning signs: Short, intense bursts of rain can trigger slides. Any slope can fail, and a past slide is the strongest sign of another. The USGS lists these clues.

  • Sounds: cracking wood, knocking boulders, groaning ground, or a rumble like a freight train.
  • Water: sudden changes in a stream, or water flowing across a slope or pooling where it never did.
  • Ground: new cracks, bulges, or shifts in ground and pavement.
  • Structures: leaning poles, bent fences, tilted trees, sagging lines, sticking doors and windows, new wall cracks, and broken water or sewer lines.
PrepareDuringAfter
  • Know your risk. Report warning signs to local authorities.
  • Consider a geologist or engineer to assess your property.
  • Keep native plants on slopes. Do not cut steep slopes.
  • Direct roof and driveway runoff away from slopes.
  • Learn your evacuation routes and alert levels. A warning means slides are happening, so leave.
  • If it is safe, leave the area at once, without waiting for an announcement.
  • Slides come in waves. The first may not be the last.
  • If a slide hits your building, do not try to run out. The USGS says to go to the highest floor or climb onto a countertop.
  • Never drive or walk through flowing water or mud on a road. Stay out of valleys in danger periods.
  • Stay away from the slide area. More slides are likely.
  • Check for trapped people without entering the slide zone. Direct rescuers to them.
  • Avoid downed lines.
  • Return only when authorities say it is safe. Have a professional check damage.

Avalanche

Avalanche safety is mostly about not being in the wrong place. Take a hands-on avalanche course before you travel in snowy mountains. No page can replace one.

Warning signs: Recent avalanches (if you cannot tell how recent, assume it was). Cracks that shoot across the snow. A loud "whumpf" as the snow collapses under you. Heavy new snow, rain, strong wind that builds drifts, and fast warming. Slides on deeply buried weak layers can strike long after a storm.

PrepareDuringAfter
  • Check the regional avalanche forecast every day. It uses five danger levels, from low to extreme.
  • Each person carries a transceiver, a probe, and a shovel, and practices with them. Gear does not stop a slide.
  • Travel with partners. Learn to measure slope angle with an inclinometer.
  • Avoid terrain traps: gullies, cliffs, trees, and rocks.
  • Avoid steep slopes when the forecast or signs say danger. Most slab avalanches start on slopes of 30 to 50 degrees, most often 34 to 45 degrees (Figure 9).
  • Flat ground below a steep slope can still be in the runout path.
  • Ask your course instructor for self-rescue steps. The sources opened for this chapter do not teach them.
  • Burial time is limited. Group members switch to receive, follow the signal, probe to pinpoint, then dig.
  • Most deaths are from suffocation or injury.
  • Call for professional rescue as soon as it is safe (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

After Any Disaster

  • Your own safety first: wear boots, gloves, and eye protection. Work with a partner and take breaks.
  • Use flashlights, not candles. Treat every wire as live.
  • Gas smell: leave, then call 911 from outside. Never run fuel-burning devices indoors.
  • Stress, worry, and poor sleep are normal. Rest, eat, and reach out. In the US, the Disaster Distress Helpline is free at 1-800-985-5990.
  • Plan for days alone. Responders may be slow to arrive (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming).
