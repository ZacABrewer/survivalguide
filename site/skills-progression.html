Skills Progression

Reading about survival does not make you able to do it. Skills fade, and they get harder under stress, so you need to practice them on a schedule. This chapter gives you three-level checklists for ten skill areas, a list of real courses worth taking, six drills, a 12-month plan for a family, and the safety rules that keep practice from becoming an emergency.

Why Practice Matters

Most people picture survival as knowledge. It is closer to a sport. You can know how a knot is tied and still fumble it with cold hands at dusk. The only cure is doing the skill, again and again, before you need it.

Skills fade faster than you think

Training groups that teach CPR say skill loss is a known problem. A physician quoted in a trade-press report on American Heart Association training goals said that skill decay is significant and shows up within months after a course. The AHA lists shorter, more frequent learning sessions as one of the key elements of better training. (That article is a secondary source; it gives no numbers for how fast skills fade.)

One small study in the Netherlands shows what long gaps do. Researchers tested 99 young adults, all university students, who had learned CPR for a German driver's license, from under a month to more than 10 years earlier. Only 2% met the European criteria on a manikin at the start. After a video of about 6 minutes plus 1 minute of practice each on compressions and rescue breaths, with live feedback, 81% met the criteria. Most were still passing 45 minutes later. The study had no control group and tested only that short delay. It does show two things: old skills are often gone, and a short, hands-on refresher brings them back fast.

A preprint (a study not yet peer reviewed) followed nurses who had passed a skills check at 100%. At later checks at 1, 3, and 6 months, roughly 73% to 92% of them needed remediation at each check, depending on the group. Treat this as a hint, not proof. Its authors say it should not guide clinical practice. Still, it matches the main message: even trained people slip within months.

Stress makes it worse

Fear and fatigue narrow your attention and make multi-step tasks harder (see Part 1 › Survival Mindset & Psychology). A skill you have done twenty times is one you can do on autopilot. A skill you have done once needs all your attention, and under stress you will not have it to spare.

Rehearsal is how plans become real

Ready.gov tells families to hold regular household meetings to review and rehearse their plan. Fire departments say the same about escape plans: practice at least twice a year. Rehearsal also exposes what is broken. The window that will not open, the headlamp with dead batteries, and the meeting place nobody remembers all show up in a drill, not in a disaster.

Tip: Short and often beats long and rare

Fifteen minutes every week does more than a full day once a year. Pick one skill, do it, and write down how it went. The progress log at the end of this chapter makes that easy.

The Skill Ladder

Each skill area has three levels. Climb them in order. Do not skip ahead because a higher level sounds more exciting.

  • Beginner (Level 1): you can do it once, calmly, in good weather, with this guide open.
  • Intermediate (Level 2): you can do it without the guide, in poor conditions or with a time target, and you can teach it to someone else.
  • Advanced (Level 3): you can do it tired, in the dark, wet, or cold, with improvised gear, and you can fix your own mistakes.

The levels below are sample targets, not official standards. Times and conditions are suggestions that you can adjust for your age, fitness, and region. Where a number matters for safety (water treatment, medical steps, distances), follow the chapter named in the table and not this page.

Tip: Test each other

Formal Courses Worth Taking

A book cannot watch your hands. A good instructor can. These are the courses that pay off most.

Warning: Online-only is not enough

Watching a video does not teach your hands to do chest compressions or pack a wound. Stop the Bleed requires an in-person skills session for a certificate. Pick courses where an instructor watches you do the skill.

Six Practice Drills

Run each drill with a goal, a timer, and a short debrief. Ask three questions: What went well? What went wrong? What do we change?

1. Home fire drill

  1. Draw a floor plan of your home. Mark two ways out of every room.
  2. Check that the doors and windows on your routes open easily and lead outside.
  3. Test every smoke alarm and name one meeting place outside, in front of the home.
  4. Have someone press the test button. Everyone walks to the meeting place. Time it.
  5. Repeat at least twice a year, and again whenever you move furniture or someone new joins the household.

Fire departments give this schedule in their home escape guidance. Make a second pass in the dark, with a child or older family member leading.

2. Power-out weekend

  1. Pick a weekend and tell the family the rules: act as if the power is out. Leave the fridge and freezer running if you want to protect food, and use nothing else that plugs in. Do not open the breaker panel unless you know how to do it safely.
  2. Cook only on your off-grid stove. Use only stored water.
  3. Light the house with lamps and headlamps. Stay in touch with a battery or crank radio.
  4. Keep a list of everything that went wrong or that you wished you had.
  5. Restock the items you used, and fix the list gaps within a week.

Warning: Never run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors

These make carbon monoxide, which kills quietly. Generators belong outdoors, at least 20 feet (6 m) from doors, windows, and vents (see Part 1 › Kits & Gear and Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning).

3. Overnight in the backyard

  1. Pitch the tarp or tent. Pack the same kit you would carry in a real emergency.
  2. Sleep out for one night, with the back door unlocked and a phone nearby.
  3. Cook, boil water, and use only the gear you packed.
  4. At dawn, list what was cold, wet, or missing. Fix it and repeat in a different season.

4. Map-and-compass walk

  1. Choose a park or trail system with a good topographic map. Tell someone your route and return time.
  2. Plot four points on the map. Take a bearing to each and walk to it. Count your paces on one leg.
  3. At each point, check your position on the map before moving.
  4. Bring a phone with offline maps as a backup, but navigate with the map first.

The NPS list of Ten Essentials includes a map, a compass, and a way to use them. It says you should know how to use them before you go. See Part 9 › Maps & Compass for the full drill set.

5. Fire in the rain

  1. Pick a calm, damp day and a safe fire spot with water and a shovel nearby (see Part 2 › Fire, Safety First).
  2. Gather your tinder, kindling, and fuel under cover before you strike a spark.
  3. Use dry inner wood and a ferro rod. Build a platform so the fire is off the wet ground.
  4. Time the drill. Repeat until you can do it with cold hands.
  5. Drown the fire, stir the ashes, and drown again. Hold the back of your hand just above the ashes; any heat means more water.

6. Signaling drill

  1. Give each family member a whistle and a signal mirror.
  2. Send one person out of sight. Have the others use the whistle pattern and the mirror to guide them back.
  3. Lay out a ground signal with bright cloth or rocks, and check it from a high spot.
  4. Run a radio check with the phonetic alphabet and a one-line message.

See Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication for the patterns and the message format.

The 12-Month Family Training Plan

Start in any month. The cycle is one theme per month, with one family session of about two hours, plus one short weekly drill. Move the outdoor items to the season that fits where you live.

MonthThemeFamily session
1PlanWrite the contact plan. Draw the floor plan. Run the home fire drill. Check all kits.
2WaterBoil and filter water. Store water for 3 days. Find the shutoff.
3First aidSign up for a CPR/First Aid and a Stop the Bleed class. Check the medical kit.
4FireLight fires with three methods. Do the rain drill when weather allows.
5MapDo the map-and-compass walk. Download offline maps.
6ShelterOvernight in the backyard. Try a tarp pitch the kids design.
7SignalWhistle, mirror, and ground signal drill.
8RadioRadio check and practice with the phonetic alphabet. Study for the ham license if you want one.
9FoodCook off-grid. Preserve a batch. Rotate the stockpile.
10PowerThe power-out weekend. Repeat the home fire drill.
11KnotsTie the essential knots. Build a lashed frame.
12TestA test day with a scenario: lights out, a minor injury, and a bag to grab. Redo the checklists and plan next year.

Roles for each age

  • Young children: learn your address and phone number, the meeting place, the whistle signal, and how to call 911. Make drills a game, not a scare.
  • Older kids and teens: carry a kit, take Stop the Bleed (no minimum age), and lead a drill. Many CERT programs have teen versions.
  • Adults: take the CPR/First Aid and WFA classes. Hold the plan, the kits, and the log.
  • Older adults: choose the drill roles that match their strength and give them jobs that use their experience (see Part 1 › Survival Mindset & Psychology).

Safety Rules for Practice

Drills should teach, not hurt. Most practice injuries come from rushing, from tools, and from weather you did not check.

Fire

  • Learn the local fire rules before you light anything. The NPS tells visitors to check the fire-use regulations of the park they plan to visit.
  • Keep water and a shovel next to every practice fire.
  • Never leave a fire alone. Put it fully out before you leave (see Part 2 › Fire, Safety First).

Knives and tools

  • Cut away from your body and away from others. Keep a circle clear around you.
  • Sharp tools are safer than dull ones, but only with good habits. Teach kids with supervision.
  • Keep a first aid kit within reach and know how to stop bleeding before you start.

Water

  • Do not practice river crossings or ice travel on your own (see Part 9 › Travel & Movement).
  • Test any "found" water with caution. Treat before drinking.
  • Wear a life jacket on boats and near deep water. See Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies for the cold-water rules.

Weather

  • Check the forecast before every outdoor drill (see Part 9 › Weather).
  • Cancel for lightning, high wind near trees, flood risk, or heat or cold beyond your plan.
  • Have an exit plan and tell someone where you are.

Do not

  • Practice injuries or medical treatments on a real person without a trained instructor.
  • Run a power-out weekend with a medical need (oxygen, refrigerated medicine) unless you have a backup.
  • Test your gear to failure on the only night you have it.
  • Skip the debrief. It is where you learn.

Logging Your Progress

A log turns practice into progress. It shows what you have done, what is stale, and what to do next. Keep it in a notebook that lives in your kit, with a copy on your phone.

Log these facts every time:

  • Date, skill, and level you tested.
  • What you did, how long it took, and who watched.
  • What went wrong, and one thing to fix.
  • Date of your next practice. Re-test every skill at least once a year, and critical skills like CPR more often.

Mark a checklist level as passed only when a second person has seen you do it. Mark it as stale if more than a year has passed. Stale means re-do.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: I took the course, so I am set for life. Fact: Skills fade within months without practice. Course cards expire for a reason.
