Clothing & Layering

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026
On this page

  2. The Three-Layer System
  3. Fabrics Compared
  4. Dressing by Climate
  5. Head, Hands, and Feet
  6. Footwear and Foot Care
  7. Sun-Protective Clothing
  8. Improvised Clothing
  9. Clothing Care and Field Repair
  10. Sources

Clothing is your first shelter. It traps a thin layer of still air around you, blocks wind and rain, and shades your skin from the sun. Most clothing mistakes come from two things: getting wet (from rain or from your own sweat) and wearing the wrong fabric for the climate. This chapter shows how heat leaves the body, how to build a layering system, which fabrics to trust, how to care for your feet, and how to make clothing from what you have.

How Clothing Fights Heat Loss

Your body loses heat in a few ways. Each way has a clothing answer. NOLS teaches four: conduction, convection, radiation, and evaporation. Breathing out warm, moist air adds a fifth, small loss (see Part 2 › Shelter for the full list and the myths about head and ground heat loss).

The key idea is trapped air. Wool, fleece, down, and synthetic fill all work by holding still air in tiny pockets. Anything that crushes those pockets, such as water, dirt, or tight packing, makes clothing colder. NOLS points out that layering lets trapped air do the insulating, and that dressing so you do not sweat is the best defense against evaporative loss.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: You lose 40 to 45 percent (some say 80 percent) of your body heat through your head. Fact: The old Army survival manual repeats the 40 to 45 percent figure, but doctors who reviewed the evidence for the BMJ traced it to a military test where the head was the only bare body part. With the whole body exposed, the head gives off no more than about 10 percent. Heat leaves from any bare skin. Cover your head and neck because they are so often left bare. The CDC notes a hat cuts heat loss from the head, but a hat does not replace a full set of layers.

The Three-Layer System

One thick coat is a poor plan. Several thin layers let you add or remove warmth in small steps. REI describes three layers, each with its own job. Carry all three if you can, since you can take layers off but cannot put on what you left behind.

  • Base layer. It sits against the skin and moves sweat away from it. Wool, silk, polyester, and nylon work. Thicker base layers are warmer, but wicking is the main job.
  • Mid layer. It holds in heat. Fleece stays warm when damp, dries fast, and breathes well, but wind passes through it. Down and synthetic fill are covered below.
  • Shell. It blocks wind, rain, and snow. A waterproof, breathable shell suits heavy storms. A water-resistant one suits drizzle and hard work. A shell with no breathability is fine for slow, sitting jobs (like fishing) but you will soak yourself in sweat if you work hard in it.

The COLD rule

US military cold-injury training uses the word COLD as a memory aid. The Army survival manual and the Defense Health Agency both give the same four points: keep clothing Clean, avoid Overheating, wear clothing Loose and in Layers, and keep clothing Dry.

The Army manual says tight clothing cuts circulation, and that several light layers beat one equally thick layer. The Defense Health Agency adds that you should remove layers as air temperature or work rate goes up, so you do not build up moisture inside your clothes. In extreme cold, do not strip down right after hard work. Wait until you are somewhere warmer.

Tip

Start a hike slightly cool. Stop and add a layer at rest breaks, before you start to shiver. Take it off again before you start to sweat. Pack spare dry clothes in a plastic bag.

Fabrics Compared

What matters most is what a fabric does when it gets wet. You will get wet from rain, from stream crossings, and from sweat.

Why cotton can kill

Every fabric loses warmth when it is soaked, because water fills the air pockets and carries heat away much faster than air. Wind and evaporation make it worse. The difference is how long the cloth stays wet. In a 2021 lab study of soaked socks (Autex Research Journal), every fiber lost about half or more of its insulation when wet. Cotton lost the most and still held water after 8 hours of drying. Polyester, nylon, and polypropylene got back their full dry insulation within about 6 hours, and the wool sock kept the most warmth while wet. That is one small study of socks, but it fits the advice of the Defense Health Agency (avoid cotton in the cold) and REI. Cotton stays cold and wet for hours. That is the reason for the saying "cotton kills."

Warning

In cold, wet, or windy weather, avoid cotton jeans, hoodies, T-shirts, and cotton socks. They are fine in a warm, dry place. They are dangerous when the weather can turn and you cannot change or get warm.

Cotton in hot, dry country

The same trait that makes cotton risky in the cold helps in dry heat. REI and the Washington Trails Association both say cotton can work in hot, dry weather because sweat that holds in the cloth and evaporates cools you. The Army manual tells desert travelers to keep clothing on, since cloth holds sweat against the skin so you get its cooling. The catch is the evening. Desert nights can turn cold, so carry a dry warm layer and avoid cotton if the temperature can drop.

Down or synthetic?

If you expect dry cold, down gives the most warmth for the least weight. If you expect rain, snow, or damp, synthetic fill is safer, since it still insulates when wet. Water-resistant down helps only with light moisture.

Dressing by Climate

Hot, dry country (desert)

  1. Wear loose, airy, light-colored clothing. Light shades reflect sunlight, and a loose fit lets heat out.
  2. Cover your skin. The Army manual says to roll sleeves down, cover the head, and shield the neck. Do not work shirtless in the day. REI and the Washington Trails Association also say long sleeves and long pants cool you, since they shade skin from sun.
  3. Wear a hat with a brim all the way around. It shades the face and neck better than a ball cap. Add a neck cloth and sunglasses.
  4. Wet a bandana or neck cloth and wear it on your head or neck. Evaporation cools you. Do not wet clothes with mineral-laden water, which the Army manual says may cause a skin rash.
  5. Wear gloves when you handle rock or brush. Shake out boots and clothing before you put them on, since spiders and scorpions can hide in them.
  6. Keep a warm layer for the night. The Army manual notes desert temperatures can fall from about 131°F (55°C) by day to about 50°F (10°C) at night. It recommends a wool sweater, long underwear, and a wool cap.

Wet cold

Cold with rain, sleet, or wet snow is hard on clothing, because water is always trying to get in and your effort pushes sweat out. Use a waterproof shell, wool or synthetic layers underneath, and change out of wet clothes as soon as you can. Use fleece or synthetic fill, which keep working damp. Put on rain gear before you get wet. Hypothermia can start in air well above freezing (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Dry cold

Dry cold lets you use down. Wind is the main problem. On the National Weather Service wind chill chart, 0°F (-18°C) air with a 15 mph (24 km/h) wind feels like -19°F (-28°C). (The older Army manual uses a pre-2001 wind chill table that gives colder numbers; use the NWS chart.) Pair insulation with a wind-blocking shell, a hood, and a face cover. Vent early, because sweat that freezes in your clothes becomes the next problem. Brush snow off before you go into a shelter, since melting snow wets your layers.

Hot and wet (tropics)

Apply the hot-weather rules, but expect that clothing will stay wet. I found no strong source for tropical clothing, so treat these as reasonable practice rather than tested rules. Cotton dries slowly in humid air, so quick-drying synthetic cloth is the better pick. Light, loose long sleeves and pants cut insect bites and sun. Keep a dry set to sleep in.

Head, Hands, and Feet

Hands, feet, ears, nose, and cheeks are the first places to go cold. They are small, far from your core, and easy to forget. Protecting them keeps you working and lowers the risk of frostbite (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

  • Head and neck. Wear a warm hat and a hood, and add a neck tube or scarf. In deep cold, cover your nose and lower face. The Army manual says to use your mittens as a cover if you are alone and have nothing else.
  • Hands. Mittens are generally warmer than gloves of the same material, because your fingers share heat. Gloves give you dexterity. Wear a thin liner glove under a thicker windproof mitten, so you can take the outer layer off for fine work and keep your hands warm. Choose liner and shell fabrics that are both natural or both synthetic, to cut friction. Leave about a quarter inch (6 mm) of room past your fingertip, and avoid anything so tight it squeezes the back of the hand.
  • Feet. The next section covers them in detail.

Warning

Do not touch bare skin to cold metal, as the CDC cautions. Keep spare socks, gloves, and a hat dry in a bag. Loose clothing is safer than tight clothing, because tight sleeves, cuffs, and boots cut circulation.

Footwear and Foot Care

If your feet fail, you stop moving. Plan for good fit first, since a poor fit causes most blisters.

Choosing footwear

  • Cold. The CDC advises waterproof, insulated boots. Leave room for toes to move, because tight boots and tight socks reduce blood flow (Defense Health Agency). In cold, a boot that is a bit roomy with a good sock system is better than a snug one.
  • Hot and dry. Wear boots or shoes that cover the whole foot. The Army manual says never to walk barefoot through ruins, dumps, caves, or rock outcrops, and to check footwear for snakes and spiders before you put it on.
  • Wet. Expect your feet to get wet. Carry spare dry socks and swap them in. If you can, keep a spare pair of dry boots or shoes (a Wilderness College author suggests this).
  • Break-in. Break new boots in on short trips before a big one.

The sock system

REI recommends a thin liner sock under a wool or synthetic sock. The liner wicks moisture and takes the rubbing, so skin slides less. REI also says to avoid cotton socks, which hold water. Socks that are too big wrinkle, and socks that are too small cause pressure and slipping. Change into dry socks after a stream crossing.

Blisters

  1. Stop at the first "hot spot." Do not wait. Even slight redness is enough.
  2. Take off your boot and sock and let the skin dry.
  3. Cover the spot. REI lists paper surgical tape, moleskin, and blister pads or gels. Duct tape works in a pinch.
  4. If a blister has formed, cut a doughnut of moleskin or foam with a hole the size of the blister. Place it around the blister so your sock does not touch it. Tape over everything.
  5. Leave a small blister closed. REI says to drain only a large one that is too painful to leave. If you must, wash it with soap, use a needle cleaned with alcohol or heat, drain it near the edge, and dress it like a wound.
  6. Watch for infection: spreading redness, pus, increasing pain, or red streaks (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

In the field, the Army manual adds to wash and dry your feet daily, trim toenails straight across, and break in footwear. If you have no blister supplies, cloth scraps, moss, or dry grass can cushion a sore spot, as one field-repair guide notes.

Keeping feet dry

Wet feet can lead to trench foot (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies). To prevent it:

  • Change wet or damp socks as soon as you can. Carry spares in a waterproof bag. The Army manual says to dry damp socks unfolded, close to your body, or hung on your pack.
  • Wash and dry your feet each day, and put on dry socks.
  • Dry leather boots slowly. The Army manual suggests drying them between the sleeping-bag shell and liner. The Defense Health Agency suggests stuffing them with paper towels. Do not rush it with strong heat.
  • Do not lace boots so tight that they cut circulation. Move your toes often.

Sun-Protective Clothing

In the sun, clothing is your best sunscreen, since it cannot wear off or get missed. The Skin Cancer Foundation explains the UPF label. A UPF 50 fabric lets through about 2 percent (1/50) of UV rays. UPF 30 to 49 is "very good" protection and UPF 50+ is "excellent." Snow and water reflect UV, so the same rules apply on a glacier or a lake.

Without a label, these things matter:

  • Weave. Dense cloth such as denim, canvas, wool, or tight synthetics blocks more than thin, loose cloth.
  • Color. Dark or bright colors absorb UV and generally block more than pale shades. There is a trade-off: pale colors reflect sunlight and feel cooler (REI, Washington Trails Association). Neither side gives a rule for which matters more, so choose a dense weave first, then pick color for the heat you face.
  • Wet or stretched. Cloth loses protection when it is stretched or wet. The Foundation notes a white T-shirt is about UPF 7 dry and about UPF 3 wet. Loose clothing protects better than tight.
  • Coverage. Long sleeves and long pants beat shorts and tanks.
  • Hats. A brim of at least 3 inches (7.5 cm) shades face, ears, and neck. Loose straw lets UV through. Pick a tight weave.
  • Eyes. Wear UV-filtering sunglasses. For snow blindness and improvised goggles, see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Improvised Clothing

If you lack the right clothing, the goal is simple: add trapped dry air, and block wind and water. Most field fixes are rough and itchy, but rough is fine if it keeps you alive.

Stuffing clothes with insulation

An Outdoor Life survival writer and Wilderness College both describe the same trick: pack the space between two layers of clothing with dry plant material. It traps dead air, which is what insulation is.

  1. Gather dry, dead material: leaves, grass, moss, ferns, or pine needles. Dead and dry works better than green and wet. Cattail down and milkweed down also insulate (Army manual).
  2. Wear your loosest garment as the outer layer so there is room. If you have only one layer, put the stuffing against your skin and tie off sleeves and cuffs.
  3. Work from the bottom up. Tuck pants into socks, open the waist, and fill each leg all around. Do the same for the shirt, front, back, and sleeves. Finish with a hat or hood.
  4. Tie or belt the openings, so stuffing does not fall out.
  5. Add more fill if it packs down and thin spots appear.

Wet fill barely helps, and if you are already chilled, cold skin may not warm damp fill.

Warning

Skip fiberglass wall insulation, which one source lists as a last resort. It is itchy and unsafe to breathe. Use natural fill instead.

Trash bags and plastic

A large garbage bag cut with holes for your head and arms makes an emergency rain poncho (one field-repair guide describes this). Cut the head hole small, and never pull a plastic bag over a head or face, especially a child's. Bags also tear and trap sweat, so vent when you work hard.

A word on vapor barriers

A vapor barrier liner is waterproof cloth that stops sweat vapor and keeps it from evaporating from you, which can help in very cold weather. Outside magazine says these liners are used mostly as oversocks or sleeping-bag liners. It warns against wearing them as clothing, since you get soaked in your own sweat, risk dehydration, and may ice up. If you must use plastic as a sock liner, put it over a thin sock inside a boot, and check and dry your feet often. The Defense Health Agency says to dry the inside of vapor-barrier boots at least once a day, more often if your feet sweat. Treat plastic the same way: take it off, dry your feet, and change the thin sock at least daily. Feet kept damp for days in plastic can get trench foot, even well above freezing (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies). I did not find a primary source on bread bags or trash bags used this way, so treat it as a last resort.

Hides, fur, and parachute cloth

The Army manual says to pick hides from large animals with heavy coats and fat. Avoid diseased animals. Clean the skin, remove meat and fat, and dry it completely. Wear the fur side in. Use hide from the leg joints for shoes, mittens, or socks. For preparing hides, see Part 3 › Animal Processing & Hides. A parachute (canopy, lines, harness) can supply cloth and cord for clothing. The manual also describes an improvised sleeping bag from parachute cloth, with dry leaves, pine needles, or moss packed between two layers.

Mittens from a blanket

This is a basic craft method, not a tested design.

  1. Fold the blanket in half. Lay your hand flat on it with fingers together and thumb out.
  2. Trace around your hand, leaving about an inch (2.5 cm) of space all around. Add space at the wrist for a cuff.
  3. Cut two matching shapes for each mitten. Cut two more if you want a double layer.
  4. Place the pieces together and sew around the edge with a small, tight stitch. Leave the wrist open.
  5. Turn them right side out. Add a strip of cord or cloth at the wrist and tie it to hold the mitten on.

Blankets work best if they are wool. Wear a thin glove or sock inside, so the rough cloth does not rub.

Clothing Care and Field Repair

  • Keep it clean. The Army manual says dirt and grease reduce insulation. If you cannot wash, shake and air clothes. It suggests airing them for about an hour or two, and sunning them if water is short. Change underwear and socks often, and check clothing for lice at night.
  • Dry as you go. Hang damp mittens and socks on your pack or put them against your body. Hang other damp clothes near the top of a shelter, where it is warmest. Put dry material between wet shoes and your feet if you must air-dry.
  • Air sleeping gear. The Army manual says to turn sleeping bags inside out, fluff them, and air them after each use, so they stay lofty.
  • Fix holes fast. A small tear grows. Stitch it, or tape it, as soon as you can.

Simple repairs

  • Thread and needle. Use dental floss, fishing line, or thin cord when you have no thread. A fishhook or a scrap of metal can be sharpened into a needle. For a hole, whipstitch the edges together, or sew a patch over it with small stitches.
  • Duct tape. It holds a patch, a loose sole, or a split seam. Wrap a few feet around a water bottle to carry it.
  • Laces. Replace a snapped lace with cord, fishing line, or wire.
  • Zippers. A paracord loop can replace a broken pull. Rub soap or candle wax on stiff teeth.
  • Soles. Tape or tie down a loose sole right away. A flapping sole can trip you.

For cord and knots, see Part 3 › Cordage, Knots & Lashings.

Do not

  • Do not wear wet cotton in the cold.
  • Do not let yourself sweat through your layers, then stop in the wind.
  • Do not put your feet in plastic bags for days without drying them.
  • Do not go barefoot in the desert, or in ruins and rubble.
