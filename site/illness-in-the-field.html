Illness in the Field

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026
On this page

  4. Respiratory Infections

Most illness in the field is not dramatic. A sick gut, a fever, a sore eye, or a bad tooth can still sink a trip when help is days away. This chapter covers the common illnesses, how to spot the ones that are getting dangerous, and what you can do with water, rest, and careful watching. It is written at first-responder level and leaves out prescription medicines. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course will teach you far more than a page can.

Limits of this chapter

You cannot diagnose most illness without tests. Treat this chapter as a guide to watching and comforting a sick person, and to deciding when to leave for care (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions). Take vital signs and write them down (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). For medicines, use over-the-counter products only as directed on the label (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).

Diarrhea, Vomiting, and Dehydration

Traveler's diarrhea is usually caused by bacteria such as E. coli or by viruses such as norovirus. Parasites are less common. Germs reach you through dirty water, food washed in it, ice, and unwashed hands (Merck Manual). Most mild cases clear up in about 3 to 5 days. The real danger is losing too much fluid, not the germ itself.

Prevent it by treating water (see Part 2 › Water), cooking food well (see Part 5 › Food Safety), and washing hands (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation).

How to tell someone is dehydrated

Watch urine first. Dark urine means mild dehydration. Little or no urine means it is getting worse. Check babies and small children often, and use the diaper and tear signs below.

WHO's child-illness charts use four quick checks: how alert the child is, sunken eyes, how well they drink, and a skin pinch on the belly. Two or more of these mean severe dehydration: very sleepy or hard to wake, sunken eyes, unable to drink or drinking poorly, and a skin pinch that goes back very slowly (2 seconds or more). Two or more of these mean some dehydration: restless or irritable, sunken eyes, drinking eagerly as if very thirsty, and a skin pinch that goes back slowly (WHO). Severe dehydration needs IV fluids, so it means evacuate now.

Danger signs: get out or get help

  • Blood in the stool, even a small amount.
  • Fever with the diarrhea.
  • Signs of significant dehydration that do not improve with drinking (above).
  • Vomiting that will not stop, so the person cannot keep fluids down.
  • A baby or small child who is very sleepy, will not drink, or has not urinated for many hours.

The Merck Manual lists fever and blood in the stool as reasons to seek care. WHO adds diarrhea lasting 14 days or more. The Rehydration Project says to go to a clinic if diarrhea increases or vomiting continues.

Cholera-type stools: an emergency

Cholera causes large amounts of watery diarrhea. The CDC says the stools are sometimes called "rice-water stools" because they look milky white. Vomiting and leg cramps are common (WHO). Only about 1 in 10 infected people get severe illness, but in them dehydration can kill within hours (CDC), and WHO warns cholera can kill within 24 hours.

  1. Start ORS right away. Do not wait to be sure it is cholera (CDC).
  2. Keep giving ORS in small, frequent sips while you head for care, and on the way.
  3. Get medical help at once. A person who is badly dehydrated may need IV fluids, but should still drink ORS as soon as they can.
  4. Do not give juice, soft drinks, or sports drinks instead. The CDC says sugary drinks can make diarrhea worse.

Do not

  • Do not give anti-diarrhea medicine such as loperamide to a child under 18 with acute diarrhea.
  • Do not use it if there is blood in the stool or fever. The Merck Manual advises against it in both cases, and also if the person recently took antibiotics.
  • Do not take leftover antibiotics. Many cases are viral, and antibiotics do not work on viruses.
  • Do not stop all drinking because the person is vomiting. Use small, frequent sips (below).

Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS)

Plain water replaces fluid but not the salts lost with it. Oral rehydration solution is water with the right amounts of sugar and salt. The sugar helps the gut pull the salt and water in. It does not stop diarrhea. It prevents the dehydration that makes diarrhea dangerous.

The packet (WHO / UNICEF reduced-osmolarity formula)

WHO and UNICEF recommend a reduced-osmolarity formula (245 mOsm/L). One 20.5 g packet holds 2.6 g sodium chloride, 13.5 g glucose, 1.5 g potassium chloride, and 2.9 g trisodium citrate (WHO/UNICEF statement, via the Rehydration Project). Mix one whole packet into exactly 1 liter (a bit over 1 US quart) of clean water, boiled and cooled if you are unsure of it. Do not boil the mix itself, because heat can burn the sugar.

The homemade recipe

If you have no packets, you can make a close substitute. Measure level spoonfuls:

Clean water
1 liter (a bit over 1 US quart; about 5 cups of 200 mL)
Sugar
6 level teaspoons
Salt
½ level teaspoon

This is the recipe in WHO's Eastern Mediterranean office leaflet for the public, which uses 1 liter of clean, safe water. The Rehydration Project gives the same amounts and says the spoons should be level. Use a real measuring teaspoon (5 mL) if you have one. A review of home recipes found that using a "pinch" of salt or a tablespoon of sugar instead of level teaspoons made many home mixes wrong, some of them dangerous. Older recipes differ: a 1980s WHO home formula used 8 level teaspoons of sugar and 1 of salt per liter, and another Rehydration Project page still lists it. This guide uses the newer, weaker 6-and-½ mix, which matches the lower-salt WHO packet better. Too much salt is the dangerous mistake. It can seriously harm a child. Too much sugar can worsen diarrhea. Use packets whenever you have them.

How to give it

  1. Wash your hands with soap and water before you mix and before you serve.
  2. Offer small amounts often, using a clean cup or spoon. Let the person drink as much as they want.
  3. After each loose stool, give extra. WHO's chart says 50 to 100 mL (about ¼ to ½ cup) for a child under 2, and 100 to 200 mL (about ½ to 1 cup) for a child 2 or older. Adults should drink freely.
  4. If the person vomits, wait 10 minutes. Then go on, more slowly (WHO).
  5. Keep it cool. Do not keep mixed solution more than 24 hours (WHO/UNICEF). Mix a fresh batch.
  6. Add only water. Do not mix ORS with milk, juice, soup, or soft drinks, and do not add extra sugar.

The Rehydration Project guide is written for children and says to give no antibiotics or other drugs for diarrhea unless a trained health worker says so. For adults, the Merck Manual says to drink plenty of safe fluids.

Feeding through diarrhea

Do not starve a child with diarrhea. The Rehydration Project says to keep giving breast milk and, for children 4 months and older, to keep giving solid food along with ORS. WHO also recommends a 10 to 14 day course of zinc tablets for children with diarrhea; it shortens the illness. Get zinc and its dose from a health worker. For adults, this guide's practical rule is to eat small, plain, easy meals when appetite returns, while you keep drinking.

Fever

Mayo Clinic counts an oral temperature of 100°F (37.8°C) or higher as a fever. Fevers usually pass within a few days.

Measuring it

Oral and rectal thermometers are the most accurate. For infants, rectal is somewhat more accurate (Mayo Clinic).

Estimating fever without a thermometer

Without a thermometer, you cannot get a number. Touch is all you have, so know its limits. Two reviews of studies on parents feeling a child's skin found:

  • Touch caught most real fevers: about 87 to 89 out of 100.
  • But it was often wrong when it said "fever." About half of the children without a fever were judged hot.
  • So if the skin feels normal, fever is fairly unlikely. If it feels hot, the person may or may not have a fever.

A practical approach (this guide's suggestion, not a tested method): feel the forehead or trunk, and compare with a healthy person in the same conditions. Then look at the whole person: chills, sweats, flushing, aches, headache, poor appetite, a faster pulse and breathing. Write down what you find every few hours and watch the trend (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Never use a "feels hot" guess to rule a serious illness in or out; the warning signs below matter more than the number.

Comfort and cooling

  • Rest in a cool, shaded place, with light, loose clothing.
  • Drink plenty of fluids. Fever dries people out. Use ORS if there is diarrhea or vomiting.
  • A cool washcloth on the head or wrists feels good (UPMC).
  • Over-the-counter acetaminophen or ibuprofen can ease discomfort. Use as directed on the label. Read the label for children's limits.

Do not

  • Do not use ice baths. UPMC warns they can drop body temperature dangerously.
  • Do not rub alcohol on the skin. It can be harmful if it is absorbed.
  • Do not pile on blankets, which raises body heat further.

A high temperature with confusion after hard work in the heat may be heat stroke, not infection (see Part 0 › Red Pages).

Warning signs with a fever

Mayo Clinic and UPMC both say to get immediate care when a fever comes with any of these:

  • Stiff neck, especially pain when bending the head forward, or a severe headache.
  • Rash, bruising, or swelling.
  • Confusion, odd speech, or fainting.
  • A seizure.
  • Trouble breathing or chest pain.
  • Severe belly pain, repeated vomiting, or painful urination.

You cannot treat these in the field. Start the evacuation now (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

How long is too long

  • Adults: Mayo Clinic says to call a provider at 103°F (39.4°C) or higher. UPMC says a fever above 103°F for over 48 hours needs evaluation, and any fever over seven days needs a call.
  • Children: more than three days, or sooner if the child is listless, confused, or vomiting repeatedly (Mayo Clinic).
  • Babies under 3 months: a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher needs a provider (Mayo Clinic).

Respiratory Infections

Most coughs and sore throats are colds. They are miserable but mild. A person with a cold is up and about, drinks well, and breathes easily. Pneumonia is an infection deep in the lungs, and it is the one to catch early, especially in children.

Colds versus pneumonia

Likely a coldThink pneumonia / get care
Runny nose, sore throat, coughFast breathing for the person's age
Breathing is easy and quietLower chest pulls in with each breath (children)
Drinks and eats a littleCannot drink or feed; vomits everything
Alert, even if tiredVery sleepy, hard to wake, or confused
Lips and nails look normalBlue lips or fingernails

Counting a child's breathing

Fast breathing is the best simple sign of pneumonia in a child. Count when the child is calm, because crying and fussing speed up breathing (MSF). WHO's IMCI chart booklet says to count the breaths in one full minute. Fast breathing means:

  • Under 2 months: 60 breaths a minute or more. If the first count is 60 or more, count again.
  • 2 months up to 12 months: 50 or more.
  • 12 months up to 5 years: 40 or more.
  1. Keep the child quiet. A sleeping or resting child is best. Lift or open clothing so you can see the chest and belly.
  2. Use a watch, or count slowly in your head to 60. Count each rise of the chest as one breath.
  3. Compare the count to the limits above.
  4. If the count is high, count again after a few minutes. Look for the other signs in Figure 3.

Pneumonia danger signs in children

MSF lists these signs of severe disease: chest pulling in (clearly visible and constant, not just when upset), blue lips or fingernails, flaring nostrils, grunting on breathing out, a harsh noise on breathing in (stridor), being very sleepy or hard to wake, refusing to drink or feed, and any age under 2 months. WHO's chart adds general danger signs for any sick child: unable to drink or breastfeed, vomiting everything, convulsions, or being very sleepy or unconscious. Chest pulling in or a danger sign means the child needs a clinic or hospital. In a child under 5 who has only fast breathing, the WHO guide says the usual treatment is an oral antibiotic given by a health worker (a prescription medicine, so not covered here). Get care.

A harsh noise (stridor) when a calm child breathes in can mean a swollen airway and can be life-threatening. Get help at once.

Adults and older children

A healthy adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times a minute (StatPearls; MedlinePlus gives 12 to 18). Count for a full minute while the person rests.

  • More than 20 a minute at rest is fast breathing (tachypnea) in an adult. Fever, pain, and fear can raise it, so recount after rest and comfort.
  • More than 25 a minute is a serious warning. In hospital patients it predicts a higher risk of death (StatPearls). With a fever or cough, treat it as a reason to evacuate.
  • Also evacuate anyone with trouble breathing at rest, blue lips, chest pain, confusion, or weakness that grows each day.

Field care is rest, fluids, and fever comfort measures.

Steam: weak evidence, real burn risk

A Cochrane review of six trials found mixed results for breathing heated, humid air with a cold. Some trials showed relief and others did not, and the authors did not recommend it as routine treatment. If someone wants to try it for comfort, it is optional, not treatment. A bowl or pot of hot water can tip and scald, so never use it with children, and never let anyone lean over a pot on a stove or fire.

Eye Problems

Eyes heal poorly and fail fast. A good rule: if an eye hurts, vision changes, or you are not sure, treat it as serious. Never assume an eye injury is harmless (Prevent Blindness).

Chemical splash: flush first

This is the one eye problem where seconds count. Do not stop to look for a first aid kit. A tap or bottle of clean water is fine.

  1. Start flushing right away with a gentle stream of cool, clean water. Prevent Blindness and MedlinePlus say at least 15 minutes. Better Health Victoria says 15 to 20. Aim for 20, and go longer if the eye still burns.
  2. Tilt the head so the injured eye is down and to the side, lower than the good eye. Pour from the nose side outward (Figure 4). If both eyes or a lot of skin are splashed, put the person under a shower (MedlinePlus).
  3. Hold the lids apart with your fingers. Have the person roll the eye around.
  4. If a contact lens is in, start flushing over it. The flow may wash it out. If it is still in after flushing, take it out (MedlinePlus).
  5. Get urgent care. Bring the chemical's name or label. Say whether it was an acid or alkali, and a liquid or powder.

Specks and foreign bodies

  1. Do not rub the eye.
  2. Let tears wash the speck out. Or lift the upper lid out and over the lower lid while looking down. Or use an eyewash (Prevent Blindness).
  3. If it will not wash out, look in good light without pressing on the eye. Have the person look up, down, and side to side. Gently pull the lower lid down to check under it (MedlinePlus).
  4. Check under the upper lid. AAFP says always flip the upper lid to look for a hidden object, and says several vertical scratch lines on the upper cornea suggest one is there.

Flipping the upper eyelid

MedlinePlus describes this method (Figure 5):

  1. Wash your hands. Have the person look down and keep looking down.
  2. Lay a clean cotton swab across the outside of the upper lid.
  3. Grasp the upper lashes gently.
  4. Fold the lid up and back over the swab, so the inside of the lid shows.
  5. Flush with clean water to wash the speck off. If it is on the lid and will not rinse off, touch it lightly with a second clean swab.
  6. Let go. The lid rolls back when the person looks up and blinks.

Do not

  • Do not touch the colored part of the eye with a swab. Use swabs only on the lid or the white of the eye (MedlinePlus).
  • Do not use tweezers or tools, and do not try to remove anything stuck in the eye.
  • Do not rub or press on an injured eye.

Scratched cornea

A scratch (corneal abrasion) causes pain, tearing, light sensitivity, and a gritty feel. AAFP gives these points:

  • Do not patch a simple scratch. Patching does not ease pain and may slow healing.
  • Do not use numbing drops at home. Repeated use can damage the cornea, delay healing, and hide a worsening problem.
  • Contact lens wearers are at higher risk. Their eyes can carry germs that infect a scratch fast and even punch a hole in the cornea. Take the lenses out and keep them out until symptoms are gone.
  • Get an exam if symptoms are worse or unchanged after 24 hours, if vision is blurry, or if a scratch has not healed in three to four days.

Cuts, punctures, and blows

If the eye is cut or something is stuck in it, do not wash it, press on it, or try to pull anything out (Prevent Blindness, MedlinePlus). Protect it with a rigid shield, such as a clean paper cup or the bottom half of one, taped to the brow and cheekbone so nothing touches the eye (Figure 6). MedlinePlus also says to loosely bandage both eyes, because the eyes move together and covering the good eye keeps the injured one still. Keep the person calm. Evacuate.

For a blow or a bleeding cut, hold a clean, cool compress on it without pressure. If blood pools in the eye, cover both eyes with a clean cloth (MedlinePlus). Seek care for pain, blurry vision, a bulging eye, blood inside the eye, or a black eye (Prevent Blindness).

When to patch, and when not to

ProblemCover the eye?
Simple scratch (corneal abrasion)No. Patching does not ease pain and may slow healing (AAFP).
Cut eye or object stuck in itYes: rigid shield or cup over the injured eye, nothing touching it, and loosely cover both eyes (MedlinePlus).
Blood pooling in the eye after a blowYes: cover both eyes with a clean cloth, no pressure (MedlinePlus).
Chemical splashFlush first, for at least 15 minutes. Do not delay flushing to cover or bandage.

Pink eye (conjunctivitis)

Pink eye brings redness, a gritty or itchy feeling, watery eyes, and discharge that can crust the lids shut overnight (CDC, Mayo Clinic). It is most often viral and often comes with a cold. Viral and bacterial pink eye spread easily. Allergy and irritation pink eye do not.

  • Wash hands often. Do not touch or rub the eyes.
  • Do not share towels, pillowcases, eye makeup, or eye drops. Stop wearing contact lenses.
  • Get care for eye pain, light sensitivity, or blurred vision, or if it does not improve in 12 to 24 hours (Mayo Clinic). Any newborn with symptoms needs a doctor right away.
  • A painful red eye after an injury is not simple pink eye. Treat it as an injury.

Dental Emergencies

Knocked-out permanent tooth

This is a true dental emergency. A tooth put back within minutes has the best chance, and the odds fall the longer it stays out. A dental training source (Smiles for Life) says re-planting within about five minutes gives the best result. The Ontario Dental Association says a tooth put back within 10 minutes has a fair chance, and after an hour the chances are poor. Mayo Clinic says a late re-plant is unlikely to work. Do it right away if you can.

  1. Find the tooth. Pick it up by the crown (the white chewing part). Do not touch the root.
  2. If it is dirty, rinse it very briefly. Smiles for Life says to use saline or milk. Mayo Clinic says to use milk or the person's saliva and to avoid tap water. Do not scrub it.
  3. Push it gently back into the socket, the right way around. If it will not seat fully, have the person bite gently on gauze or a clean damp cloth to hold it.
  4. If you cannot put it back, keep it wet. Best: a small container of cold milk (Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic, Ontario Dental Association). Next: the person's saliva (Mayo Clinic). If you have neither, Cleveland Clinic suggests water with a pinch of table salt, or a tooth-saving kit if you carry one. Never let it dry out, and do not wrap it in tissue or cloth.
  5. Get to a dentist or emergency room as soon as you can.

If bleeding from the socket is heavy, press a clean gauze pad and have the person bite on it (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

The temporary fixes below come from dental association and hospital patient pages (Cleveland Clinic, Ontario Dental Association). They buy time. They do not repair the tooth, so see a dentist as soon as you can.

Lost filling or crown

  • Lost filling: press a softened piece of sugarless gum into the hole, or use over-the-counter dental cement. Sugary gum causes pain (Cleveland Clinic).
  • Loose or lost crown: keep it. To hold it in place for now, coat the inside with over-the-counter dental cement, toothpaste, or denture adhesive and seat it. Never use super glue (Cleveland Clinic).

Broken, chipped, or cracked tooth

  1. Save any broken pieces. The Ontario Dental Association says keep them in cold milk.
  2. Rinse the mouth with warm water.
  3. If it bleeds, press gauze on the spot for about 10 minutes.
  4. Hold a cold compress on the cheek or lip to ease pain and swelling.
  5. Cover a sharp edge with orthodontic (dental) wax so it does not cut the tongue (Cleveland Clinic).

Toothache

  • Rinse with warm water. Floss gently to remove trapped food.
  • For swelling, hold a cold compress on the outside of the cheek. Do not put heat on the jaw (Ontario Dental Association).
  • Over-the-counter pain relievers such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen may help; use as directed on the label.
  • Never put an aspirin or other pain pill against the gum or tooth. It can burn the gum and cause ulcers (Cleveland Clinic, MedlinePlus).

Clove oil: what the evidence says

Clove oil (its main chemical is eugenol) is an old toothache remedy. The evidence for it is thin. A drug-and-supplement reference based on the Natural Medicines database (RxList, a secondary source) rates it as having insufficient evidence for toothache, and says the U.S. FDA downgraded eugenol because there was not enough proof it works. The same reference warns:

  • Applying it to the mouth often and repeatedly can damage the gums, the tooth pulp, and skin.
  • Clove oil taken by mouth is likely unsafe for children. It can cause seizures, liver damage, and fluid problems. Keep it away from children.

At most, it may dull pain for a while. It does not treat infection, and it is no reason to delay care.

Tooth abscess

An abscess is a pocket of infection at the root of a tooth or in the gum. Signs include a severe, constant, throbbing toothache, pain when chewing, a bad taste or breath, a pimple-like bump on the gum, swollen glands in the neck, and fever (MedlinePlus). MedlinePlus calls a swollen jaw a very serious sign.

  1. Rinse with warm salt water several times a day: Cleveland Clinic suggests ½ teaspoon of table salt in 8 ounces (240 mL) of water. Spit it out.
  2. Use OTC pain relievers as directed on the label for pain and fever.
  3. Head for a dentist. Home care only eases symptoms. The infection needs drainage, a root canal, removal of the tooth, or antibiotics (MedlinePlus).

Evacuate now: spreading tooth infection

An untreated abscess can spread to the jaw, the blood, and other parts of the body (MedlinePlus). One deadly form, Ludwig angina, often starts from an infected tooth root and swells the floor of the mouth fast. Watch for swelling under the tongue or in the neck, trouble swallowing, drooling, a muffled "hot potato" voice, a tongue pushed up or out, or any trouble breathing. The swelling can block the airway. MedlinePlus calls breathing difficulty an emergency. Get emergency help at once; keep the person sitting up.

Urinary Tract Infections

A urinary tract infection (UTI) is most often a bacterial infection of the bladder. Women get them far more often than men. Risk factors include being sexually active, using a diaphragm or spermicide, pregnancy, menopause, diabetes, kidney stones, and having a recent catheter (Office on Women's Health).

Signs of a bladder infection

  • Pain or burning when urinating.
  • A strong urge to go often, with only a little coming out.
  • Pressure or pain in the lower belly.
  • Cloudy or strong-smelling urine; sometimes blood in the urine.
  • In older adults, a UTI may show up only as tiredness, shakiness, confusion, or weakness.

Signs of a kidney infection

Fever and back pain point to a kidney infection. The University of Michigan adds one-sided upper back pain with nausea and vomiting. It needs medical care. A UTI in pregnancy also needs care right away, because it more easily spreads to the kidneys.

What you can do in the field

  1. Drink plenty of safe fluids. The Office on Women's Health suggests six to eight glasses a day for prevention. The University of Michigan says about 2 liters (a little over 2 quarts).
  2. Empty the bladder when you need to. Do not hold it for hours; the Office on Women's Health says not to go longer than three to four hours.
  3. Wipe front to back, urinate before and after sex, change out of wet swimsuits, and avoid douching.
  4. OTC pain relievers such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen may ease discomfort; use as directed on the label.

A bladder infection that does not clear up with fluids generally needs a prescription antibiotic from a clinician. Plan your way out (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).

Get care now if

There is blood in the urine, fever or chills, back or side pain, nausea or vomiting, or confusion. Also act at once for a pregnant woman with any UTI symptoms. If you have had two UTIs in six months or three in a year, see a provider after the trip.

Skin Infections

Damp feet, sweaty clothes, and shared gear make skin infections common on long trips. For wound infection, see also Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection.

Fungal infections: athlete's foot and ringworm

Ringworm is a fungus, not a worm. It makes an itchy, scaly, often ring-shaped rash that looks red on light skin and gray or brown on darker skin. It spreads by skin contact with people or animals, and through shared towels, bedding, and shower floors (CDC). Athlete's foot is the same kind of fungus on the feet. It causes cracked, flaking, peeling skin between the toes, itching, burning, and sometimes oozing blisters (MedlinePlus).

  1. Wash the area with soap and water at least twice a day, and dry it well, especially between the toes.
  2. Use an over-the-counter antifungal cream or powder (such as clotrimazole, miconazole, terbinafine, or tolnaftate) as directed on the label.
  3. Keep using it for 1 to 2 weeks after the skin looks clear, so it does not come back (MedlinePlus).
  4. Change into clean, dry socks often. Air out boots and feet in camp.
  5. Do not share towels, socks, or bedding. Wear sandals in shared showers (CDC).

Do not

  • Do not put a steroid (hydrocortisone) cream on a rash that could be ringworm. The CDC says it can make the infection worse.

Get care if it is not better after 2 to 4 weeks of self-care, or if the foot becomes swollen and warm, with pus, red streaks, pain, or fever. Those signs suggest a bacterial infection on top (MedlinePlus). Scalp and nail infections often need prescription medicine (CDC).

Cellulitis and spreading redness

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the skin and the tissue just below it. The skin gets red, warm, swollen, tight, and tender, and the red area grows. The person may have fever, chills, and tiredness (MedlinePlus). Watch the edge.

  1. Trace the edge of the red area with a pen. Write the date and time next to the line. Clinicians use the same trick to see whether redness spreads past the mark (MedlinePlus).
  2. Check again every few hours, and at the same time every day.
  3. Rest, and raise the infected part above heart level to reduce swelling (MedlinePlus).
  4. If the redness crosses your line, the infection is winning. Plan to evacuate (Figure 8).

Cellulitis usually needs prescription antibiotics, so the field plan is to watch, rest, and move toward care. Red streaks, ongoing fever, drowsiness, blisters, a fast pulse, or confusion signal a spreading infection (MedlinePlus). Untreated, it can reach the blood (sepsis) or bone. Infection near the eye needs hospital care (MedlinePlus). Treat any of these as an emergency.

Boils and abscesses

An abscess is a pocket of pus under the skin. Do not push on it, squeeze it, or cut it open yourself (MedlinePlus). Warm, moist compresses can help it drain and heal (MedlinePlus). Get care if it comes with fever, spreading redness, or new symptoms, or if it is on the face.

Caring for a Sick Person in a Group

One sick camper can sicken the rest of the group. Basic habits stop most spread. The CDC and Mayo Clinic pages on pink eye stress hand washing and not sharing towels or eye products. The same logic works for stomach illness, colds, and skin infections.

  • Pick one caregiver. Fewer people touching the patient means fewer people exposed. The caregiver should rest too.
  • Wash hands with soap and clean water after helping the patient, after using the toilet, and before cooking or eating. (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation).
  • Separate dishes, cups, and towels for the sick person. Someone with diarrhea or vomiting should never cook for others.
  • Handle waste carefully. Bury stool and vomit well away from water and camp (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation). Wear gloves or a plastic bag over your hand for soiled cloths.

These are this guide's practical rules, built on the hygiene advice above. No single wilderness guideline for camp isolation was found.

Keep a log

When help is days away, a written log shows trends. Every few hours, note the time, temperature, pulse, breathing, fluids drunk, urine, stools, vomiting, pain, and mental state. Use it to decide when to leave (see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care).

Leave for care when

  • Confusion, fainting, or trouble breathing. An adult breathing more than 25 times a minute at rest.
  • Large, milky "rice-water" stools with fast dehydration.
  • A tooth infection with jaw, neck, or under-tongue swelling, or trouble swallowing.
  • Stiff neck with fever, or a new rash with fever.
  • Blood in stool or urine, or fever with diarrhea.
  • A child or baby who will not drink, or passes no urine for many hours.
  • Redness spreading past your pen line, or red streaks.
  • Any chemical eye injury, a cut or punctured eye, or vision loss.
  • The person is getting worse, not better.
