Signaling, Rescue & Communication

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 11, 2026

Most people who are rescued are found because someone knew where they were going, they stayed visible, and they made noise or light at the right time. This chapter shows you how to be found: what to do before you leave, whether to stay or move, how to signal by sight, sound, and radio, how to work with a rescue helicopter, and what to say when help answers.

Being Found: Plan, Stay, Be Seen

Leave a trip plan

The best rescue tool costs nothing. Tell someone who is not on the trip where you are going, what route you will take, who is with you, what vehicle you drive, and when you will be back. The National Park Service asks hikers to leave a written trip plan with a trusted contact. The Forest Service adds that you should list your gear and expected weather. Tell your contact when to call for help, and check in when you return so no one starts a search for nothing.

Stay put or move?

If you are lost and cannot retrace your steps, stay where you are. The National Park Service says moving makes you harder to find. Move only if staying puts your life in immediate danger, such as a flood, rockfall, or lightning. The Forest Service also advises that if backtracking is not practical, you should remain in place. The American Hiking Society allows one exception: you may leave to find water, but you must return to the same spot.

If you are only a little off the trail and know which way it is, you can carefully backtrack using your map, compass, and trail markers. If you split up, send at least two people for help, never one. Mark your camp so it can be seen from the air.

  1. Stop walking. Sit down and take a few minutes to calm down.
  2. If you have a signal, call 911 or send a message (see "Phones" below).
  3. Get warm and dry first. Shelter and clothing matter more than food.
  4. Make your spot easy to see and hear (rest of this chapter).
  5. Use signals in groups of three, with rests in between, to save energy and batteries.

Children: "Hug a Tree"

The Hug-A-Tree and Survive program, used by many search-and-rescue teams, teaches children four rules. Teach them before the trip.

  1. Tell an adult where you are going, who is with you, and when you will be back.
  2. If you are lost, stop and stay by one landmark such as a tree or large rock. Hug it if it helps you stay calm.
  3. Stay warm and dry. Cold is the main danger to a lost child.
  4. Help searchers find you. Blow a whistle, make noise, and leave clues such as footprints.

Tell children that no one will be angry that they got lost. Give each child a whistle on a cord and show them how to use it.

Make yourself big and visible

A single person on the ground is very hard to see from the air. Move to an open spot such as a clearing, ridge, or beach if you can do it safely. Spread bright gear. Orange, red, yellow, and blue contrast with most land. A tarp or space blanket on the ground or tied to a tree adds shine and color. If you need help and an aircraft comes near, stand in the open and give a big, clear signal, such as both arms raised in a Y (see "Body signals" below). Do not wave at a search aircraft just to say hello. Rescue teams warn that crews can mistake a friendly wave for a call for help.

Warning

Do not build a signal fire during high fire danger or in wildland fire season. The National Park Service tells lost hikers to skip fire in those conditions. Use mirrors, panels, and noise instead.

Visual Signals

Signal mirror

A glass signal mirror is one of the best daytime signals. Colorado Parks and Wildlife says a good glass mirror can be seen from 20 to 25 miles (32 to 40 km) in bright sun, and that metal mirrors are much weaker. Practice before you need it. A US Army aviation survival course teaches the finger-V method and says it works with any mirror.

  1. Hold the mirror just under your eye, with the shiny side facing the target.
  2. Stretch your other arm toward the target. Make a V with two spread fingers.
  3. Line up your hand so the aircraft or rescuer sits in the gap of the V.
  4. Tilt the mirror until the bright spot of reflected light lands on your fingers.
  5. Adjust until most of the light passes through the V. A little light on your fingertips tells you that you are still on target.
  6. Rock the mirror slowly so the beam sweeps across the target and back.

The flash is easiest to aim when the sun is in front of you or off to one side. If your mirror has a sighting hole, the same Army course says to hold it 3 to 6 inches (8 to 15 cm) from your face, find the target through the hole, and tilt the mirror until the spot of sunlight you see on the back of the mirror lines up with the hole. If you cannot see the aircraft, flash toward the sound of its engine. Do not flash fast or hold the beam on a cockpit for more than a few seconds. The US Army manual warns pilots may mistake rapid flashes for hostile fire, and the beam can dazzle them. It also says pilots have seen mirror flashes up to 100 miles (160 km) away in ideal conditions.

Fire and smoke

Smoke works by day. Fire works at night. Use each when it will show best.

  • White smoke shows against dark ground, such as forest. Smother a fire with green leaves, moss, or a little water.
  • Dark smoke shows against snow, sand, or light ground. The Army manual says rubber or oil-soaked rags make black smoke.
  • Wind, rain, and snow break up smoke. Choose calm, clear moments, and light the signal when you hear or see an aircraft.
  • At night a bright, open flame is the signal. Keep dry tinder and wood ready under cover so you can light it fast.

Three fires or three smoke columns in a triangle or a line is a recognized distress signal. Colorado Parks and Wildlife suggests spacing the fires 30 to 50 feet (9 to 15 m) apart. The Army manual gives about 82 feet (25 m) when you have room. Sources differ, so use the spacing you can safely control. If you are alone and short on fuel, one good fire is acceptable.

Ground-to-air symbols

Aviation authorities share a simple code for survivors. Lay large shapes in an open area. The FAA says symbols should be at least 10 feet (3 m) high and laid out exactly as shown. The Army manual gives each symbol as 1 meter (3.3 ft) wide and 6 meters (20 ft) long, and says to keep that 1:6 shape if you go bigger. Make them as large and as dark or bright against the ground as you can.

SymbolMeaning
VRequire assistance
XRequire medical assistance
NNo or negative
YYes or affirmative
ArrowProceeding in this direction

Tip

Use X only for a true medical emergency. If you are not sure which to use, make the international SOS shape instead. On snow, stamp or shovel the shapes so shadows make them dark from above. Use rocks, branches, gear, or cloth on bare ground.

Sources word the arrow differently. The FAA and the international (ICAO) code list it as "proceeding in this direction," meaning you have left that way. The Army manual's web text and a state wildlife agency say "proceed in this direction," which a pilot could read as an instruction. Follow the FAA meaning: make the arrow only if you leave, add a written note at camp, and understand that moving makes you harder to find.

Lights

A flashlight, headlamp, or strobe is a night signal. Flash the SOS pattern or flash in groups of three, and use it sparingly. The National Park Service reminds you that it drains the battery. A headlamp strobe on a high point can be seen from far away. Do not shine a bright light directly at an aircraft cockpit.

Sound Signals: Whistle and SOS

A whistle carries farther than a shouting voice and takes much less energy. The National Park Service says to blow three times, pause, then repeat. The Forest Service lists three whistle blasts, three shouts, three smokes, or three light flashes as the standard call for help. Keep the whistle on your pack strap or on a cord around your neck, not at the bottom of your pack.

SOS is a Morse code pattern: three short, three long, three short. The letters are S (three dots), O (three dashes), and S (three dots). You can use it with a whistle, flashlight, mirror, or tapping. Rest between groups.

Searchers cannot hear every whistle. Blow when you hear voices, engines, or aircraft, and listen between blasts.

Electronic Signals

Personal locator beacons (PLBs)

A PLB sends a coded distress signal on 406 MHz to a satellite system. NOAA says PLBs are made for people in remote areas, must be turned on by hand, and have one job: alerting search and rescue. The beacon battery runs at least 24 hours once on. Some models include GPS and can narrow your position to about 100 meters. A beacon needs a clear view of the sky, so step into the open before you activate it and keep the antenna up.

Legal note

In the United States you must register a 406 MHz beacon with NOAA. Registration is free, and federal rules require it. You can register online at the NOAA beacon registration site or call 1-888-212-7283. NOAA asks you to confirm your details every two years. Register an emergency contact who knows your plans. Old 121.5 MHz beacons are not detected by satellites and should not be relied on.

If you set off a beacon by accident, call right away so rescuers can stand down. NOAA lists the US Air Force rescue center at 1-800-851-3051 for PLBs, open 24 hours. Have your beacon's ID number (Hex ID) ready. Do not assume a confirmation light means help is already on the way. For beacons with a return link, the light means only that your alert was received and passed along.

Satellite messengers

Satellite messengers can send an SOS and also text messages, share your location, and receive replies. Most need a paid subscription. A PLB has no plan fee. According to REI, a messenger can lose its link under dense forest, in a narrow canyon, or behind rock walls, and a PLB also needs sky view. Two-way messaging lets you tell rescuers what you need, which is its biggest benefit.

Satellite SOS on phones

Some newer smartphones can contact emergency services by satellite when there is no cell or Wi-Fi signal. Apple says Emergency SOS via satellite works on iPhone 14 and later in supported countries. You must be outside with a clear view of the sky and horizon. Try a normal emergency call first, then use the satellite option if the call fails, and answer the on-screen questions. Check your own phone's support page and practice with its demo before the trip. Update the phone's software and fill in your Medical ID.

Phones: calls, texts, and battery

  • Call if you can. The FCC says voice calls to 911 are preferred. Text-to-911 is not available everywhere. A bounce-back message tells you to call another way.
  • FEMA says texts need less bandwidth than calls and often get through when networks are busy. So when a call drops, try a text to a friend or family member, and ask them to call 911 for you.
  • Turn on low power mode or airplane mode to save battery. Turn the phone on only to check for signal, then turn it off again.
  • For a serious injury with no signal, the National Park Service suggests flagging down another person and asking them to travel to a spot with signal and call 911.

Helicopter Rescue

A rescue helicopter is the most dangerous moment of a rescue for people on the ground. Follow the crew's directions over any advice here. The pilot decides whether it is safe to land.

Body signals

Mountain rescue teams teach two arm signals that copy the ground symbols Y and N. Both arms raised in a Y means "Yes, we need help." One arm angled up and the other angled down, like an N, means "No, we do not need help." The Slovenian Mountain Rescue Association calls the Y the international sign for needing help, and Teton County Search and Rescue in Wyoming teaches the same pair. This is a rescue-team convention, not an FAA rule. US military survival manuals use a longer chart of body signals. Stand in the open, face the aircraft, and hold the signal still and clear. If the crew tells you something different by radio or loudspeaker, follow the crew.

If a hazard appears and you cannot use a radio, an air-medical helicopter program says to cross your arms over your head again and again to wave the aircraft off. A rescue-helicopter pilot gives the same advice: big, sweeping Xs above your head. Keep your movements wide.

Landing zone basics

Landing-zone advice differs by aircraft and operator. Air-medical helicopter programs ask for a level area at least 100 by 100 feet (30 by 30 m). One accepts 75 by 75 feet (23 by 23 m) by day but wants the full 100 feet at night. A wilderness medicine handbook suggests about 60 feet (18 m) across, while a US military survival guide asks for a pickup area about 150 feet (46 m) across. Sources differ, so clear at least 100 by 100 feet, and more if you can, especially at night.

  • Surface: flat and firm, slope under 10 degrees, free of debris. Pick up or tie down anything loose. The aircraft's rotor wash can blow objects around hard.
  • Obstacles: look for power lines, poles, trees, and antennas near the zone and along the approach path. Tell the crew about any you see, using compass directions.
  • Marking: use bright markers anchored down so they cannot blow away. At night, use lights or flashlights placed inside cones at the corners, and never aim a bright light at the aircraft.
  • Wind: helicopters land and take off into the wind. Stand just upwind of the zone with your back to the wind and your arms stretched out in front of you, toward the landing spot. The helicopter will fly toward you. Avoid areas under ridges, where the wind shifts.
  • Distance: keep other people and vehicles at least 200 feet (60 m) from the touchdown point. No smoking, no flares.

Approaching a helicopter

  1. Do not move toward the aircraft while the rotors are turning, unless a crew member signals you to.
  2. When signaled, approach from the front, where the pilot can see you. Do not come from the rear or from uphill.
  3. Keep your head and body low. Keep tall objects, poles, and loose gear below head height.
  4. Walk, never run. If debris blows into your eyes, crouch where you are.
  5. Never go near the tail rotor. It spins fast and is hard to see.
  6. Let the crew load patients and gear. Follow their instructions exactly.

Do not

  • Do not run toward a landing helicopter.
  • Do not shine lights at the pilot or throw anything to attract attention.
  • Do not leave loose items such as tarps, hats, or foil where rotor wash can lift them.

Radios

Radios can reach help that a phone cannot, but they work only if someone is listening and in range. Know which radios require a license before you transmit.

Legal note

The FCC says FRS needs no individual license, but all 22 FRS channels are shared with GMRS, and a radio that exceeds FRS power limits or uses the GMRS-only channels needs a GMRS license. In a real emergency, call for help on any radio you have. For everyday use, get the proper license and follow the rules.

Marine MAYDAY on channel 16. The Coast Guard's procedure is: say "MAYDAY" three times, then "THIS IS," then your vessel name three times. Then give your position, the kind of trouble, how many people are aboard, and any other details. End with "OVER." Repeat until someone answers. Do not use "MAYDAY" for tests or radio checks. They are hoaxes and can lead to prosecution.

On any radio, speak slowly and clearly. Spell names and codes with the NATO alphabet below.

What to Say When You Reach Help

Stay calm, speak slowly, and do not hang up until the dispatcher says so. Give the facts in this order.

  1. Who: your name, your phone number, and the name of anyone with you.
  2. Where: GPS coordinates if your phone or GPS can show them. Also give the trailhead, your last known point, landmarks, and the direction you were heading.
  3. What happened: lost, injured, stranded, or in danger from weather. Say how long ago it happened.
  4. How many people: total in your group, and how many need help.
  5. Injuries: who is hurt, how badly, and what first aid you have given.
  6. Supplies and conditions: food, water, shelter, light, phone battery, and weather. Say what you are wearing, including colors.
  7. Your plan: say that you will stay where you are, and how you can be seen or heard.

Tip

Write the key facts on paper before you start, or have a partner read them. Stress makes people forget numbers. Ask the dispatcher to repeat the plan back to you.

Common Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Walking out is safer than waiting. Fact: Searchers start from your trip plan and your last known point. Moving away makes the search area larger. Stay put unless there is an immediate danger.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A phone with a good battery will always work in the backcountry. Fact: The Forest Service warns that phones likely will not work in remote areas. Carry a map, compass, whistle, and a way to signal that does not depend on coverage.

Do not

  • Do not use a PLB or SOS button for a minor problem. Use it when there is a real threat to life and you cannot get out safely.
  • Do not leave a fire unattended, especially in dry conditions.
  • Do not forget to tell your contact when you are safe.

Consider a search-and-rescue, wilderness first aid, or navigation class. Practice with your own mirror, whistle, and radio before a trip.
