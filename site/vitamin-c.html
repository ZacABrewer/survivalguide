Vitamin C & Scurvy

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 11, 2026
On this page

  4. Too Much Vitamin C
  5. Best Sources: Your Stockpile

  11. Sprouting for Winter Vitamin C

Your body cannot make or store much vitamin C, so you must eat it every few days. Without it, you slide toward scurvy over weeks to months. Grains, dry beans, and long-stored foods carry almost none, so a stockpile and a long winter can set you up for it. This chapter shows how much you need, how to spot trouble early, and how to get vitamin C from stores, gardens, wild plants, and sprouts.

What Vitamin C Does

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin. Your body needs it to build collagen, the "glue" in skin, gums, blood vessels, tendons, and bone. It also helps make certain brain chemicals and a compound that burns fat for energy. It works as an antioxidant, supports the immune system, and helps you absorb iron from plant foods (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements).

Without it, collagen turns weak. Gums bleed, small blood vessels leak, and wounds will not close.

How Much You Need

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) is the amount that covers nearly all healthy people. Babies have an Adequate Intake (AI) instead. Numbers below are from the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements.

Smokers need 35 mg more per day than nonsmokers. If you use tobacco, add that to the number in the table.

The RDA is generous. A study of Arctic diets (see Animal Foods below) cites about 10 mg a day as the least that prevents and cures scurvy in adult men, and 15 mg a day as enough on long sledge journeys. Plan for the full RDA anyway, because storage and cooking losses shrink your margin.

Scurvy: The Deficiency Disease

How fast it develops

NIH says scurvy can show up within about one month of eating less than roughly 10 mg a day. A clinical review (StatPearls) describes vague symptoms after about 4 to 12 weeks of low intake. Plan on weeks to a few months. Fatigue comes first, and it is easy to blame on cold or hard work.

Early signs

  • Tiredness, weakness, and a general "unwell" feeling
  • Red, swollen gums
  • Easy bruising
  • Small red or purple pinpoint spots on the skin, often around hair follicles on the lower legs

Later signs

  • Gums that bleed, swell, and pull away from loose teeth
  • Corkscrew or coiled body hairs
  • Big bruises and bleeding under the skin
  • Slow healing of cuts and wounds
  • Painful, swollen joints, and bleeding into joints
  • In the worst cases: swelling, jaundice, seizures, organ failure, and death

Untreated scurvy is fatal. Children can also get bone and joint disease.

Who is at risk in a survival situation

  • Anyone living mostly on stockpiled grains, rice, dry beans, pasta, and canned or dried food for more than a month or two
  • People stuck through a long winter with no fresh produce
  • Smokers, who need more
  • Infants fed cow's milk (including evaporated or boiled milk) instead of breast milk or infant formula
  • Older or isolated people who eat a narrow diet, such as tea and toast

How fast it reverses

Treatment works quickly. Based on the StatPearls review:

  • Fatigue, aches, and poor appetite improve within about 24 hours.
  • Bruising, gum bleeding, and weakness improve in 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Corkscrew hairs return to normal in about a month.
  • Most symptoms clear within about 3 months. Severe bone damage may need surgery.

Doctors usually treat adults with 500 to 1,000 mg a day for one to three months. Get medical advice if you can. If you cannot, restore real food sources of vitamin C right away and keep them in the diet.

Warning

Do not wait for gum bleeding. By then you have been short for weeks. Start fixing the diet as soon as fatigue and sore gums appear together on a low-produce diet. A child with bone or joint pain or bleeding gums needs a medical exam as soon as you can reach one.

A little history

Scurvy killed sailors for centuries, because ships carried grain, salt meat, and biscuit but no fresh produce. In 1747, Royal Navy surgeon James Lind tested six treatments on twelve sick sailors aboard HMS Salisbury: cider, an acid tonic called elixir of vitriol, vinegar, sea water, a medicinal paste made with garlic and mustard, and oranges and lemons. The two men given citrus recovered fastest. One was fit for duty in about six days, and the other soon helped nurse the rest. Vitamin C had not been discovered, but the trial showed that a food could cure the disease.

Too Much Vitamin C

The Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) is the most you can take daily from food, drinks, and supplements without expected harm. Per NIH:

AgeUL (mg/day)
Babies up to 12 monthsNot set (use only breast milk or formula and food)
1–3 years400
4–8 years650
9–13 years1,200
14–18 years1,800
Adults 19 and up2,000

Above the UL, the common side effects are diarrhea, nausea, and stomach cramps. Unabsorbed vitamin C causes them. Kidney stones are a concern mainly for people who already have a stone-forming problem. People with hereditary hemochromatosis (iron overload) should avoid high doses, because vitamin C increases iron absorption.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Megadoses of vitamin C make you immune to illness. Fact: Aim for the RDA, not the UL. Diarrhea from excess also wastes scarce water.

Best Sources: Your Stockpile

Dry staples give almost nothing. USDA lists raw lentils at 4.5 mg per 3.5 oz (100 g), and raw soybeans at 6.0 mg. Cooking cuts even that. You must add vitamin C on purpose.

What keeps it, and what loses it

  • Canned and dried fruit and vegetables: They lose vitamin C over time. A study of vacuum-sealed dried vegetables stored in the dark at room temperature found vitamin C dropped sharply over 12 months. It disappeared entirely in some dried onions and carrots.
  • Home-canned food: Kansas State Extension says to store jars cool (50–70°F, or 10–21°C), dark, and dry, and to use them within a year. Heat above 95°F (35°C) ruins quality fast.
  • Potatoes: They keep, but they lose vitamin C. A French study of Bintje potatoes stored in the dark at 54°F (12°C) found about 50% lost after 3 months, and about 66% after 6 to 8 months. Potatoes help, but they cannot be your only source in late winter.
  • Fortified foods: Some drink mixes and cereals add vitamin C. Check the label, and check the date.

Ascorbic acid powder and tablets

Plain ascorbic acid is the cheapest, most compact insurance. One adult's RDA of 90 mg for a year is about 33 grams (a little over an ounce). One manufacturer's data sheet (BASF) lists its crystalline ascorbic acid as stable for 36 months when kept dry, away from light, below 77°F (25°C), in a tightly sealed container, and away from metal. Treat that as a best case. Tablets and opened containers may not last as long.

  1. Buy plain ascorbic acid powder or tablets. Check the label for dose.
  2. Keep the container sealed, dry, dark, and cool.
  3. Do not store it in a metal tin. Use glass or plastic.
  4. Rotate stock. Replace it by the date on the label, and never keep it past three years.
  5. Give each person no more than the UL for their age. Keep powder out of children's reach.

Tip

Do not depend on one source. Combine powder or tablets, sprouting seeds, and garden crops.

Best Sources: Your Garden

The best garden sources are peppers, kale, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, and potatoes. Potatoes are lower per serving, but you can grow and store a lot of them.

  • Kale: USDA lists raw kale at 93.4 mg per 3.5 oz (100 g).
  • Peppers: Raw red bell pepper has 95 mg per half cup. Green has 60 mg.
  • Broccoli and Brussels sprouts: Cooked, a half cup has about 51 mg and 48 mg.
  • Cabbage: Raw cabbage has 36.6 mg per 3.5 oz (100 g). A caution on fermenting: USDA lists kimchi at 0.0 mg, so do not count on ferments as your main source.
  • Potatoes: A baked potato has about 17 mg. Cook them with the skin on (see "Losses" below).

Best Sources: Wild Foods

Warning

Never eat a wild plant unless you are certain of the species. Learn plants with a local expert before you need them. If someone eats something you cannot identify, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 (US) right away.

Rose Hips Rosa species

◐ P
Vitamin C
USDA lists wild rose hips at 426 mg per 3.5 oz (100 g), far above oranges. Your portion will be much smaller, because the seeds and hairs are removed.
Season
Fall. Cape Cod Cooperative Extension says to pick hips when they are bright red and fully ripe. Some kinds do not ripen until after a frost.
Prepare
Cut off the ends. Split the hip, and scrape out the seeds and the fine hairs around them, as the Extension recipes direct. Foragers report that the hairs irritate the mouth and throat. Eat the red flesh raw or simmer it into tea or sauce.

Heat destroys vitamin C, so use raw or lightly cooked hips when you can. Drying at low heat is another way to store them. The Extension dries hips in an oven that starts near 110°F (43°C) and rises slowly to 130–140°F (54–60°C).

Conifer Needle Tea

◐ P✖ D⚠ L

Many people steep needles from certain conifers (cone-bearing evergreens) to make a vitamin C tea. The needle tea may help, but I found no published figure for how much vitamin C a cup holds. Treat it as a bonus, not a main source.

Commonly named as usable
Eastern white pine (Pinus strobus), which has needles in bundles of five. WebMD also lists spruces and firs, but firs have flat needles that can be mistaken for yew.
Do not use
Yew (Taxus), ponderosa pine, lodgepole pine, and Norfolk Island pine. Also avoid Christmas trees, which may be sprayed.
Pregnant or nursing
Skip all needle tea.

The deadly look-alike: yew

Yew is a poison. NC State Extension rates it as highly toxic. The needles, bark, and seeds contain taxines, and eating them can cause vomiting, abnormal heart rhythms, breathing failure, and death. The National Capital Poison Center notes that adults have been poisoned by brewing tea from yew needles. Yew needles are flat and sit in two rows along the twig. Female plants bear a single seed partly wrapped in a red, cup-shaped aril. The seed is poisonous.

Firs also have flat needles. A Michigan Tech tree key notes that balsam fir needles have two white stripes underneath and rounded tips, while yew needles have no white lines underneath and sharp points. Use that as one check among several, never alone. If the tree is not clearly a pine with five needles per bundle, or a spruce or fir you know well, walk away.

Pregnancy cautions

USDA researchers report that ponderosa pine needles, green or dry, cause abortion in pregnant cattle. Lodgepole pine, common juniper, and Monterey cypress carry the same toxin. That research is in cattle, and the USDA page says nothing about people. No source I checked proves harm to pregnant women, but the Ho-Chunk Health and Wellness Division advises pregnant and nursing women to skip pine needle tea to be safe. Follow the cautious advice.

Norfolk Island pine is not a pine

The houseplant sold as Norfolk Island pine (Araucaria heterophylla) is not a true pine, according to Purdue Extension. It belongs to a different genus. The Ho-Chunk Health and Wellness Division lists it among trees not to use. Do not brew it.

Do not

  • Drink tea from any tree you cannot name with certainty.
  • Use yew, ponderosa pine, lodgepole pine, or Norfolk Island pine.

How to make it

  1. Confirm the tree with several features. Eastern white pine has five soft needles per bundle. If you are not certain, stop here.
  2. Rinse the needles. Cut off the brown base of each bundle.
  3. Chop them small. Discard any brown needles.
  4. Bring water to a boil, then take it off the heat.
  5. Add about 2 tablespoons of chopped needles per mug. Cover and steep at least 5 minutes.
  6. Strain and drink. Make a fresh batch each time, since heat and time wear down vitamin C.

Staghorn Sumac Rhus typhina

◐ P☐ S⚠ L

The Missouri Department of Conservation says the tart, hairy red berries are steeped into a lemony tea. I found no source with measured vitamin C values for sumac, so count it as a pleasant drink, not a proven fix. Sumacs belong to the cashew family, which includes poison ivy, and people who react strongly to poison ivy may react to the tea. Staghorn sumac bears red, velvety fruit in upright clusters at the branch tips (UMass Extension). True poison sumac (Toxicodendron vernix) is a different plant. It grows in swamps and bogs, and its berries start green, turn white, and droop. Never use a sumac with white or green berries, or one growing in a swamp.

Wild greens: amounts vary by species and season, and some look-alikes are deadly. I could not verify any specific wild green here, so learn local greens from a trained forager.

Best Sources: Animal Foods

Fresh meat has some vitamin C, but far less than fruit or greens, and it varies by animal and organ. A common claim says raw liver is rich in vitamin C. USDA data do not back that up for beef. Raw beef liver has only 1.3 mg per 3.5 oz (100 g). Do not count on beef liver. Liver from other animals varies (see the seal liver below).

Traditional Arctic diets held off scurvy with very little plant food. A summer study of Inuit hunters in Holman, Northwest Territories (published 1979), estimated that adults got at least 11 to 118 mg a day, with a mean of at least 30 mg. Women and children got less. Plants such as mountain sorrel and licorice root added some, but they were only a small part of the diet. Fresh animal foods did most of the work:

  • Muktuk (beluga whale skin with its blubber) added roughly 50 to 100 mg a day when available.
  • Ringed seal liver gave the most of the boiled foods tested: about 68 mg in a 1 lb (500 g) meal.
  • Broth from boiled meat, which people drank, added another 2 to 37 mg a day.
  • Meat and fish were eaten fresh-boiled or dried in summer, and often raw-frozen (char and caribou) in winter.

The same paper cites an older report that 28 of 30 Inuit children examined at Point Barrow, Alaska, had mild (subacute) scurvy, blamed on a lack of fresh meat.

The lesson: fresh animal foods, eaten with their organs and broth, help. Raw or undercooked wild meat has its own infection risks, so see Part 4 (Food Procurement) and Part 5 (The Kitchen) before you copy this.

How Food Loses Vitamin C

Vitamin C dissolves in water, breaks down with heat, and reacts with oxygen. Light, long storage, and metal speed the loss. To save more:

  • Eat produce raw when it is safe to do so.
  • Microwave or steam instead of boiling. NIH says steaming or microwaving may limit losses. A study of several vegetables found microwaving kept the most vitamin C and boiling the least, with retention ranging from 0% to 91%.
  • Use little water, and drink the cooking water. Vitamin C leaches into it. In the Arctic study, the broth from boiled meat added 2 to 37 mg a day, and people drank it.
  • Cook short and serve at once. In French potato tests, cooked potatoes held hot for an hour lost up to 70%.
  • Keep the skin on potatoes. Peeled potatoes lost about 40–45% more during cooking, unpeeled only about 7%.
  • Eat fresh produce first. Time is the enemy.

Sprouting for Winter Vitamin C

Dry seeds hold very little vitamin C. When they sprout, they make it. In a 2019 lab study, mung bean sprouts grown in constant light reached 78 times their starting vitamin C level by day three. USDA lists lentils at 4.5 mg per 100 g dry and 16.5 mg per 100 g sprouted. Mung bean sprouts have 13.2 mg per 100 g.

That amount is modest. A 3.5 oz (100 g) serving of raw lentil sprouts has a bit more than the 10–15 mg a day that older studies (cited in the Arctic paper) found enough to prevent scurvy, but it is well under the 90 mg RDA. Sprouts are a floor, not a full plan.

Warning

Sprouts are a real food-safety hazard. Salmonella, Listeria, and E. coli can grow fast in warm, wet sprouting conditions. The FDA says children, older adults, pregnant women, and people with weak immune systems should not eat raw or lightly cooked sprouts of any kind. Everyone else should cook sprouts thoroughly too, because cooking kills the bacteria. Stir-fried mung sprouts still show 16.0 mg per 100 g in USDA data.

How to sprout in a jar

  1. Buy seeds sold for sprouting, not seeds for planting. Planting seed is often treated with chemicals. Use seed that is not old or moldy.
  2. Scrub a wide-mouth quart jar. UNL Extension suggests sanitizing it for 5 minutes in a bleach solution (¾ cup unscented bleach per gallon of water), then rinsing and drying it.
  3. Put in ½ to 1 inch (1–2.5 cm) of seeds. Lentils and mung beans work well. Cover with water and soak 12 to 16 hours. Skim off floaters.
  4. Drain well. Cover the jar with a mesh or cheesecloth lid.
  5. Rinse with cool water 2 to 4 times a day, draining all water each time. Never leave seeds standing in water.
  6. Lay the jar on its side in a dark place at 68–72°F (20–22°C).
  7. Sprouts are ready in about 3 to 6 days. Rinse off the hulls. Chill them at 40°F (4°C) or colder and eat within a few days.
  8. Cook before eating.

Light may help. The 2019 mung bean study found far more vitamin C in sprouts grown in constant light than in the dark, though a 1950 study found dark-grown mung sprouts made more. Sources differ. UNL suggests a few hours in a sunny window before eating, and warns that too much sun makes sprouts tough and bitter.

Sprout safety rules

  • Cook sprouts until hot throughout. Cooking is the most reliable way to kill the bacteria.
  • Do not trust "organic" seed to be safe. UConn says organic seed is just as likely to be contaminated.
  • Throw out any batch that smells musty or looks slimy or moldy. Wash and sanitize the jar before reuse.
  • Keep sprouts at 40°F (4°C) or colder once they are ready.

Do not

  • Sprout seeds from poisonous plants, or seeds that are old or moldy.
  • Eat raw or lightly cooked sprouts if you are pregnant, a young child, an older adult, or have a weak immune system.
