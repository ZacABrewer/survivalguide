Hygiene & Sanitation

Dirty hands, bad toilets, and filthy water cause serious illness in a long emergency. This chapter covers handwashing, waste, laundry, menstrual care, lice and scabies, camp disease control, a few homemade supplies, repellents with evidence ratings, and safe pest control. Most of it is cheap and simple. The hard part is doing it every day.

Handwashing

The CDC says to wash your hands at these times:

  • Before, during, and after you prepare food, and before and after you eat.
  • Before and after you care for someone with vomiting or diarrhea, or treat a wound.
  • After the toilet, diapers, coughing or sneezing, and touching animals, animal waste, or garbage.
  1. Wet your hands with clean water. If you have a tap, turn it off while you lather.
  2. Apply soap and lather the backs of your hands, between your fingers, and under your nails.
  3. Scrub for at least 20 seconds. Humming a short song twice is a good timer.
  4. Rinse well with clean, running water.
  5. Dry with a clean towel, or air dry.

No soap and water? The CDC says to use a hand sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol.

The tippy-tap

A tippy-tap is a hanging jug you tip with a foot stick, so you can wash under a thin stream without touching the jug. Builders adapt the design to what they have; Figure 2 and the steps below follow the CDC version.

The CDC's tippy-tap instructions use a plastic container with a handle and a cap, a bar of soap, a long and a short rope, a long and a short stick, a big nail, a candle, and pliers:

  1. Mark the outlet on the side of the container opposite the handle, about one finger's length below the top.
  2. Hold the nail with pliers and heat its point in the candle flame.
  3. Push the hot nail through the mark to make the outlet hole.
  4. Make a second hole in the top of the cap the same way.
  5. Thread one end of the long rope through the cap hole from the inside, and knot it inside the cap. Screw the cap on.
  6. Tie the other end of the long rope to one end of the long stick.
  7. Make a hole through the middle of the soap with the hot nail. Thread the short rope through it and tie it to the short stick, so the soap hangs close to the stick.
  8. Fill the container with treated water (see Part 2 › Water).
  9. Hang the container by its handle, high enough that the rope lifts the end of the long stick off the ground.

Hang it where people wash often, and use soap every time. A shared bar of soap on a string is normal. A construction guide from Watershed Management Group says a good lather kills germs on the bar, and it prefers air drying to a shared towel. Put one by every latrine and kitchen.

Human Waste: Cat Holes and Latrines

Feces carry the germs behind cholera, typhoid, and dehydrating diarrhea. Keep waste away from water, food, hands, and flies.

One person, short stay: the cat hole

Leave No Trace says to dig a hole 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) deep and about 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm) wide, with a small trowel. Put it at least 200 feet (about 60 m) from water, trails, and camp. That is about 70 to 80 adult paces. Cover it and disguise it when you finish.

Bury toilet paper in the hole or pack it out (in deserts, pack it out). Do not burn it. Pack out wipes, even "biodegradable" ones (Leave No Trace).

Groups or long stays: trench and pit latrines

A group or a long stay needs a shared latrine. The humanitarian emergency standard gives these targets:

  • Enough and close. At least 1 toilet per 20 people, no more than 50 m (about 165 ft) from the shelters, so people use it at night.
  • Far from water. The standard says at least 30 m (100 ft). This guide uses 200 ft (60 m), the larger Leave No Trace figure, so the book gives one number. Put the latrine downhill and downstream of wells and kitchens.
  • Dry below. Keep the pit bottom at least 1.5 m (5 ft) above the wet-season water table. Fissured rock and limestone need more.
  • Sealed. Keep out rain, runoff, animals, and insects. Clean it regularly to limit smell and flies.
  • Handwashing and a pad bin. Provide water and soap (ash is the standard's second choice) and a container for menstrual materials.

The sources I opened give no fixed pit depth. Dig no deeper than the dry-clearance rule allows. A trench works the same way: a narrow slot you cover as you go. When you leave a pit for good, cover it with soil and dig a new one at the same distances.

Warning

Never put a latrine uphill of your water source or where it floods. Flood water carries waste into wells and streams.

Trash and Wash Water

  • Pack out trash. Leave No Trace says to carry out all litter and leftover food, including grease, and not to rely on burning or burying it. In a fixed camp, keep trash in a lidded container away from the kitchen and empty it often.
  • Strain dish water. Carry water 200 feet (60 m) from streams and lakes before washing dishes. Strain it through fine mesh, pack out the bits, and scatter the rest.
  • Wash yourself away from water. Wash 200 feet (60 m) from shorelines with a little biodegradable soap, rinsing from a pot or jug (Leave No Trace).
  • Greywater. Wash water from dishes, laundry, and bathing is greywater. Keep it out of your drinking supply, and drain it into a gravel pit like the one in Figure 2 so it does not pool.

Laundry Without a Machine

Heat matters most when clothes carry lice, scabies mites, or bed bugs. The temperatures below come from CDC and extension sources.

  1. Fill a tub with water as hot as you can safely handle. Do not wash in a stream you drink from.
  2. Add soap. Scrub and squeeze the cloth for several minutes.
  3. Rinse until the water runs clear, and wring it out.
  4. Hang it in the sun until fully dry.
ProblemWash and dryIf you cannot wash it
Head liceHot water, about 130°F (54°C), then high-heat dry (CDC)Seal in a plastic bag for 2 weeks
ScabiesWater above 122°F (50°C) for 10 minutes, then hot dry (CDC)Seal in a bag at least 72 hours, up to a week (CDC)
Bed bugsHot dryer on high for 30 minutes (EPA, Texas A&M). Washing alone might not do it.Bag and freeze at 0°F (-18°C) for 8 to 10 hours (Texas A&M)

Without a dryer, wash as hot as you can and use the bagging or freezing options. No source I opened gives a sun-drying time that kills bugs, so do not count on sun alone.

Menstrual Care and Diapers

Periods do not stop in an emergency. A reusable pad costs little and can be washed at camp.

Care for cloth pads

An IFRC flyer for reusable pads gives these steps:

  1. Wash a new pad with soap and dry it in the sun before first use. This helps it absorb.
  2. Change the pad at least 3 times a day. If you cannot wash a used one right away, keep it in a pouch.
  3. Soak it in cold water for 15 minutes to loosen the blood.
  4. Wash with clean water and soap, then rinse with clean water.
  5. Dry in the sun. Use only pads that are washed and fully dry. Damp pads can cause itching or infection.
  6. Wash your hands with soap and water after you change a pad.

Disposable pads, tampons, and cups

Leave No Trace says to pack out used pads and tampons, not bury them, because animals dig them up. Cup waste can go in a cat hole 200 feet (60 m) from water, trails, and camp.

Diapers

Safe diaper changing reduces illness for babies and caregivers (CDC). Empty solid waste into the latrine, never a stream, and wash your hands. Wash cloth diapers away from drinking water, like the pads above. I found no source with cloth-diaper wash temperatures.

Lice, Scabies, and Bed Bugs

These pests spread where people share close quarters. Use treatments as directed on the label, and see a doctor when the notes say so.

Head lice

The CDC says head lice spread mostly by head-to-head contact. They crawl and cannot hop or fly. Shared hats, combs, and bedding are a less common route. Lice do not spread disease.

  1. Confirm a live louse. Nits more than a quarter inch (about 6 mm) from the scalp are usually old and need no treatment.
  2. Use an over-the-counter product as directed on the label. The CDC lists permethrin 1% lotion (from age 2 months) and pyrethrins with piperonyl butoxide (from age 2). People allergic to chrysanthemums or ragweed should avoid pyrethrins.
  3. Do not use extra medicine, mix products, or use conditioner first. Flush your eyes with water if any gets in.
  4. Comb with a fine-toothed nit comb 8 to 12 hours after treatment, then every 2 to 3 days for 2 to 3 weeks.
  5. Treat again only when the label says. The CDC notes pyrethrins need a second treatment 9 to 10 days after the first, and permethrin often about day 9.
  6. Wash used clothes, towels, and bedding in hot water (about 130°F, 54°C) and dry on high. Soak combs in 130°F water for 5 to 10 minutes. Seal unwashable items in a bag for 2 weeks.

Do not

  • Use fumigant sprays or foggers. The CDC says they are not needed and can be toxic.
  • Put fuels such as gasoline or kerosene on the scalp. I found no source for them, and they burn and poison.

See a provider if you are unsure it is lice, if live lice remain after a full course, or if they persist after 2 to 3 treatments with the same medicine. That may mean misuse or resistance. Extensive housecleaning is not needed. Vacuum where the person sat or lay (CDC).

Scabies

Scabies is a skin infestation by tiny mites. It causes intense itching and a rash. The CDC says treatment is by prescription: a scabicide cream, lotion, or pill. Over-the-counter products are not tested or approved for scabies and do not treat it.

  • Wash bedding, clothing, and towels in water above 122°F (50°C) for at least 10 minutes, then dry hot. Seal unwashable items in a plastic bag for at least 72 hours, up to a week. Mites generally die within 2 to 3 days off human skin.
  • Avoid skin-to-skin contact until treatment is done. Anyone with skin contact in the past two months should get medical attention.
  • Itching can last weeks after the mites die. See a provider if it lasts 2 to 4 weeks after treatment, new burrows or rashes appear, or sores or infection develop. For infants, use only what a pediatrician recommends.

Far from a doctor, isolate bedding and heat-wash or bag clothes to cut the spread. Treatment needs a prescription, so get to care soon (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).

Bed bugs

The CDC says bed bugs are not known to spread disease, but bites itch and cost sleep. Look for rusty blood spots on sheets, shed skins, and bugs in mattress seams. They stay within about 8 feet (2.4 m) of where people sleep.

  1. Bag the bedding. Wash it hot and dry it on high for at least 30 minutes (EPA, Texas A&M).
  2. Vacuum the mattress, frame, and floor edges. Seal the vacuum bag in plastic and put it in an outside bin. Vacuuming misses eggs.
  3. Cover the mattress and box spring with a bed-bug-proof encasement. It contains the problem but does not cure it.
  4. Keep bedding off the floor, pull the bed at least 6 inches (15 cm) from the wall, remove cardboard and clutter, and caulk baseboard gaps (EPA).
  5. For a heavy infestation, call a professional. Texas A&M calls this the best solution. Renters should tell the property manager.

Do not

  • Use bug bombs. Texas A&M calls them mostly ineffective.
  • Use homemade sprays, or spray liquids into electrical outlets.

Preventing Waterborne and Fecal-Oral Disease in Camp

"Fecal-oral" means germs from feces reach someone's mouth through water, food, hands, or flies. Block each route.

RouteHow to block it
WaterTreat all drinking water (Part 2 › Water). Keep latrines 200 ft (60 m) from water and downhill. Wash diapers and clothes away from drinking sources.
HandsWash after the toilet and diapers, after tending the sick, and before food. Put a station at each latrine and kitchen.
FoodCook thoroughly and keep it covered (Part 5 › Food Safety).
FliesCover and clean latrines. Keep trash lidded.
Sick peopleGive them their own bucket or latrine spot and one caregiver. See Part 7 › Illness in the Field for rehydration.

Diarrhea that lasts, has blood, or comes with fever, confusion, or very little urine is serious. Seek care (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies for dehydration).

Personal Care From Scratch

Store-bought supplies run out, but some can be made. I give recipes only where I opened a source. Where I found none, I say so.

Soap

Soap is fat plus lye (sodium hydroxide). Lye is the dangerous part.

Lye can burn your skin and blind you

NIOSH lists sodium hydroxide as corrosive. It burns eyes and skin and irritates the airways. In contact with water it can heat enough to ignite combustible material. A soap supplier (BrambleBerry) warns of severe chemical burns and permanent eye damage. Skip this section if you cannot follow every rule.

  • Wear chemical-resistant gloves, goggles or a face shield, long sleeves and pants, and closed shoes until cleanup is done.
  • Work in good airflow. Do not lean over the mix.
  • Always add lye to water. Never add water to lye. It can erupt.
  • Use stainless steel, heat-safe glass, or heavy plastic. Never aluminum or tin.
  • Lye in water can reach about 200°F (93°C). Let it cool before you add it to the oils.
  • Keep children and pets out. Store lye in its labeled original container, locked away.
  • Skin or eyes: flush right away with lots of water for at least 15 minutes, and take off contaminated clothing (MedlinePlus, NIOSH). Use plain water; do not waste time looking for vinegar or another "neutralizer."
  • Swallowed: do not make the person vomit. If they can swallow and are alert and not vomiting or seizing, give water or milk right away (MedlinePlus).
  • Breathed in: move to fresh air. For any lye exposure, get medical help right away. In the US, Poison Help is 1-800-222-1222.

A Healthline guide gives the cold-process method: warm the oils to about 115°F (46°C); add lye slowly to room-temperature water and cool it to about 115°F; combine and stir until thick; pour into molds; rest about 48 hours; cut; cure about 6 weeks. Use a lye calculator for your oils. Crumbly soap hints at too much lye. This is a secondary source, since I found no extension guide. If you have stored soap, use that instead. I found no verified shampoo recipe, so I give none.

Teeth

A chew stick, such as the miswak from Salvadora persica, is a twig chewed until the end frays into bristles. A systematic review of 8 studies concluded that miswak controlled plaque about as well as a toothbrush. It also claimed better gum results, but the studies varied widely, so treat that cautiously. Use a fresh twig from a plant you are sure of (see Part 4 › Foraging Principles), and do not share it. I found no authoritative source for tooth powder, so I give no recipe.

Deodorant and lip balm

Body odor comes from bacteria on damp skin, so soap, thorough drying, and clean clothes come first. I opened no authoritative source for homemade deodorant or lip balm, so I give no recipes.

Insect Repellents and Evidence

Mosquitoes and ticks carry serious diseases. The CDC recommends repellents with an EPA registration number. It lists six active ingredients: DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), para-menthane-diol (PMD), and 2-undecanone. The CDC says the effectiveness of non-registered repellents, including some natural ones, is unknown. In general, a higher concentration of the active ingredient lasts longer, but sweat, water, heat, and rubbing shorten protection (CDC Yellow Book). The grades in the table below are this guide's own summary of these sources.

OptionEvidence gradeNotes
DEETStrongCDC-listed. Called the most effective and widely used (America's Poison Centers). The CDC says protection peaks near 50% concentration; stronger adds little.
PicaridinStrongCDC-listed. Not recommended under age 2 (poison centers).
IR3535Strong to moderateCDC-listed. Weaker against Anopheles, so not advised in malaria areas.
Lemon eucalyptus or PMDModerateCDC-listed. Best plant-based option, but can cause skin allergy. Not for children under 3. "Pure" lemon eucalyptus essential oil is not the same product and is not recommended (CDC).
CitronellaWeakAbout 1 hour. Candles and incense do not cut bites enough.
Homemade essential-oil mixesUnprovenNot EPA-registered, so untested. Can irritate skin. Do not rely on them where bites carry serious disease.

Apply sunscreen first, then repellent (CDC). Keep repellent off cuts, irritated skin, and a child's hands, eyes, and mouth. Put it on your own hands first, then a child's face. Do not apply it under clothes. Never put permethrin products on skin. Permethrin-treated clothing stays protective after repeated washing (CDC). For ticks, see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Natural means safe and effective. Fact: Plant oils can irritate skin and trigger allergies, and most are untested.

Household Pests: Rodents, Flies, Cockroaches

The safe approach is the same for all pests: remove food, water, and shelter, block entry, trap, and use poison last.

Rodents

Mice fit through gaps about ¼ inch (6 mm) wide, and rats can gnaw through holes as small as ½ inch (13 mm). Plastic, wood, rubber, and foam do not stop them. Use metal, copper wool, or concrete. Steel wool works as a temporary plug but rusts (Missouri Extension, UC IPM).

  • Sanitation. Seal food, pet food, and grain in containers, keep trash lidded, and clear brush and clutter. Keep woodpiles and feeders away from the house. Sanitation alone rarely clears mice, but poor sanitation lets them thrive (UC IPM).
  • Snap traps. Missouri Extension and UC IPM call them cheap and effective. Set several along walls and where you saw droppings, about 10 ft (3 m) apart (UC IPM). Use a small dab of peanut butter. Check daily.
  • Poison. UC IPM says rodenticides are toxic to people, pets, and wildlife, and mice die inside walls. Trapping is often a safer first choice indoors.

Hantavirus-safe cleanup

Rodent urine, droppings, and nests can carry hantavirus. Stirring up dust spreads it. The CDC bleach mix is 1.5 cups (about 350 mL) of household bleach in 1 gallon (3.8 L) of water, which is 1 part bleach to 9 parts water. Make it fresh each time. An EPA-registered disinfectant labeled "disinfectant" also works. It is a cleaning mix, not a drinking-water dose (see Part 2 › Water).

  1. Open all doors and windows and leave the area for 30 minutes before you clean a closed building. Air out a vehicle for 20 minutes.
  2. Put on rubber or plastic gloves.
  3. Spray droppings and urine with the bleach mix or disinfectant until they are soaked, and let them sit 5 minutes (or the time on the disinfectant label).
  4. Wipe up with paper towels and put them in a covered trash can that is emptied often.
  5. Mop or wipe hard surfaces with disinfectant.
  6. Wash your gloved hands, take the gloves off, then wash your bare hands with soap and warm water.

Dead rodents and nests: wear gloves, spray the rodent, nest, and area, wait 5 minutes, then bag it all with used traps. Seal that bag inside a second bag and put it in a covered trash can. Wash contaminated bedding and clothing in hot water with detergent, then dry on high or in the sun (CDC).

Heavy infestations

For a heavy infestation, the CDC says to wear disposable coveralls, rubber boots or shoe covers, rubber, latex, or vinyl gloves, goggles, and a respirator with HEPA filters (a half-mask air-purifying respirator or a powered one). For heating and cooling ducts, the CDC says to hire a professional.

Flies

Flies breed in feces, garbage, and rotting food. Control starts with sanitation: covered latrines, lidded trash, covered food. The humanitarian standard names regular toilet cleaning to limit smell and flies. I found no source for fly traps or homemade sprays, so there are none here.

Cockroaches

UC IPM and UConn Extension agree on the order of work:

  1. Sanitize. Seal food, lid trash, fix leaks, and clean crumbs, especially behind appliances.
  2. Seal gaps around pipes, doors, and windows. Check bags, boxes, firewood, and laundry before bringing them in.
  3. Remove hiding places such as stacked paper and cardboard.
  4. Set sticky traps along walls and in cupboards to find them. Check daily.
  5. Use baits, which UC IPM says work better than sprays. UConn says boric acid bait in childproof stations works on German cockroaches. Follow the label and keep it from children and pets.

UC IPM advises against sprays and foggers. Call a professional for serious infestations.

Myths and Deadly Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Lice jump or fly from head to head. Fact: The CDC says they only crawl, and spread mostly by head-to-head contact.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Over-the-counter lotions cure scabies. Fact: The CDC says they do not. Scabies needs a prescription.

Do not

  • Dig a latrine within 200 ft (60 m) of water, or uphill of a well.
  • Add water to lye, or use anything but plain water to flush a lye burn.
  • Sweep or vacuum rodent droppings.
  • Use bug bombs or fumigant sprays.
  • Put permethrin clothing treatment on skin, or an OLE or PMD repellent on a child under 3.
  • Wear damp or unwashed cloth pads.

Take a first-aid course that covers infection and diarrheal illness (see Part 7 › Illness in the Field). Learn soap making from an experienced teacher.
