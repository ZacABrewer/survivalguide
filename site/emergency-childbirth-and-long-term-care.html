Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026
On this page

  3. Delivering the Baby: Hands Off, Hands Ready
  4. Newborn Care: Dry, Warm, Breathe
  5. The Cord and the Placenta
  6. Bleeding After Birth: First Aid

Most babies are born without trouble, and the best help you can give is calm, clean, and mostly hands-off. This chapter covers an emergency birth when no clinician is there, then the long work of nursing someone who cannot get out of bed, then how to support a person in mental crisis. A birth class, a first aid course, and Psychological First Aid training teach these skills far better than any page.

Call first, then follow this page

If anyone can reach emergency services, call before the birth, and keep the line open if the dispatcher will stay on (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming and Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). Everything below is for the time before help arrives, or when none can. Check the person's breathing and bleeding as in Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs.

Is the Baby Coming? Signs Birth Is Close

Labor has early signs and late signs. St John New Zealand lists cramps that come and go, fluid leaking from the vagina (the waters breaking), and an urge to push. These mean labor has started. These mean birth is close:

  • A strong, hard-to-resist urge to push, or to move her bowels. StatPearls lists this first.
  • Contractions about 2 minutes apart or closer (StatPearls).
  • The skin between the vagina and anus bulges and looks thin and shiny during a contraction, or the top of the baby's head can be seen. This last sign is called crowning. The WHO childbirth guide treats a bulging, thinning perineum or a visible head as the start of the pushing stage.

If you can see the head, birth is minutes away. If contractions are regular but she has no urge to push, you usually have more time (Ohio State). Time them from the start of one to the start of the next.

Do not

  • Do not tell her to push before she feels the urge. Ohio State warns that pushing too early can tear the cervix and cause serious bleeding.
  • Do not leave her alone. The WHO guide says never leave a woman alone in labor.

Prepare the Space and Supplies

Pick a clean, warm, quiet spot. The WHO guide suggests a room near 77°F (25°C). Put a waterproof sheet, plastic, or a pad under her, with towels on top. Wash your hands with soap and clean water, and wear gloves if you have them (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation). Have someone fetch the supplies while you stay with her (Figure 1).

Ohio State says sterile clamps work best, with floss as a stand-in, and scissors should be boiled first. Keep both wrapped in a clean cloth until you need them.

Delivering the Baby: Hands Off, Hands Ready

The mother's body does the work. Your job is to keep her safe, catch the baby, and stay calm. Let her choose her position. The WHO guide says she should be upright or comfortably supported, and StatPearls says not to keep her lying flat on her back, because that can cut blood flow to the womb. Good choices are semi-sitting, side-lying, squatting, or hands and knees.

  1. Tell her to pant or breathe steadily when she wants to push too hard. Let her push with the contractions as the body demands.
  2. When the head shows, keep one hand lightly on it. The WHO guide says to keep the head under gentle control as it advances. Put a clean pad or cloth in the other hand and support the skin between the vagina and anus (Figure 2).
  3. Never pull on the baby. Lamaze says the baby comes out with each contraction on its own.
  4. After the head is out, feel at the neck for the cord. If it is a loose loop, slide a finger under it and lift it over the baby's head, or let the baby slip through the loop (Lamaze, WHO).
  5. If the cord is tight and will not slip free, do not force it. Support the head and keep going. StatPearls and the WHO guide describe clamping and cutting a tight cord, but StatPearls calls that a last resort for trained people. Call for help.
  6. Wait for the head to turn sideways on its own. The WHO guide says this usually takes 1 to 2 minutes.
  7. Then ease the head gently downward so the upper shoulder slips out. Next lift the baby gently toward the mother's belly so the lower shoulder comes out (WHO). The rest of the body follows fast. The baby is slippery, so hold it firmly with both hands and a cloth.
  8. Note the time of birth.

If it goes slowly or wrong

The WHO guide says a pushing stage of 2 hours or more without steady progress needs urgent help. If the shoulders stay stuck after the head is out, the WHO guide says to call for help, lay her back with her knees pulled up to her chest, and have someone press down above her pubic bone. Never pull hard on the head. See "Danger signs" below for breech, cord prolapse, and seizures.

Newborn Care: Dry, Warm, Breathe

Place the baby on the mother's belly or chest right away. Lamaze and Ohio State agree on the next steps (Figure 3).

  1. Dry the baby hard with a clean, dry cloth, including the head. Wipe the face. Replace the wet cloth with a dry one.
  2. Wipe fluid from the mouth and nose with a cloth. StatPearls warns against vigorous suction of the throat. MSF says to use a bulb syringe only if a lot of fluid blocks the baby. Go in no more than 0.8 inch (2 cm) from the lips, for no more than 5 seconds.
  3. Check breathing while you dry. A healthy newborn breathes 40 to 60 times a minute (StatPearls), cries or breathes within seconds, and soon turns pink and moves.
  4. Put the baby skin to skin on the mother's bare chest, face to the side so the nose and mouth are clear. Cover both with a blanket and cover the baby's head with a hat (WHO guide, Lamaze).
  5. Offer the breast when the baby is ready. Give no water or other feeds (WHO guide).
  6. Check the baby's breathing and warmth (feel the feet) every 15 minutes for the first hour (WHO guide). Fast breathing, grunting, pulling in at the ribs, or cold feet means the baby needs a clinic.

If the baby is not breathing

Most babies need nothing more than drying. MSF says about 10% of newborns need help to start breathing. If the baby does not breathe or cry within about 5 seconds of drying, rub the back and the soles of the feet firmly, but not roughly. Lamaze and Ohio State say the same. MSF puts the next step in the first minute: if the baby still gasps or does not breathe, trained helpers give breaths with a bag and mask, 30 to 60 a minute, with the head in a neutral position and the chest rising with each breath.

That is a trained skill. Take a newborn-resuscitation course, such as Helping Babies Breathe, and an infant CPR class from the Red Cross or the American Heart Association. If you have not been trained, keep drying and stimulating, keep the baby warm, call for help, and follow the dispatcher. For CPR basics see Part 0 › Red Pages. I could not verify a lay "no equipment" newborn breathing method from a source I could open, so this page does not describe one.

The Cord and the Placenta

When to clamp

Do not rush. WHO lists delayed cord clamping, about 1 to 3 minutes after birth, as routine. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) advises waiting at least 30 to 60 seconds in a baby who is breathing well, and StatPearls allows up to 3 minutes. Hesperian's village-health guide says there is no rush: wait until you can no longer feel a pulse in the cord. In short: wait at least 1 minute, and the cord is fine to leave alone until it stops pulsing. The exception is a baby who needs resuscitation. The WHO guide says to cut the cord quickly then and move the baby to a firm, warm surface.

Do you need to cut it at all?

Often, no. Lamaze says you need not cut the cord if help will arrive within 20 to 30 minutes. Keep the baby and the placenta together, and the baby warm. Ohio State prefers that paramedics clamp and cut with sterile tools. Do not pull on the cord, because it is still attached to the placenta.

Tying and cutting when help is far away

  1. Wait until the cord is limp and no longer pulsing, and at least 1 minute has passed.
  2. Tie the first tie tightly around the cord about 1 inch (2 cm) from the baby's belly. Use a sterile clamp, or clean string or floss (Ohio State). Tie the second about 2 inches (5 cm) from the belly (WHO guide). Hesperian gives a similar rule of thumb: two fingers' width from the body, then two fingers' width farther out.
  3. Cut between the ties with boiled scissors or a new razor blade. Hesperian says boiling is best; if you cannot boil, pass the blade through a flame or wipe it with alcohol (Figure 4).
  4. If the stump oozes blood, add a second tie between the first tie and the skin (WHO guide).
  5. Leave the stump open to the air. The WHO guide says not to put anything on it or bandage it.

Where sources differ

The WHO guide gives 2 cm and 5 cm from the belly. StatPearls puts the first clamp about 4 inches (10 cm) out, and Ohio State leaves 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) on the baby. If you are unsure, tie farther from the belly. A long stump is harmless, and a cut that is too close is dangerous.

The placenta

The placenta usually comes out within 5 to 15 minutes, sometimes up to 30 (StatPearls). Signs it is coming: the cord gets longer, the belly feels firmer, and there is a gush of blood (Ohio State, StatPearls). Let her bear down and catch it in a bowl. Never pull on the cord (StatPearls, Lamaze). Rubbing her belly from the outside can help the womb tighten (Ohio State). Keep the placenta in a bag or bowl to show a clinician. If it has not come out within 30 minutes, call for care. The WHO guide treats a placenta that is still in after 1 hour as an emergency.

Bleeding After Birth: First Aid

Heavy bleeding after birth is the biggest threat to the mother. WHO says a womb that fails to tighten is the leading cause. These three actions help.

  1. Massage the womb. Find the top of the womb in the lower belly. A soft womb is the problem; a well-contracted one feels hard and round, and sits in the middle at or below the navel (WHO). WHO's rule of thumb: a firm womb feels like your forehead, a soft one like the tip of your nose. Rub it firmly in circles with your hand until it hardens. WHO says a soft womb needs massage and that massage also expels clots. Recheck it about every 15 minutes, and any time you see bleeding (Hesperian). If it softens again, massage again.
  2. Empty the bladder. A full bladder can stop the womb from tightening (WHO). Help her to pass urine as soon as she can.
  3. Let the baby nurse. Ohio State says nursing releases oxytocin, which helps the womb tighten. If the baby cannot latch, stroking the nipples by hand can help. WHO also calls for skin to skin contact and breastfeeding.

Watch for danger (Figure 5). Sources use different cutoffs, so use the lowest. The CDC says to get help if she soaks one pad or more in an hour, or passes clots larger than an egg. The WHO guide says soaking a pad in under 5 minutes, or a steady trickle, is heavy bleeding. Hesperian adds that a small gush when the placenta comes is normal, but any big gush, or a smaller flow that keeps going, is an emergency. Signs of shock include pale skin (check the eyelids and palms), a fast pulse, sweating, dizziness, fast breathing, and confusion (WHO). Eyeball estimates of blood loss run low, so trust her condition (WHO).

The first day and the weeks after

Keep mother and baby together, warm, and watched. In the first hour the WHO guide checks the mother every 15 minutes: is the womb hard and round, how much is she bleeding, and is she drinking and passing urine? The CDC also lists warning signs for the year after birth, including a fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, trouble breathing, chest pain, one swollen calf, and thoughts of harming yourself or the baby.

Caring for a Bedridden Person for Days or Weeks

When someone cannot get up, the first danger is the injury or illness. The second is lying still. Merck lists pressure sores, blood clots, constipation, muscle loss, stiff joints, and low mood as problems of long bed rest. Good nursing prevents most of them. Plan for shifts from the start (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming).

Skin and pressure sores

Pressure on skin against the bed cuts off blood flow, and skin and the tissue under it can start to die within a few hours (Merck). Sores form over bone: the tailbone, hips, heels, elbows, and ankles (Merck, MedlinePlus). Figure 6 shows the risk points.

Turn on a schedule

Merck says a bed-bound person should be turned at least every 2 hours, and a person in a chair repositioned every hour. MedlinePlus says to change position every 1 to 2 hours in bed. An older CDC-hosted nursing manual says hourly. Sources differ, so turn at least every 2 hours, and every hour if skin stays red or the person is very thin or frail. The 1-to-2-hour interval comes from the sources. The back, left, right order in Figure 7 is this guide's suggestion.

  • Lift and slide the person on a sheet. Dragging causes friction injury (Merck).
  • Keep the head of the bed as low as is safe. Raising it pulls the skin sideways as the person slides down (Merck). MedlinePlus says to keep it at or below 30 degrees. Raise it for meals, then lower it.
  • Use pillows or foam between the knees and ankles (Merck, MedlinePlus). On the back, float the heels with a pillow under the calves, not under the knees (MedlinePlus). Egg-crate foam does not work well and does not replace turning (Merck). Avoid donut-shaped cushions (MedlinePlus).
  • Check all pressure points once a day. Redness over a pressure point means: stay off that spot until the color returns to normal (Merck).
  • On dark skin, redness may not show. Feel for warmth, firmness, or tenderness (Merck).

A stage 2 or deeper sore needs a clean, dry dressing, no weight on it, and a clinician (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Hygiene

Keep skin clean and dry. Moisture raises the risk of sores (Merck). Wash with mild soap and water, pat dry rather than rub, and change wet sheets and clothes at once. A thick barrier cream can protect skin from urine and stool (Merck). Wash your hands before and after each task (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation).

Feeding and fluids

  • Protein and vitamins. Merck recommends a balanced, high-protein diet. Shortages of protein, vitamin C, or zinc slow skin healing (see Part 6 › Vitamin C & Scurvy).
  • Fluids. Offer drinks often. Merck says plenty of fluids prevent constipation. Dark or scant urine means more fluid is needed (see Part 7 › Illness in the Field for dehydration and oral rehydration).
  • Swallowing. Coughing or choking with food or drink, a wet voice, or food coming out of the nose are signs of swallowing trouble (Merck). Stop feeding. Aspiration can cause pneumonia. A person who cannot swallow safely may need a feeding tube, which is a medical decision (Merck). Do not force food or drink.
  • Position. Sit the person up as far as is comfortable for meals, with small bites and slow pace (Merck: small bites; head position should match a clinician's advice). Lower the bed afterward.

Toileting

Use a bedpan, urinal, or commode, and respect privacy. Sitting upright helps the bladder empty (older CDC-hosted manual). Incontinence raises sore risk (Merck), so clean and dry the skin each time. For constipation, Merck lists fluids, fiber, and movement. Record stools and urine on the log. Call for help for a hard, swollen belly, vomiting, blood in stool or urine, or no urine for a long stretch (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).

Preventing blood clots

Slow blood flow in still legs lets clots form. A leg clot can travel to the lungs and kill (Merck). The CDC says to get moving as soon as possible after being confined to bed. For a bed-bound person:

  • Do ankle pumps: flex and point the feet about 10 times every 30 minutes while awake. Merck gives this advice for long sitting, and this guide extends it to the bedridden.
  • Keep the legs raised when resting, and get the person up as soon as the injury allows (Merck).
  • Watch for a swollen, warm, painful, or red calf, thigh, or arm. About half of people with a clot have no symptoms (CDC). Get a medical check for any such swelling, and do not rub the leg hard until it has been checked. The "do not rub" part is this guide's caution. Sources say only to get it checked.

Clot in the lung: emergency

Sudden shortness of breath, chest pain that is worse with a deep breath, coughing blood, a racing heart, or fainting can mean a clot has reached the lungs. The CDC says to get medical help immediately.

Preventing pneumonia

Lying flat and still makes it hard to breathe deeply and clear the lungs. An older nursing manual hosted by the CDC says that sitting up in a chair improves breathing depth, and that prolonged flat or slumped positions restrict it. So get the person up in a chair if they can, and sit them up in bed often. Wash hands, keep sick visitors away, and keep up with vaccines (CDC). Those at higher risk include older, weak, or stroke-affected people and those with swallowing trouble (Merck). Watch for fever, a new cough, fast breathing, or confusion. Aspiration pneumonia can take a day or two to show, and the sputum may smell foul (Merck). Treat these as an infection emergency (see Part 7 › Illness in the Field).

Joints, muscles, and mind

Without use, muscles weaken and joints stiffen, and the stiffness can become permanent (Merck). Move each joint through its full range at least twice a day, helping when the person cannot (older CDC-hosted manual). Stop if it hurts. Long bed rest also lowers mood (Merck), so give company, talk, and reading, and let the person help decide things.

Seek help or evacuate for pressure sores that open, fever with new confusion, trouble breathing, signs of a clot, an inability to swallow, or no urine. See Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions.

Caregiver rotation and rest

Caregivers who do not rest fail, and the patient suffers. The CDC says: "To continue being a good caregiver, you need to take care of yourself." It advises regular breaks, and notes that even a few hours of respite a week can improve well-being.

  • Share the work in shifts, so that no one is the only caregiver. The night turns need a plan too, with a long unbroken sleep for each person on a rotation.
  • Ask for help in a specific form: a task or a block of time. The CDC suggests a shared calendar so helpers can sign up.
  • Write a handover note at each shift: last turn, last meal, last fluids, skin, mood, and anything new.
  • Eat, drink, wash, and move. Caregivers get sick too.
  • Talk to someone. If you feel hopeless or think of hurting yourself, see the next section.

Mental Health in Crisis

After a disaster, a lost partner, a birth gone wrong, or weeks of caring for someone sick, strong reactions are normal. Most people recover with time, safety, rest, and support. A few need more help quickly. This section covers how to help the first group and how to spot the second.

Psychological first aid: Look, Listen, Link

WHO describes psychological first aid (PFA) as humane, supportive, practical help for people in serious crisis. It is not counseling, and it needs no special degree. WHO, the War Trauma Foundation, and World Vision wrote the guide in 2011. Its action steps are Prepare, Look, Listen, and Link (Figure 8).

What to say and do

  • Pick a quiet place with few distractions, and stay near the person.
  • Be calm, honest, and patient. Use simple words. Let silence happen. Ask before touching.
  • Acknowledge their feelings, their losses, and their strengths.
  • Offer practical comfort, such as water or a blanket (all from the WHO guide).

Do not

  • Do not pressure anyone to tell their story, or interrupt and rush them.
  • Do not judge their feelings, or tell them how they should feel.
  • Do not make false promises or invent information.
  • Do not talk about your own troubles, or share anyone else's story.
  • Do not take over and solve everything for them (WHO guide, Catholic Relief Services summary).

Normal stress reactions, and when they are not

The U.S. National Center for PTSD lists common reactions after trauma:

  • Fear, sadness, numbness, anger, guilt, and withdrawing from others.
  • Poor sleep, upset stomach, racing heart, shaking, and fatigue.
  • Trouble concentrating or deciding, being jumpy, bad dreams, and avoiding reminders.

Most people recover without formal treatment, and reactions ease over time. The Red Cross says to seek professional help if such reactions last 2 weeks or longer, such as frequent crying or anger, trouble eating or sleeping, loss of interest, and hopelessness. The VA page adds anger that leads to violence, drinking or drug use to numb feelings, and any suicidal thought. Help any distressed person with the basics: a safe place, food, water, rest, and company. The Red Cross also says to limit news and social media exposure and to take things one small step at a time.

Children may cling, act out, or need extra reassurance, and some reactions show up weeks later (Red Cross). The WHO childbirth guide lists severe depression or suicidal thoughts after birth as a reason to get care. For grief after a death, see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming.

Signs someone needs urgent help

  • Talk of wanting to die, hopelessness, unbearable pain, or being a burden (CDC).
  • Withdrawal, isolation, or more alcohol or drug use (CDC).
  • Too distressed to eat, drink, or care for self or children (WHO).
  • Threats or plans to harm self or others (WHO).

If someone may be thinking of suicide

  1. Ask directly. NPR quotes a suicide-prevention psychologist advising a plain question: "Have you ever had thoughts of suicide?" Asking does not plant the idea.
  2. Find out how urgent it is. Ask whether they are thinking of killing themselves soon, and whether they have a plan or a way to do it. A specific plan means higher risk (NPR).
  3. Stay with them. Do not leave a person in crisis alone (WHO guide, NPR). Intense urges often ease within 24 to 48 hours, so getting through that stretch matters (University of Washington psychologist quoted by NPR).
  4. Remove the means. Ask what is nearby, and help lock it away or remove it. Make sure any firearm is not readily accessible (NPR). The CDC says that putting guns or medicines out of sight or reach is not secure storage. Lock them up, or move them out of the house.
  5. Listen without judging. People mostly want to be heard (NPR). Do not argue or lecture. Say that you care and that you will get through this together.
  6. Get help. Call or text 988 in the United States, or call 911 or the local emergency number if there is immediate danger. See below.
  7. Follow up. After the crisis, help them make a safety plan and see a mental health professional. Keep checking in.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Asking about suicide puts the idea in a person's head. Fact: NPR's experts say it does not. A calm, direct question shows you are willing to listen.

Crisis lines

988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (United States)
Call or text 988, or chat online at 988lifeline.org. Confidential, 24 hours a day, every day (FCC, CDC). For Spanish, call and press 2 or text AYUDA to 988. People who use American Sign Language can call 988 by videophone (FCC). It is also for someone worried about a loved one. Calls are routed by area code, not location, so you may reach a center in another state; that is normal (FCC).
Veterans
Call 988 and press 1, or text 838255 (FCC). Chat is at VeteransCrisisLine.net.
Crisis Text Line (United States)
Text HOME to 741741 (NPR).
Disaster Distress Helpline (United States)
Call or text 1-800-985-5990, free and 24/7 (Red Cross).
Immediate danger
Call 911 in the United States, or the local emergency number elsewhere.
Other countries
Lines differ by country, and 988 works only in the U.S. Before travel, look up the local crisis line and the emergency number, and write them down. Ask a local clinic or hospital. One reliable way to find a line in another country is Find A Helpline (findahelpline.com), a free directory run by ThroughLine whose listings are verified with the helplines and that covers more than 175 countries. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points people to it. Look up and write down the number before you need it, since directories need internet.

With no phone signal, you are the help. Stay, remove the means, keep company in shifts, and get the person to care as soon as you can (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication and Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions). Take care of yourself afterward. Helping in a crisis is heavy.
