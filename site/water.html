Water: Finding, Collecting, Purifying & Storing

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 11, 2026
On this page
  1. How Much Water You Need
  2. Finding Water by Terrain
  3. Collecting Water
  4. Purifying Water
  5. Improvised Filters
  6. Storing Water
  7. Myths and Deadly Mistakes
  8. Sources

Water comes before food, shelter tools, and almost everything else on your list. This chapter shows how much you need, where to look for it, how to collect it, how to make it safe to drink, and how to store it at home. Clear water can still make you sick, so the rule is simple: find it, clean it, then disinfect it.

How Much Water You Need

Your needs change with heat, work, altitude, illness, and body size. Use these numbers as a floor, not a target.

SituationRough needSource
Planning and storage, per personAt least 1 gallon (3.8 L) per day, for drinking and basic cleaningReady.gov
Hot climateAbout double the planning figureReady.gov
Average adult at 68°F (20°C), normal activity2 to 3 quarts/liters per dayUS Army FM 21-76
Hiking in desert heatAt least 1 gallon (3.8 L) per day, and do not ration itNational Park Service
Extreme, dry climateArmy manual lists 14 to 30 liters (about 4 to 8 gallons) per dayUS Army FM 21-76
Hourly pacing in desert heat0.5 liter (about 1 pint) per hour below 100°F (38°C); 1 liter per hour aboveUS Army FM 21-76

Children, nursing mothers, and sick people may need more than the planning figure. Check your urine: pale yellow is fine; dark yellow or very little means drink more.

Signs of dehydration

  • Mild to moderate: thirst, dry or sticky mouth, less urine, dark yellow urine, headache, and muscle cramps.
  • Severe: very little or no urine, confusion or irritability, dizziness, fast heartbeat and breathing, sunken eyes, and listlessness. Severe dehydration is a medical emergency. Call for emergency help if a person passes out, becomes confused, or has a seizure.

Do not overdrink

Drinking far more than you need can dilute the sodium in your blood. This is called hyponatremia. Early signs are bloating, headache, nausea, dizziness, and weakness. Severe cases bring confusion, collapse, and seizures. The Wilderness Medical Society advises drinking in response to thirst and having salty foods on hand during long exertion in heat. MedlinePlus warns not to take salt tablets for dehydration.

The Army manual instead calls thirst an unreliable guide and gives fixed hourly amounts. Use those only in extreme heat, and stop forcing fluids if you feel bloated or sick.

Save sweat, not water

Rest in shade, move at dawn and dusk, and keep your clothes on. Phoenix Fire Department advises sitting at least 12 inches (30 cm) off the hot ground, and not eating when you lack water, because digestion uses it.

Finding Water by Terrain

Any natural source can carry germs or chemicals, so treat everything you find (see "Purifying Water" below). Never use water tainted by fuel or toxic chemicals; no treatment makes it safe.

Desert and dry country

  • Dry washes. Water last flowed along the outside edge of a sharp bend. Phoenix Fire Department advises digging 3 to 6 feet (1 to 2 m) there; that depth is from one source, and digging costs sweat. If the sand turns wet, keep going.
  • Cliff bases and canyon walls. Phoenix Fire notes that water can sit at the base of rock cliffs. One desert-skills writer lists seep signs (dark streaks, alcoves, damp walls with plants) and potholes below steep drops that hold flood water.
  • Green plants. Lush green plants and trees such as cottonwoods often grow where water is near the surface.
  • Animal sign. The Army manual notes that converging game trails often lead to water, and that grain-eating birds such as finches and pigeons stay near it.

Do not camp in a wash. Flash floods can arrive from storms many miles away.

Forest and lowland

Water runs downhill. Check valley floors, low spots, and drainages. Neither streams nor ponds are safe without treatment.

Mountains

Snowmelt streams and springs are common, but animals and hikers can foul them upstream. Collect above any camp you can see, and treat it anyway.

Coast

Do not drink seawater. For a beach well, one desert-skills writer advises digging at the low spot behind the first line of dunes, stopping when water seeps in, and checking again after about an hour. Digging too deep can pull in salt water. If the water tastes salty, do not drink it. Boiling will not remove salt.

Arctic and cold regions

Frozen water is plentiful but must be melted first. Old sea ice looks bluish or blackish, shatters easily, and has rounded corners. It tastes much less salty. New sea ice is milky or gray, hard to break, and very salty. Do not drink water from new sea ice.

Tropics

Rain is your best source. Bamboo and vines can help too (see "Water from plants").

Urban and at home

SourceUse?
Water heater tank (the one that feeds your taps)Yes. Do not use the separate tank of a heating system.
Toilet tankOnly if the water is clear and has no cleaner tablets or colored chemicals. Never the bowl.
Ice cubesYes, if the water used was not contaminated.
Liquid from canned fruit and vegetablesYes.
Swimming pools and spasWashing and cleaning only. Not for drinking.

Follow local officials about your main water valve. They may tell you to shut it off to keep contaminated water out of your pipes.

Collecting Water

Rain

Rain is often the easiest source of large amounts. A preprint review (not yet peer reviewed) puts a typical rain catch at 5 to 10 liters per event, with under 15 minutes of setup. That beats nearly every other method in this chapter.

  1. Tie one corner of the tarp high. Tie the opposite corner low, with room for a container under it.
  2. Pull the tarp taut so water runs to the low corner and not along the edges.
  3. Let the first minutes of rain run away. This "first flush" carries the dust, droppings, and dirt that built up on the surface.
  4. Move the clean container under the low corner.
  5. Treat the water before you drink it.

The CDC warns that roof rainwater can carry germs, plus lead, copper, or asbestos from roofing and gutters. Roof water is better for washing than for drinking unless you treat it. Do not mix rainwater with water you know is safe.

Dew

At dawn, wipe grass and leaves with a clean cloth and wring it out. The Army manual notes that some people have gathered as much as a liter an hour this way. The preprint review reports far less, about 0.1 to 0.3 liter per day. Treat dew as a bonus.

Transpiration bag

Green plants release water vapor. A clear plastic bag tied over a leafy branch in full sun traps it.

The Army manual expects 0.5 to 1 liter per 24 hours from its stills, including a bag of cut leaves. The preprint review estimates 0.3 to 1 liter per bag. Use several bags. Military manuals warn that poisonous plants give poisonous water, so if you cannot name the plant, skip it. The water will taste like the plant.

Solar still

A solar still draws moisture from soil and plants under a plastic sheet. The Army manual says to expect about 0.5 to 1 liter in 24 hours, and that you would need at least three stills to meet one person's daily needs. The preprint review found lower yields, about 0.3 to 0.6 liter per day, and called stills marginal: useful only in hot, dry country with plastic sheeting and no other water.

  1. Dig a bowl-shaped hole about 3 feet (1 m) across and 2 feet (60 cm) deep, in sun, in damp soil if you can find it.
  2. Dig a small sump in the center and set a container upright in it.
  3. Run a drinking tube from the container up and out of the hole.
  4. Cover the hole with clear plastic. Weight the edges with soil to seal it.
  5. Put a rock in the middle of the sheet so it sags into a cone, pointing at the container.
  6. Add cut green plants to the hole to increase the yield. Use non-poisonous plants only.

Know the trade-off

Digging makes you sweat. In hard, hot ground you may lose more water than the still ever gives back. If you have a choice, move to shade first and use the water you already have.

Snow and ice

Do not eat snow or ice to stay hydrated. It chills your body and can injure your lips and mouth. Melt it first.

  • Start with a little water in the pot so the metal does not scorch, then add snow slowly.
  • Use ice if you can. The Army manual notes that a cup of ice yields more water than a cup of snow. One survival author claims twice the water for half the heat; treat that as a rough rule of thumb.
  • Get snow from a clean spot, away from where anyone urinates.

Melted snow still needs treatment if it may be dirty.

Water from plants

  • Green bamboo. The Army manual describes clear, odorless water. Bend a green stalk down, tie it, cut off the top, and let it drip overnight.
  • Water vines. Cut a notch high up, then cut the vine low and let it drip. Do not drink liquid that is sticky, milky, or bitter. Vine identification is hard. If you are not sure, leave it alone.
  • Plantain or banana stumps. The first few fillings taste bitter, and later ones are fine.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A cactus is a canteen with spines. Fact: Most cactus flesh holds acids and alkaloids that can cause vomiting and diarrhea. A few species, such as prickly pear and fishhook barrel cactus, have milder chemistry and may give some hydration in an emergency. Some look-alike plants have a milky sap that burns skin and can blind you. Cactus is a last resort.

Purifying Water

Even clear running water can hold germs and chemicals. The three germ groups are bacteria, viruses, and protozoa such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium. Each method below covers different ones.

If the water is cloudy, let it settle and strain it through a clean cloth or coffee filter first. Cloudy water weakens chemicals and blocks UV light.

Boiling

Boiling is the best field method. It kills viruses, bacteria, and protozoa, and it needs nothing but fire and a metal container.

  1. Strain cloudy water through a clean cloth first.
  2. Heat it until large bubbles roll across the whole surface.
  3. Keep it at a rolling boil for 1 minute. Above 5,000 ft (about 1,500 m), boil 3 minutes. If fuel allows, boil 3 minutes anywhere.
  4. Let it cool on its own. Keep it covered.

Sources differ. The CDC switches to 3 minutes above 6,500 ft; the EPA, above 5,000 ft (its page also lists 1,000 m, about 3,300 ft, which does not match). This chapter follows the EPA, the more cautious agency. The Army manual is stricter: add 1 minute per 1,000 ft (300 m), or boil 10 minutes anywhere.

Household bleach

Use plain, unscented liquid bleach labeled 6% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite. Avoid scented, color-safe, or "splash-less" kinds and anything with added cleaners. Use fresh bleach, or bleach stored at room temperature for under a year.

  1. Clarify and strain cloudy water first.
  2. Add the bleach from the table. Double the dose if the water is cloudy, colored, or very cold.
  3. Stir, then let it stand 30 minutes.
  4. Smell it. It should have a faint chlorine odor. If not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
  5. If it still has no chlorine smell, throw it out and find another source.

This is the EPA's table. Ready.gov gives 1/8 teaspoon per gallon for 5.25 to 6% bleach, and the CDC gives 8 drops per gallon for any bleach from 5% to 9%. Bleach works on bacteria and viruses but poorly on protozoa. If the source could hold Giardia or Cryptosporidium, filter first or boil instead.

Iodine

Iodine is a backup. The EPA lists 5 drops of 2% tincture of iodine per quart or liter, and 10 drops if the water is cloudy or colored, with a 30-minute wait. Follow the label on tablets. Do not use iodine if you are pregnant, have thyroid problems, or are allergic to iodine. Nobody should drink iodine-treated water for more than a few weeks. Iodine does not kill Cryptosporidium. Ready.gov does not recommend it, so use bleach or boiling when you can.

Chlorine dioxide tablets

These kill bacteria, viruses, and Giardia. They are the only chemical choice that reaches Cryptosporidium, and only slowly. The CDC rates them somewhat effective against it, says they need several hours of contact, and says to follow the label exactly. A Colorado State University Extension guide gives 30 minutes for most germs and 4 hours for Cryptosporidium. If that parasite is possible, wait the full time.

Filters

Pore size is what matters. The CDC lists these cutoffs:

  • 1 micron absolute or smaller (or NSF 53/58 for cysts): removes protozoa, but not viruses and not every bacterium.
  • 0.3 micron absolute or smaller: removes protozoa and bacteria, but not viruses.
  • Reverse osmosis: removes protozoa, bacteria, viruses, and salt.

The CDC Yellow Book adds that only ultrafilters (about 0.01 micron) and reverse osmosis can be trusted against viruses. Buy a filter certified for cysts, and add a chemical step for viruses. A filter sold only to improve taste does not make water safe.

UV pens

A battery-powered UV pen can kill bacteria, viruses, and protozoa in small amounts of clear water. Cloudy water blocks the light, so filter first. You cannot see whether the dose was enough, so carry spare batteries and a backup method.

Solar disinfection (SODIS)

SODIS uses sunlight and clear plastic bottles. It is slow and a last resort. The timing below follows the CDC Yellow Book.

  1. Use clear PET soft-drink bottles. Glass and some other plastics block UV.
  2. Strain or settle cloudy water first. The method needs water that is fairly clear.
  3. Fill each bottle about three-quarters full, cap it, and shake for about 20 seconds. Then fill it to the top.
  4. Lay the bottles on their sides in direct sun. A reflective surface such as foil or corrugated metal helps.
  5. Leave them about 6 hours in full sun, or 2 days if the sky is mostly cloudy. If it rains the whole time, collect rain instead.

SODIS has gaps

The CDC says only that SODIS can improve water quality; it does not promise to kill any germ group. The Akvopedia water wiki reports weaker results against viruses and protozoa than bacteria, and a 2022 study cites work finding viruses survive SODIS in plastic bottles. Treat it as partial protection.

Distillation

Distillation removes salts, heavy metals, most chemicals, and tough microbes. It is slow and uses fuel.

  1. Fill a pot halfway with water.
  2. Tie a cup to the handle of the lid so the cup sits upright when the lid is flipped over. It must not touch the water.
  3. Boil the water for about 20 minutes.
  4. Collect the water that drips from the lid into the cup.

Chemical contamination

Boiling and disinfecting do not make water safe if it is contaminated with fuel, toxic chemicals, or radioactive material. Use bottled water or another source instead.

Improvised Filters

Cloth, sand, gravel, and charcoal can clear muddy water. Think of them as a pre-filter that makes boiling or chemicals work better. The Army manual says plainly that filtering does not purify.

  1. Cut the bottom off a bottle, or poke holes in a bucket or can.
  2. Add cloth at the bottom, then gravel, charcoal, and sand.
  3. Wash the layers with clean water before first use.
  4. Pour dirty water in the top and catch the drip in a clean container.
  5. Boil or chemically treat what comes out.

Campfire charcoal is not store-bought activated carbon. It may help the smell, but it will not make water safe. The Army manual notes that letting muddy water stand for about 12 hours also clears it.

Storing Water

  • Amount. Ready.gov says store at least 1 gallon per person per day for several days. The CDC says at least 3 days, and a 2-week supply if possible. Store more for hot climates, pregnancy, illness, and pets.
  • Containers. Unopened, store-bought bottled water is the safest choice. Otherwise use food-grade containers with tight lids. Avoid glass, milk and juice jugs, and anything that once held chemicals, including old bleach jugs.
  • Cleaning. Wash with dish soap, then sanitize with 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per quart of water. Rinse with safe water (Ready.gov) or let it air-dry (CDC).
  • Treating. If the water is not from a treated public supply, disinfect it before storing. Use the bleach table above (8 drops of 6% bleach per gallon of clear water) and wait 30 minutes.
  • Rotation. Replace water you filled yourself every 6 months. For store-bought water, follow the printed date.
  • Place. Keep it cool, ideally 50 to 70°F (10 to 21°C), out of direct sun, and away from gasoline and pesticides.
  • Label. Write "drinking water" and the date on each container.

Myths and Deadly Mistakes

Myth vs. fact

Myth: A little seawater won't hurt. Fact: Your kidneys cannot make urine as salty as seawater. Flushing out that much salt takes more water than you swallowed, so you dry out faster and grow thirstier.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: You can drink your own urine in an emergency. Fact: Military survival guidance lists urine with seawater, blood, and alcohol as fluids not to drink.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Alcohol keeps you warm and hydrated. Fact: It speeds fluid loss. Phoenix Fire says not to drink it in the desert, and the Park Service warns that caffeine also speeds water loss.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Clear, cold mountain water is safe. Fact: Streams and springs can carry animal waste and chemicals you cannot see. Treat it.

Do not

  • Drink from the toilet bowl or from water with fuel, chemical, or sewage smell.
  • Use scented or "splashless" bleach, or any product that has added cleaners.
  • Eat snow or ice to stay hydrated in the cold.
  • Camp in a dry wash. Flash floods can arrive from far away.
  • Drink water from vines or plants that is milky, sticky, bitter, or cloudy.
  • Gulp liters at once or force fluids when your stomach is bloated.

Take a first-aid course that covers heat illness and dehydration (see Part 7 › Wilderness First Aid). Learn wild plants from a local expert before you rely on any for water.
