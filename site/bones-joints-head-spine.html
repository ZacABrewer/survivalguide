Bones, Joints, Head & Spine

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026
On this page

  2. Checking Circulation, Sensation, and Movement (CSM)
  3. Splinting: Principles and Improvised Splints
  4. Sprains and Strains
  5. Dislocations
  6. Spinal Assessment and Motion Restriction
  7. Head Injury
  8. Litters and Carries
  9. When Help Is Days Away
  10. Sources

Broken bones, wrecked joints, head blows, and back or neck injuries are the classic wilderness emergencies, and they are the ones most likely to leave you days from a hospital. This chapter covers how to recognize each injury, check a limb before and after you splint it, build splints and slings from what you have, decide whether to move or reduce a joint, assess the spine, watch a head injury over hours and days, and care for a fracture over a long wait. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course teaches the hands-on parts far better than any page.

Order of care

Life threats come first. Stop heavy bleeding, check breathing, and look for shock before you work on any bone (see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Splinting is never an excuse to delay airway care or bleeding control. Get help moving early if you can (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

Recognizing Fractures

A fracture is a complete break, a chip, or a crack in a bone. The American Red Cross sorts fractures into two kinds. In a closed fracture the skin is intact. In an open fracture the bone has broken through the skin, or a wound sits over the break (Figure 1).

Signs of a fracture

The Red Cross lists these signs. You do not need all of them.

  • Pain, swelling, and bruising. Bruising can take hours to show.
  • The person cannot or will not use the part.
  • A bent, crooked, or odd-shaped limb.
  • A pop or snap at the moment of injury, or a grating feel when the part moves.
  • Cold, numb, or tingling skin below the injury.
  • Bleeding, or bone visible through the skin.

The Red Cross says to treat every muscle or bone injury as a possible fracture. An X-ray is the only way to be sure.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If the person can move it, it is not broken. Fact: The Red Cross notes that a person who can move a limb may still have a broken bone. Splint it if you suspect a break.

Fractures that threaten life

The Red Cross says injuries to the head, neck, spine, pelvis, or upper leg need emergency medical care. Military trauma teaching adds that a broken pelvis can hide a large amount of internal bleeding, roughly 1,000 mL (about 1 quart) or more. A broken thigh bone (femur) can also bleed heavily inside the thigh. For these injuries, treat for shock (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs), keep the person still, and move them as little as you can. MedlinePlus says not to move someone with a hip, pelvis, or upper leg injury unless it is absolutely necessary, such as to escape danger.

Checking Circulation, Sensation, and Movement (CSM)

A broken bone can pinch or cut the blood vessels and nerves that run beside it. A splint that is too tight can do the same. So you check the part beyond the injury (the side farther from the heart) before you splint, right after each tie, and again after the splint is done. Military first-aid and trauma teaching both say to do this, and some texts call it PMS (pulse, motor, sensation) instead of CSM.

  1. Tell the person what you will do. Expose the limb and loosen tight clothing. Remove rings, watches, and bracelets from the injured limb, because swelling can trap them (Army first aid).
  2. Check circulation: skin color, warmth, and the pulse below the injury. Press the skin or nail bed. Color should come back fast (MedlinePlus).
  3. Check sensation: touch the fingers or toes and ask what they feel. Ask about numbness or tingling.
  4. Check movement: ask them to wiggle fingers or toes. Do not make them move a part that hurts a lot.
  5. Write down what you found and the time. Compare with the other limb.
  6. Repeat after the splint. A splint check is not done until you have re-checked.

If circulation is lost or the limb goes numb

Army teaching says that if the part was fine before and is poor after splinting, undo the ties, reposition or pad the splint, retie more loosely, and check again. If circulation is still poor, evacuate as soon as you can. A limb with no pulse, or one that is cold, pale, and numb, is a limb in danger. Call for help early (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).

Splinting: Principles and Improvised Splints

A splint keeps the broken ends from moving. That cuts pain, protects nerves and vessels, and lowers bleeding. Military trauma teaching sets three goals: prevent more injury, protect nerves and vessels, and make the person more comfortable.

Principles

  1. Control bleeding first. Dress any open wounds before you splint (Army first aid, TCCC).
  2. Do the first CSM check (above).
  3. Splint the limb in the position you find it in. Do not straighten a bent joint to fit a splint. The Army manual says this about elbows in particular.
  4. Include the joint above and the joint below the break. For a break at a joint, include the bone above and below.
  5. Pad hollows and bony spots (elbow, wrist, knee, ankle, groin, armpit). Keep the hard edge of the splint out of the armpit and groin.
  6. Use wide ties. The Army first-aid manual (FM 4-25.11) calls for at least four: two above the break and two below. Never tie over the break itself.
  7. Tie each knot on the splint, away from the person, snug but not tight. You should be able to slip a fingertip under the tie. Avoid wire, shoelaces, cord, or other narrow ties. They cut into the skin and can shut off blood flow (Army).
  8. Do a CSM check after each tie and again at the end. Loosen and retie if needed.
  9. Keep the person warm and treat for shock if needed.

Padded board or stick splint (arm or lower leg)

The Army first-aid teaching names tree limbs, poles, boards, sticks, and even rolled newspaper as splint material. A commercial malleable splint (SAM-style foam-padded aluminum, wire ladder, or air splint) works if you carry one. Pack one in your kit (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines). Padding can be a jacket, spare clothes, a sleeping pad cut to size, or even the person's own sleeve.

Shaping a SAM-style splint

A flat SAM-style splint bends easily. It gets its strength from curves. The maker says a simple curve along its length (a "C-curve") makes it much stiffer, and its instructions also show a reverse C-curve and a T-curve, the strongest of the three.

  1. Check CSM.
  2. Hold the splint next to the limb and bend it to follow the limb's shape, reaching past the joint above and below.
  3. Pinch a C-curve along its length where the limb needs the most support.
  4. Cut it with ordinary scissors if it is too long. Tape over or fold under any cut edge so the metal cannot cut skin.
  5. Pad any gaps and secure it with a wrap or tape, not over the break. Recheck CSM.

Practice on a healthy friend before you need it. Bend points for each body part are shown in the maker's instructions and are taught in first-aid courses.

Sling and swathe (arm, collarbone, shoulder, upper arm)

A sling carries the weight of the arm. A swathe is a wide band that holds the arm against the chest so it cannot swing. Based on the Army first-aid manual:

  1. Check CSM first.
  2. Lay a triangular cloth (a bandana, a cut square of shirt, or a folded jacket flap works) under the forearm, with the point toward the elbow.
  3. Raise the hand a little above the level of the elbow, with the fingertips showing so you can check them.
  4. Tie the two ends at the side of the neck on the uninjured side, so the weight rests on that side. Pad the knot so it does not dig in. Tuck or pin the point at the elbow.
  5. Wrap a wide swathe around the chest and the injured upper arm. Tie it on the uninjured side. Do not place it over the break.
  6. Recheck CSM in the fingers. If the elbow is broken and stiff, do not bend it to fit a sling. Splint it as found (see above).

A jacket flap also works. Fold the bottom of the jacket up over the forearm and pin or stick it closed.

Leg: splint to the other leg

For a lower-leg or thigh break, the simplest field splint is the good leg. The Army first-aid text says a fractured leg can be tied to the uninjured leg. Put padding between the legs, especially at the knees and ankles. Tie above and below the break, plus at the knees and ankles if you can. Never tie directly over the break. Add a stick or board along the outside of the injured leg if you have one. Leave the toes free so you can check them (Figure 5). Check CSM in the foot every time you tie.

Open fractures

  1. Control bleeding with pressure around the wound (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  2. Cover bone and wound with a clean dressing. Do not push exposed bone back in. Military trauma teaching says not to replace protruding bone ends.
  3. Irrigate dirt out with potable water at good pressure, ideally at least 1 quart (1 L), as early as you can, as in the wound chapter. This comes from an experienced mountain-rescue physician's lecture notes. I did not find a firm guideline on how to irrigate bone.
  4. Splint as found and check CSM.
  5. Expect to need antibiotics. An open fracture has a high risk of infection, including deep bone infection (osteomyelitis). Antibiotics for this come from a clinician (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines). Evacuate.

Realigning a deformed limb

The Red Cross and the Army both say to splint a break as it lies and not to straighten it. But a badly bent limb can pinch vessels. Wilderness courses teach trained responders to use gentle, steady pull along the limb ("traction in line") when the foot or hand beyond the break has lost its pulse, feeling, or movement. NOLS says this can improve the splint, ease pain, and restore circulation. Stop if you meet resistance or the pain climbs. Splint the limb in the position where it is easiest to hold. The same experienced mountain-rescue source notes that realigning an ankle break and splinting it is not a complete fix. An X-ray is still needed. Do not try this without training.

Femur fractures and traction splints

A traction splint is a device that pulls on the leg along its length while it holds the leg straight. It was designed for midshaft thigh fractures, the biggest and bloodiest long-bone break. In theory the pull realigns the bone, eases pain, and lowers bleeding.

  • When it is used. Trained responders use one for an isolated midshaft femur fracture, with no other injuries that bar it (see below).
  • When it is not. Two independent paramedic and crisis-medicine sources list a broken pelvis and breaks of the kneecap, shin, or ankle on the same leg as reasons not to use one. One also lists a partly amputated leg. The pull can move those injuries.
  • Harms. A 2019 paramedic journal article notes that traction splints are often put on wrong, cites case reports of nerve damage below the knee from badly applied splints, and notes that applying one can lengthen time on scene.
  • The controversy. A review of traction splints (on Life in the Fast Lane) found only 5 relevant studies, all low-level evidence. It found no proof of the claimed benefits. It argues for simple splinting until good data show a benefit. A 2022 systematic review in the journal Injury (Philipsen and colleagues) also found no clear benefit over ordinary splinting. Earlier studies it discussed hinted at less need for blood transfusion. These are secondary summaries; the evidence is thin either way.
  • The wilderness problem. The Life in the Fast Lane review says improvised traction splints, built from ski poles, webbing, and carabiners, often cannot deliver real traction. They cost the person gear and clothing. They get in the way of litters. They also take training that fades if unused.

What this means for you: most lay rescuers should not try to build a traction splint. Splint the broken thigh to the good leg and a long outside board, pad well, tie it with wide bands, and treat for shock. If you carry a manufactured traction splint and are trained to use it, that is a decision for your course and medical direction. The evidence does not show it is mandatory.

Do not

  • Do not straighten or push bone back under the skin.
  • Do not tie over the break, or use wire or narrow cord for ties.
  • Do not test a deformed joint or bone to see if it works. MedlinePlus warns against this.
  • Do not splint a person you suspect has a spine injury in a way that moves the spine (see below). Splints are for limbs.

Sprains and Strains

A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament, the tough band that holds bones together at a joint. A strain is a stretched or torn muscle or tendon. The Red Cross points out that these can look just like a fracture. If you cannot tell, treat it as a fracture.

Care in the field

  • Rest. Stop using the part and let it hang or rest without being moved or straightened.
  • Cold. The Red Cross says to put a cold pack, wrapped in a thin dry towel, on a closed injury for no more than 20 minutes. Then wait 20 minutes before using it again. Do not put ice straight on skin, and do not use heat on a fresh injury (Red Cross, MedlinePlus).
  • Compression. For an ankle, wrap an elastic bandage with each turn covering half of the one before. The Red Cross says to wrap tighter near the toes than higher up, and to leave the toes and heel out so you can check them. Check CSM beyond the wrap. If toes turn pale, cool, or numb, loosen it.
  • Pain. Over-the-counter pain relievers (ibuprofen, acetaminophen) may help. Use as directed on the label. Ask a clinician or pharmacist first if you are unsure they are safe for this person.

Can you walk out?

Wilderness medicine borrows the Ottawa ankle rules from emergency medicine. According to a rescue physician's lecture notes, a person who can take four steps may not need an X-ray, and tenderness over certain bone points (the back edge of the ankle bones, the foot's navicular bone, and the base of the fifth metatarsal on the outer foot) argues for one. In practice:

  • If they can bear weight, the ankle is not deformed, and the bony points are not tender, treat it as a sprain. Support the ankle with a brace or wrap, and walk out slowly, reassessing often.
  • If they cannot bear weight, the part is deformed, or the bony points are tender, splint it and plan an assisted evacuation.

The same notes describe a tape field alternative to a brace: duct tape applied on the outside of the ankle only, with the ankle held slightly bent while taping.

Seek care if pain and swelling do not improve. The Red Cross says to get medical advice if pain or discomfort continues and symptoms do not improve.

Dislocations

A dislocation is a joint pushed out of place. The Red Cross says it usually comes from violent force. The joint looks wrong, hurts badly, and will not move normally. Dislocations often come with broken bone, nerve injury, or damaged vessels, and a joint that has dislocated once may dislocate again (MedlinePlus).

Where the sources disagree

Lay first-aid agencies say not to try to put a dislocated joint back. The Red Cross, MedlinePlus, and St John Ambulance all say to splint it as found and get care. Wilderness medicine curricula teach some reductions to trained responders when the hospital is far away. NOLS calls field reduction of dislocations unique to wilderness medicine and says it can give dramatic pain relief and lower circulation and nerve risk. This guide gives you the lay rule first, then names what trained responders do. It does not teach you the reduction itself. That needs hands-on practice and, ideally, medical direction.

For everyone: stabilize and check

  1. Do a CSM check. Pulse, feeling, and movement beyond the joint matter most.
  2. Support the joint in the position you found it. Pad it with clothing. For a shoulder or elbow, use a sling and a swathe. For a knee, ankle, or hip, keep weight off the leg and pad it so it cannot move.
  3. Use a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
  4. Check CSM again after splinting. St John Ambulance says to check circulation beyond bandages every 10 minutes at first, and loosen if needed.
  5. Treat for shock, and keep the person warm.

Do not reduce if

Every source that discusses reduction names reasons to stop. Skip it and splint as found if:

  • The hand or foot beyond the joint is pale, cold, numb, or has no pulse (World Extreme Medicine; EMS World).
  • You feel grinding, which may mean a fracture.
  • You are not sure it is a dislocation and not a break.
  • The joint is a true knee, a hip, an elbow, or an ankle. I found no source that supports reduction of these by lay or first-aid-level rescuers. A knee dislocation can tear the artery behind the knee.
  • Pain surges or you meet resistance. NOLS gives that as a stop rule for shoulders.
  • You are untrained, or medical direction is not available to guide you.

Shoulder

The shoulder is the dislocation wilderness courses focus on. NOLS cites Wilderness Medical Society guidance and a 2010 study of non-medical personnel reducing shoulders. The methods taught are slow and gentle, and small studies report good success. Studies that small can miss rare harm, and no method is risk-free. The risks are nerve or vessel injury, and breaking a bone that was already cracked. Shoulder reduction is for trained responders only. This guide does not teach the steps; learn them hands-on in a Wilderness First Responder course. A shoulder that is not reduced is still safe in a sling and swathe, padded in the position of comfort. Wait for a trained responder or an exit.

Kneecap (patella)

A kneecap dislocation means the kneecap has slipped sideways. The kneecap sits out of place toward the outside of the knee, which hurts and swells, and the person cannot straighten the knee (Merck). Merck notes the kneecap may slide back into place on its own. Some EMS and wilderness programs teach trained responders a quick field reduction; that is for trained responders only, and this guide does not teach it. For everyone else: pad and support the knee in the position of comfort, check pulse and feeling in the foot, and get the person to care. A true knee dislocation (the shin out of line with the thigh) is different. It can tear the artery behind the knee and is an emergency. Do not try to move it. Even if the kneecap goes back by itself, the person needs to be checked, because the force can chip bone or cartilage (Merck).

Finger

Finger joints are small, and one summary says a reduction attempt in a clear finger dislocation generally has few consequences if it fails. Even so, an X-ray afterward is advised, because a missed fracture or a poorly aligned joint can cost hand function. The source cautions that trying to relocate a fracture is "painful and pointless." Unless you are trained, splint the finger as found (a stick or a buddy-tape to its neighbor with padding between) and have it checked.

Spinal Assessment and Motion Restriction

Most backs and necks are not broken after a fall. Some are. The goal is to protect the few without hurting everyone else by tying them to a board for hours. The Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) spinal-cord guidelines set that goal: protect the spinal cord and spine while keeping patient and rescuer safe. For full detail and the first steps, see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs.

When to suspect a spine injury

Think about it after a fall from height, a crash, a dive or slide, a hard blow to the head, or being thrown. Be extra careful with older adults. Be suspicious if the person has neck or back pain, numbness or weakness, a changed mental state, alcohol or drugs on board, or another painful injury that could distract them.

The focused spine assessment

Wilderness courses teach trained responders a method called the focused spine assessment. The 2024 WMS guideline suggests not calling this "clearing the spine," because only a hospital can truly rule out a spine injury; in the field you are deciding whether motion restriction is needed. The 2024 update also adds a decision chart for suspected spine injury built on these same rules. NOLS describes it as selective spine immobilization taught with many practice sessions, and one NOLS case notes that doing it takes WFR-level training and is beyond a 16-hour Wilderness First Aid course. WMS names the NEXUS rules and the Canadian C-spine rule as tools trained personnel can use to decide if neck motion should be restricted. In a NOLS case study, the team cleared a teen who had rolled down a slope only when all three conditions held:

  • He was a reliable patient: alert and oriented, sober, and not distracted by another painful injury.
  • He had normal circulation, feeling, and movement in all four limbs, unless another injury explained a movement problem.
  • He had no pain or tenderness when the whole spine was pressed from the neck to the tailbone.

If any one fails, or you are not trained, or you are unsure, keep protecting the spine. Deciding to stop is a judgment call. One case study had the team check with a medical control physician by radio before letting a patient walk.

Spinal motion restriction, not rigid immobilization

For years the standard was a hard collar and a backboard. Current WMS guidance has moved away from that. Its 2024 update says care should aim at the goal (protecting the spinal cord) rather than a technique (immobilization), and that suspected spine injuries should not be immobilized with rigid collars or backboards. The points below come from the WMS 2024 guideline summary and match its earlier 2019 version.

  • Immobilization is not the goal. The goal is spinal motion restriction (SMR): limit motion, not lock the person down.
  • Rigid collars, bought or improvised, have no required role outside the hospital; WMS says they carry many risks and no proven benefit. A soft collar is one of several tools if you want to reduce neck motion, as long as it does not get in the way of urgent care. A vacuum splint is better than a rigid collar. Rigid collars must not be used on a person known to have ankylosing spondylitis (a stiff, fused spine).
  • Support the neck in the position of comfort.
  • A person with numbness or weakness from a possible spine injury still gets soft, passive motion restriction (padding, rolled clothing, a vacuum splint), not total immobilization.
  • Do not immobilize for an isolated penetrating injury (for example, a stab wound with no fall or blow).
  • An alert, reliable person may get out of a hazard on their own, keeping neck movement small.
  • If the head is not in line, bring it gently back to neutral using light to moderate pull. Stop if you meet resistance, the pain increases, or new numbness or weakness appears.
  • A vacuum mattress restricts motion better than a backboard and is more comfortable. A backboard is only a tool for short moves, never for holding someone still for hours. Boards cause pressure sores. Take the person off any board or device as soon as the move is done.
  • For an unconscious person, facial fractures, or an airway worry, consider placing them on their side. If you must open the airway, WMS prefers a jaw thrust over head tilt and chin lift. Airway always beats spine care.

Moving the person: lift-and-slide first, log roll if you must

To get a pad, tarp, or litter under someone, WMS (2019 and again in 2024) prefers a lift-and-slide transfer, with the head held by a grip called the trap squeeze, over the log roll whenever motion restriction is the goal. Mountain-rescue teaching adds that the log roll protects the spine less well and can worsen bleeding from a broken pelvis. In a lift-and-slide (also called a "many hands" or BEAM lift), rescuers kneel on both sides, lift the person straight up together just far enough, and another person slides the pad or litter underneath. The head holder calls every move. Learn both the lift and the trap squeeze hands-on in a course.

The log roll turns a person onto their side as one unit. It is still widely taught, and you may need it when you do not have enough hands to lift, or to check the back. Avoid it if you suspect a broken pelvis. A long-board teaching text (StatPearls) uses four people at minimum and five if possible:

  1. Tell the patient what will happen. Cross their arms over their chest. Gently bring the legs into line with the body if they are not already.
  2. The head holder kneels at the head, holds the head and neck in line with the body, and is the only one who gives the commands.
  3. Helpers kneel on one side at the chest, the hips, and the legs. Each reaches across and grips the far side of the body.
  4. The head holder announces the count, often "on three." Everyone turns the person to their side together. Keep the head, neck, and trunk in line, like a log.
  5. A fifth person (or the leg helper, if there are only four) checks the back and slides the pad or litter in.
  6. On the head holder's next count, everyone lowers the person onto it together. Recheck feeling and movement in all four limbs.

Do not

  • Do not force a twisted head into line. Stop and support it where it is if it resists or hurts more.
  • Do not let spine care delay airway care or bleeding control.
  • Do not leave a person on a hard board for long. It can cause pressure sores. Pad bony areas and move them off it as soon as you can.
  • Do not decide a spine is fine in a person who is drunk, confused, in severe pain elsewhere, or not fully alert.

Head Injury

A blow to the head can cause a concussion, which is a temporary change in how the brain works. It can also cause bleeding or swelling that gets worse over hours. In the field the two can look the same at first. That is why you watch.

Concussion

The CDC and a wilderness concussion review list the usual signs. The most common is headache. Others are dizziness, balance trouble, nausea, confusion, memory gaps (amnesia), light or sound sensitivity, fogginess, and mood or personality change. Passing out is uncommon and does not by itself measure severity. The same review warns that concussion can look like altitude illness, low sodium, dehydration, carbon monoxide poisoning, or bleeding in the skull (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).

Danger signs: get help now

The CDC lists signs that need 911 or an emergency department right away after a bump or blow:

  • Seizure.
  • Unable to recognize people or places.
  • Repeated nausea or vomiting.
  • Increasing confusion, restlessness, or agitation, or odd behavior.
  • Passing out along with growing drowsiness, or being unable to wake up or stay awake.
  • Slurred speech, weakness, numbness, or poor coordination.
  • A headache that keeps getting worse.
  • One pupil larger than the other, or double vision.

MedlinePlus adds a severe headache or stiff neck, not being able to move all or part of an arm or leg, vomiting more than once, and any loss of consciousness, even brief. NOLS adds signs that point to rising pressure in the skull: a slow, bounding pulse, odd or irregular breathing, warm flushed skin, and rising blood pressure with a widening gap between top and bottom numbers. The Merck Manual describes the same late pattern (high blood pressure, slow pulse, slow or irregular breathing). This combination is late and grave. Do not wait for all of it.

Signs of a broken skull base (Merck): clear fluid or blood leaking from the nose or ears, bruising behind an ear, or bruising around both eyes ("raccoon eyes"). Any of these means evacuate. If you suspect a skull fracture, MedlinePlus says not to press on the bleeding spot or pick debris out of the wound; cover it with a clean dressing.

Who should be evacuated

The wilderness concussion review lists these reasons to call for evacuation as quickly as possible for adults: any loss of consciousness, severe headache, repeated vomiting, age over 60, alcohol or drug intoxication, a seizure after the injury, anything less than fully normal alertness, weakness or other nerve findings, signs of a skull-base fracture (see above), or a fall of more than about 3 feet (1 m) with a head injury. Children with loss of consciousness (especially over 60 seconds), a skull fracture, or nerve findings are in the same group. NOLS adds that any loss of responsiveness means evacuate, even if the person seems to recover, and that mild symptoms that do not improve after 24 hours do too.

Monitoring over hours and days

  1. Check the neck first. Neck pain, tenderness, or numbness in the limbs means treat as a possible spine injury (see above).
  2. Check alertness, orientation, speech, and pupils (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). NOLS says to base decisions on the patient's current mental status, not on what bystanders say.
  3. Ask questions that fit the setting: what day it is, what you were doing, what happened just before. The wilderness review says speed and accuracy both count, and plain "name, place, date" questions are less reliable.
  4. Test balance on safe, level ground with a spotter next to the person.
  5. Watch for at least 24 hours. NOLS says to monitor if the person is fully alert and has only mild symptoms. The Merck Manual says that even people sent home from a hospital with a mild head injury need family or friends to watch them closely for another 24 hours. Record the time of each check and what you saw.
  6. At night, sleep next to the person. None of the sources checked for this guide set a schedule for waking checks, and CDC guidance for children says to let them sleep as usual. But being unable to wake up is a CDC danger sign. If you are worried, or the person was getting worse before sleep, rouse them and make sure they wake normally and answer sensibly. Ask your course or medical direction what schedule they use.
  7. Compare each check with the last. Worse headache, new vomiting, growing sleepiness, new confusion, or a new difference in the pupils all mean evacuate.

Resting and getting going again

The 2014 wilderness concussion review says the main treatment is rest from physical and mental strain until symptoms are gone. Newer CDC advice (written for children) is gentler on rest: limit demanding activity for the first day or two, but do not keep the person in bed all day, and encourage light activity such as short walks as long as it does not make symptoms worse. In the wilderness review's plan, after 24 symptom-free hours, build back up over 2 to 3 days: light hiking, then harder hiking, then hiking with a pack. If symptoms return, rest 24 hours and repeat the last step that was fine. The CDC says to avoid anything that risks another head blow while the brain heals, and that sports should never resume on the day of the injury. If you are within an easy day's travel of base, the patient may walk out slowly with frequent stops. If you are 2 or more days away, rest until symptoms improve or ask for evacuation. Anyone with a concussion should see a clinician once back in a controlled setting. The CDC says a child with a possible concussion needs to be seen by a health care provider.

Warning

Do not give sleep aids or sedatives; the CDC says to ask a doctor or nurse before giving a child any sleep medicine. No alcohol or street drugs for at least 48 hours after a serious head injury (MedlinePlus). The Merck Manual names acetaminophen as the preferred pain reliever for concussion; use it as directed on the label, and ask a clinician if you are unsure. Stop and evacuate if the headache is getting worse. A worsening headache is a danger sign, not a pain problem.

Do not

  • Do not shake a dazed person to rouse them (MedlinePlus).
  • Do not take off a helmet if you suspect a serious head or neck injury (MedlinePlus). The one exception is when it stops you from keeping the airway open, because airway comes first.

Litters and Carries

Think before you move. Walking a person out with a sprain is one thing. Carrying someone with a back, head, pelvis, or thigh injury is another, and it takes many people and many hours. See Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions for the full decision and carrying detail. Here are the basics.

  • Can they walk? A person with a mild sprain or a cleared spine and a stable, splinted arm may walk with help. Someone with a thigh, pelvis, or spine injury, a worsening head injury, or any shock should not.
  • Carry as a last resort. If help is coming, it is often safer to stay put, keep the person warm, and wait.
  • Packaging. Whatever you build, pad it, insulate the person from the ground, and wrap them in a bag or blankets to prevent chilling.

Improvised blanket-and-pole litter

The Army first-aid manual (FM 4-25.11) lists pole material such as strong branches, tent poles, skis, or lengths of pipe. For the bed, use a blanket, poncho, tarp, or jackets and shirts. A door, board, bench, ladder, or cot also works if you pad it. Choose poles long enough to leave handholds beyond the head and feet.

  1. Spread the blanket flat on the ground.
  2. Lay one pole along the middle of the blanket, so it splits it into two halves.
  3. Fold one half over the pole and onto the other half.
  4. Lay the second pole along the middle of the doubled part.
  5. Fold the free edges over the second pole and back across toward the first pole (Figure 8).
  6. Before you load anyone, have a person of similar weight lie on it and lift it a few inches to make sure the poles hold and the folds do not slip.

Jacket or shirt litter: button two or more jackets or shirts, turn them inside out so the sleeves are inside, and pass the poles through the sleeves (Army). No poles: roll the edges of a blanket or tarp in toward the person from both sides and grip the rolls to carry.

Move the person onto the litter with a lift-and-slide or a log roll (see above). The Army manual says two or four bearers lift together, keep the person as level as possible, and take care on slopes. It warns that an improvised litter is an emergency measure that must be built as well as you can so you do not drop the person, and that it should be swapped for a real litter as soon as possible. On a long carry, plan for several teams of carriers and switch often.

A suspected spine injury raises the bar. A litter that sags in the middle bends the back. Use a firm base such as a sleeping pad on a board or a vacuum mattress if one is available. For most people with suspected spine or pelvic injury, waiting for a rescue team is safer than a long carry.

When Help Is Days Away

This is the wilderness part of wilderness medicine. A splinted bone does not need a hospital in the first hours. What it needs is a calm, warm, hydrated person and someone watching the limb. Call for help early, even if it will take days (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication and Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming).

Keep the splint working

  • Check CSM often at first. St John Ambulance suggests every 10 minutes after tying a first bandage. No source checked for this guide gives a schedule for later days; a reasonable habit is to check at least a few times a day and any time the person says the limb feels odd.
  • Swelling makes ties tighter. Loosen and retie as needed. Swelling can also go down and leave the splint loose.
  • NOLS says to check inside splints and under bandages again later, because bruising and swelling can appear hours after the injury and new injuries may show.
  • Look at the skin under pads and ties daily. Pad bony points. Move the person off hard surfaces, because boards cause pressure sores.
  • Keep the limb still. For leg injuries, set up the camp so the person does not need to put weight on it.

Keep the person going

  • Keep them warm and out of the wind and ground. Shock and cold make every injury worse (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies).
  • Give fluids and food as they can take them (see Part 2 › Water).
  • Pain control: a wrapped cold pack for the first day, comfortable positioning, and over-the-counter pain relievers used as directed on the label.
  • For open fractures, watch for infection every day (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  • For long waits, see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care.
