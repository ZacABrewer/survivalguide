Eastern Deciduous Forest, Appalachians, Great Lakes & Ozarks

Researched & fact-checkedNA-08Checked Nov 11, 2026

The eastern deciduous forest is the green belt of oak, hickory, maple, and beech that runs from the Missouri and Iowa prairie edge to the Atlantic. It is rich in water, wood, and food, and it is also home to ticks, venomous snakes, and many poisonous plants that look like good food. This chapter covers the main hazards first, then what you can safely use.

At a Glance

Where
Eastern US from Missouri, Iowa, and Minnesota to the Atlantic, plus southern Ontario and Quebec: the Ohio Valley, Great Lakes, Appalachians, and Ozark and Ouachita highlands.
Neighbors
West: the prairie of NA-07 (see Part 13 › NA-07 Great Plains & Prairies). North: NA-02 boreal conifer forest. South: NA-09 Southeast pine and swamp country. Read both chapters near a border.
Climate
Four seasons. The north is humid continental with warm summers and cold, snowy winters (Köppen Dfb). The middle is humid continental with hot summers (Dfa). The south is humid subtropical (Cfa). US Forest Service ecoregion maps use the related Trewartha codes Dcb, Dca, and Cf, and the exact lines between types differ by system.
Forest types
Oak-hickory, maple-beech-birch ("northern hardwoods"), mixed hardwoods in Appalachian coves, and oak-pine in the Ozarks.
Elevation
Near sea level on the coast to 6,684 ft (2,037 m) at Mount Mitchell, North Carolina, the highest point east of the Mississippi River (NC State Parks).
Water
Great Lakes and St. Lawrence; Ohio, Tennessee, Missouri, and Mississippi systems; more than 4,400 springs in Missouri alone.

Seasons and Weather

Every season has a weather hazard here (National Weather Service, NWS). See Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies and Part 9 › Natural Disasters.

SeasonMain hazardsWhat to do
WinterIce storms: freezing rain of at least 0.25 in (6 mm) coats trees and wires, and limbs snap (NWS). Lake-effect snow: bands drop 2 to 3 in (5 to 8 cm) an hour or more downwind of the lakes, late fall through winter, and can change sharply within a mile or two.Stay off roads in freezing rain. Camp away from heavy limbs.
SpringSevere storms and tornadoes. NWS says tornadoes can strike any time of year and are most common in the Central Plains and Southeast, but have been reported in all 50 states. Flash floods in hollows and Ozark creeks.Pick shelter before storms. Camp above creek beds.
SummerHeat and humidity: sweat cools you less when air is damp, so heat illness comes faster (NWS). Lightning. Flash floods. Algal blooms. Peak tick and snake season.Hike early or late. Drink often.
FallFirst hard freezes, early snow, lake-effect starts. Deer-season hunters in the woods. Rattlesnakes bask on ledges in spring and fall.Wear bright color in hunting season.

Flash floods and lightning

Six inches (15 cm) of fast water can knock an adult down. Twelve inches (30 cm) can carry away most cars. Never walk or drive into floodwater (NWS). Steep hollows and narrow Ozark valleys can fill fast after rain falling miles upstream. If you hear thunder, lightning can reach you. Wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. Stay away from isolated trees, ridges, cliffs, and water. No place outside is safe in a thunderstorm (NWS).

Heat illness

Heat exhaustion: heavy sweat, pale clammy skin, fast weak pulse, cramps, nausea. Move to shade, loosen clothes, cool with wet cloths, and give small sips of water. Get medical help if the person vomits, gets worse, or is not better within 1 hour. Heat stroke is an emergency: confusion, slurred speech, body temperature above 103°F (39.4°C), hot red or damp skin. Call 911, cool the person, and do not give fluids (NWS).

Water

Water is easy to find here, and almost none of it is safe raw. Treat every source (see Part 2 › Water). Near towns and river mouths, even Great Lakes shore water carries runoff.

Germs

Giardia is the classic stream parasite. Symptoms (diarrhea, gas, cramps) start 1 to 2 weeks after infection and can last 2 to 6 weeks. CDC says to boil lake, river, or spring water for at least 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft, or 2,000 m), or filter it.

Karst and Ozark springs

Karst is limestone ground full of sinkholes, caves, and underground channels. Missouri has more than 4,400 springs. The Missouri Geological Survey says water that enters a sinkhole can travel more than 30 miles (48 km) underground before it reaches a spring. USGS says surface contaminants move through Ozark karst quickly with little to no natural filtering. A clear, cold spring can still carry manure or septic waste from far away. Treat spring water.

Algal blooms

Blooms look like foam, scum, mats, or paint on green, blue, brown, or red water. CDC says pets and livestock can die within hours of contact. Stay out of the water and keep pets out. You cannot treat this water in the field. CDC warns that boiling does not make toxin-laden water drinkable and may even concentrate the toxins. Utah's environmental agency says no known camping filter removes these toxins, and chlorine does not either. Choose another source. If you feel ill, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222.

Acid mine drainage

Old coal mines in Pennsylvania, Ohio, northern West Virginia, and Maryland leak acid water (USGS). Dissolved iron can leave red, orange, or yellow crust on stream beds and tint the water. Some drainage has a pH of 2.5 to 4 and carries heavy metals such as copper, lead, and mercury (USGS). Boiling kills germs but does not remove metals or acid (see Part 2 › Water). If a streambed is stained orange, skip it.

Finding water in the woods

  1. Walk downhill and follow green, mossy ground to streams and springs.
  2. Collect from flowing water, not stagnant edges.
  3. Treat it: boil, filter, or both.

Fire and Shelter Materials

See Part 2 › Fire, Part 2 › Shelter, and Part 2 › Firewood & Woodlots for methods.

Tinder

  • Birch bark. River birch bark peels in papery layers all year (NC State). Take only loose curls from dead or fallen trees. Peeling live bark scars the tree.
  • Eastern redcedar bark. The red-brown bark comes off in long, narrow, fibrous strips (NC State). Dry strips are worth testing as a tinder bundle.
  • Tinder fungus. Fomes fomentarius is a "birch-loving conk." The inner layer can be scraped to a fluffy mass that a small spark will light. Other conks, like false tinder fungus, look similar, so confirm the species.
  • Tinder must be bone dry.

Friction fire

The US Army manual describes a seasoned hardwood drill on a seasoned softwood board. It says the bow itself can be any resilient green stick. Species lists vary a lot between sources, so test local dry, non-resinous, non-aromatic softwoods and practice before you need it.

Fuel and shelter

The Army manual advises non-aromatic, seasoned hardwood for fuel. NC State lists hickory as fuelwood and says beech has high fuel value. For debris huts, oak and maple leaf litter is plentiful, but check every handful for poison ivy vines and rodent nests.

Do not burn

  • Poison ivy, poison oak, or poison sumac. The smoke carries urushiol and can cause severe airway swelling and death (Alabama Extension).
  • Vines you have not identified.

Ramps: look-alikes and overharvest

In Vermont in 2020 the poison center handled 25 possible false hellebore cases, over four times the usual number. Symptoms include severe nausea and vomiting, slow heartbeat, low blood pressure, and slowed breathing. If someone may have eaten it, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 at once; do not wait for symptoms. Call 911 if the person passes out or has trouble breathing (Vermont Department of Health). Ramps need 5 to 7 years to flower, so heavy picking empties patches (Penn State). Penn State asks people not to harvest narrow-leaf ramps (Allium burdickii) in Pennsylvania until research shows how common they are. Take a few leaves from big patches and leave bulbs in the ground.

Sassafras and safrole

NC State notes safrole is a weak carcinogen in animal studies. Federal rules treat foods with added safrole or oil of sassafras as adulterated, and they name sassafras tea as an example (21 CFR 189.180). This guide does not recommend sassafras root tea.

Medicinal Plants

Most "wild medicine" has weak evidence. Use these as comfort measures, not as replacements for real care. Dosing and preparation are in Part 7 › Herbal Medicine & Health Tonics. Never use any plant if you are pregnant, on medicines, or unsure of its identity without asking a clinician.

PlantRatingTraditional use and caution

Its fruits can upset the stomach if eaten (NC State). For urushiol, the recommended step is washing skin with soap and water or rubbing alcohol within 10 to 20 minutes, not with hot water (Alabama Extension).
Plantain Plantago major◇ MLeaves were used on bites, stings, and cuts. Not evaluated. Keep real wounds clean (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Witch hazel Hamamelis virginiana◇ MSkin-care uses. Shrub flowers in fall (Oct to Dec) with spidery yellow petals. No safety data on the NC State page.
Slippery elm Ulmus rubra◇ MMoist inner bark is used in throat lozenges (NC State). Leaves feel rough like sandpaper. Take twigs, never strip a trunk; a ring of stripped bark kills a tree.
Mullein Verbascum thapsus◇ MFuzzy first-year rosette, tall yellow spike in year two. Historic remedy; no toxicity data on the NC State page.
Bee balm Monarda didyma◇ MSquare stem; leaves smell minty when crushed. Its old name, Oswego tea, comes from Native people of the Oswego area of New York, who used the leaves for tea (NC State). Skip mildewed plants.
Elderberry Sambucus◇ MNCCIH: limited evidence it may ease cold and flu symptoms. Raw berries and leaves are toxic; cooking removes the toxin.

Dangerous Plants

Many of these grow next to good food. Badges: ✖ D may be fatal, ▲ T toxic, ☐ S skin hazard, ✱ X psychoactive. For poisoning first aid see Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning. Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222.

PlantRatingDanger and key ID
Poison ivy Toxicodendron radicans☐ SUrushiol oil in every part, all year. Three leaflets, often toothed or mitten-shaped. Larger vines look hairy from tiny roots. About 85 percent of people are allergic (Alabama Extension). Never burn.
Poison sumac T. vernix☐ SSwamps and bogs. Shrub or small tree with 7 to 13 smooth-edged leaflets (Alabama says up to 15), red leaf stalks, dull yellowish-white fruit (NC State). Not the harmless red-fruited sumacs.
Poison hemlock, water hemlock✖ DCarrot-family killers. See Part 4 › Deadly Look-Alikes: The Carrot Family.

Mayapple Podophyllum peltatum▲ TSee Figure 4. Leaves, stems, roots, seeds, and unripe fruit are highly toxic. Only the soft, yellow, fully ripe fruit flesh is edible, and only in small amounts; never eat the seeds (NC State, Wisconsin Extension).
Pokeweed Phytolacca americana▲ TPurple-red stems, dark purple berries. All parts are poisonous. Wear gloves. This guide gives no way to cook it.
Ohio buckeye Aesculus glabra▲ T5 to 7 fingerlike leaflets; shiny brown nuts with a pale "eye." Seeds, leaves, and bark are poisonous; causes vomiting, muscle weakness, and even paralysis (NC State).
Canada moonseed Menispermum canadense✖ DGrape-like blue-black fruit with one crescent seed. Convulsions; may be fatal.
Virginia creeper Parthenocissus quinquefolia✖ DVine with 5 toothed leaflets and small blue-black berries. NC State says eating the berries may be fatal. Do not mistake it for wild grape.
Jimsonweed Datura stramonium▲ T✱ XWhite trumpet flowers, spiny egg-shaped pods. All parts toxic. Causes fever, delirium, hallucinations, convulsions, and coma (NC State).
Bittersweet nightshade Solanum dulcamara✖ DVine with purple flowers and red berries. Highly toxic. Don't eat any wild nightshade-family berry you cannot name.
Jack-in-the-pulpit Arisaema triphyllum▲ THooded spathe, three-part leaves, red fall berries. Calcium oxalate crystals cause severe mouth pain and swelling.
False hellebore Veratrum viride✖ D2 to 6 ft (0.6 to 1.8 m), pleated leaves, wet ground. Ramp look-alike; see Figure 2.
Wild ginger Asarum canadense▲ TPair of hairy heart-shaped leaves; ginger-scented root. Aristolochic acid harms kidneys and causes cancer. Do not eat any part.
Lily of the valley Convallaria majalis▲ TCardiac glycosides upset the heartbeat. NC State rates severity low, Penn State calls it deadly; treat it as dangerous.
Yew Taxus✖ DEvergreen with flat needles and red cups. Ornamental yews such as English yew are planted around homes; Canada yew (T. canadensis) is a low native shrub of cool, shady northern woods (NC State). Yew bark, leaves, and seeds can be fatal to people and animals through heart and breathing failure (NC State). Treat every yew as deadly.
Giant hogweed, wild parsnip☐ SSap plus sunlight causes blistering burns. Giant hogweed can reach 20 ft (6 m). It is a federal noxious weed (NC State). See Part 4 › Deadly Look-Alikes: The Carrot Family.

Fungi: Warning

Do not eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has confirmed it in person. The full rules are in Part 4 › Mushrooms & Fungi. These are the traps in this region.

  • True morel or false morel? True morels are completely hollow when sliced lengthwise and have a pitted, honeycomb cap (Missouri Department of Conservation). False morels have lobed, wrinkled caps and chambered insides. In Missouri, true morels appear from late March through April. MDC treats all false morels as toxic. Gyromitrin from Gyromitra causes headache, severe diarrhea, and vomiting 2 to 24 hours later, and can damage the liver and kidneys (NAMA). Cook true morels thoroughly.
  • Chanterelle or jack-o'-lantern? Both are bright orange to yellow and funnel-shaped, so color and shape do not separate them. Chanterelles have blunt, forked, cross-veined ridges, not true gills, and grow on the ground, alone or in scattered groups, never on wood (Missouri Department of Conservation). Jack-o'-lanterns (Omphalotus illudens) have sharp-edged, crowded true gills that also run down the stalk, and they grow in clusters from stumps, tree bases, or buried wood, July through October (MDC). A cluster that seems to rise from bare soil may be on buried wood. They are poisonous even when cooked. Nausea and vomiting usually start 1 to 3 hours after eating, and victims often thought they had found chanterelles (NAMA).
  • Amanita. Amatoxin mushrooms are the deadliest. For 6 to 24 hours you may feel fine while the toxin is already damaging the liver and kidneys. Then come violent vomiting and diarrhea, then a day of seeming recovery, then liver and kidney failure (NAMA). NAMA gives about 10 percent fatality with good care and about 50 percent without prompt treatment, and says there is no antidote. If you think you ate one, go to a hospital at once, even if you feel well.

Animals

Game and food animals

White-tailed deer, wild turkey, squirrels, rabbits, groundhogs, raccoons, fish, and crayfish are all common. See Part 4 › Hunting, Part 4 › Trapping, Part 4 › Fishing, and Part 5 › Food Safety. Wear gloves when field-dressing and cook wild meat thoroughly.

Raccoon droppings can carry roundworm eggs (below), and raccoons are among the most common rabies carriers (CDC).

Species cards

Black Bear Ursus americanus

Risk
The main danger is a bear that learns people mean food. Feeding bears makes problems worse (NPS).
Prevent
Never give a bear access to your food. Hike in groups, which bears notice sooner. Avoid surprising a bear, and never get between a mother and her cubs (NPS).
Bear is still
Stay calm and talk in a low voice so it knows you are human. Move away slowly and sideways, and leave it an escape route. If you cannot detour, wait for it to leave. A bear standing on its hind legs is usually just curious (NPS).
Bear comes closer or charges
Stand your ground and slowly wave your arms. Do not run, scream, or climb a tree. Make yourself look big, pick up small children, and keep your pack on. Many charges are bluffs that turn away at the last moment. If you carry bear spray (an EPA-approved bear product, not personal pepper spray), it is for a charging or attacking bear (NPS).
If attacked
Do not play dead with a black bear. Get to a car or building if you can. Otherwise fight back with anything you have and aim at the face and muzzle. If any bear stalks you or attacks your tent, it may see you as prey: fight back (NPS).

Timber Rattlesnake Crotalus horridus

✖ D
ID
Heavy body 3 to 5 ft (0.9 to 1.5 m). Dark bands on tan, yellow, brown, or gray; black tail; rattle. A heat pit sits between each eye and nostril. A rattle is not proof: tails break and startled snakes may stay silent (MDC). Harmless snakes also shake their tails (NJ Fish & Wildlife).
Habitat
Mature forest and rocky wooded hillsides, ledges, and bluffs; south- and southwest-facing slopes near streams. Active about April to mid-October.
Behavior
Shy and camouflaged; often stays still. It usually tries to escape, but coils and rattles if cornered. The bite can kill, so get medical care at once. Even a freshly killed snake can bite by reflex (MDC).

Copperhead Agkistrodon contortrix

▲ T⚠ L
ID
Stocky, usually 2 to 3 ft (0.6 to 0.9 m), rarely over 4 ft. Gray, copper, tan, or pinkish tan with an hourglass pattern: dark bands wide on the sides, narrow at the spine (Clemson, NJ Fish & Wildlife, MDC). Vertical pupils and an arrowhead-shaped head fit copperheads, but checking them needs a close look, which is unsafe. Young have a bright yellow to greenish tail tip.
Mix-ups
Northern water snakes show the reverse: blotches wide at the spine, narrow at the sides. Young rat snakes have blotches that fool people. Adult water snakes can look solid dark. Color is the weakest clue (NJ Fish & Wildlife).
Behavior
Hides in leaf litter and often stays still. Bites usually follow stepping on or handling it. It may curl, shake its tail, or raise its head first (Clemson). MDC calls the venom mild, and deaths are very rare, but every bite needs medical care.

Northern Cottonmouth Agkistrodon piscivorus

✖ D⚠ L
Range
Southern edge only. In Missouri: the southeast corner plus scattered Ozark populations; none north of the Missouri River (MDC). Check your state wildlife agency for range maps elsewhere.
ID
Heavy, dark olive brown to nearly black, usually 3 to 4 ft (0.9 to 1.2 m). Head much wider than the neck, pit between eye and nostril, most with a white upper lip. It gapes to show a white mouth when threatened. Young are lighter and banded, with a yellowish tail tip (MDC).
Mix-ups
Harmless water snakes are less heavy, with a smaller head, no pit, and no light line from the eye to the mouth corner. They are often killed by mistake (MDC). Treat any heavy, dark snake at the water's edge as venomous. All Missouri snakes are protected by law.
Bite
Can be fatal without prompt treatment. About 4 to 5 percent of Missouri venomous bites.

Snakebite: what to do

  1. Move away from the snake. Do not touch or trap it, even if it is dead.
  2. Get emergency care fast. Do not wait for symptoms, and do not drive yourself.
  3. Keep calm. Sit or lie down with the bite in a neutral, comfortable position.
  4. Take off rings and watches before swelling starts.
  5. Wash the bite with soap and water.
  6. Cover it with a clean, dry dressing.
  7. Mark the edge of the swelling on the skin and write the time next to it.

Do not cut, suck, use a tourniquet or electric shock, apply ice, or soak the bite in water. Do not drink alcohol or take pain relievers such as aspirin, ibuprofen, or naproxen (CDC NIOSH). See Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.

Ticks (blacklegged, American dog, lone star)

Diseases
Blacklegged tick Ixodes scapularis: Lyme, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan. American dog tick: tularemia and Rocky Mountain spotted fever. Lone star tick Amblyomma americanum: ehrlichiosis, Heartland and Bourbon viruses, and alpha-gal syndrome, a red-meat allergy (CDC).
Range
Blacklegged ticks are widespread across the eastern US, and Lyme is most common in the Northeast, mid-Atlantic, and upper Midwest. Dog ticks live east of the Rockies. Lone star ticks are widespread in the Northeast, South, and Midwest (CDC).
Prevent
Use an EPA-registered repellent such as DEET or picaridin. Wear clothes treated with 0.5 percent permethrin (CDC). Check your body, clothes, gear, and pets during and after time outdoors, and shower soon after. Remove an attached tick right away; delay can raise the risk of Lyme (CDC).
Symptoms
Fever, chills, aches, or a rash days to weeks after a bite. See a clinician and say when and where you were bitten. Lyme often causes a characteristic rash called erythema migrans.

Brown Recluse Loxosceles reclusa

▲ T⚠ L
Range
South-central and Midwestern US. The University of Arkansas says Arkansas lies at the center of its range. In Kentucky it is more common toward the west. It is rare outside its native range and often over-reported (University of Kentucky).
ID
Tan to brown, quarter-size with legs out, no bands on the legs. A violin mark is a clue, but six eyes in three pairs is the real feature and needs a lens.
Bite
Often painless at first. Red and tender in 3 to 8 hours. Most heal in about 3 weeks. Rarely a sunken sore forms, or fever and vomiting follow. Many other conditions mimic it, including staph infections.
Care
Apply ice, raise the limb, and get medical care right away. Save the spider if you can, even crushed (University of Kentucky). Shake out shoes and bedding in infested areas.

Rabies, Raccoon Roundworm, Hantavirus

✖ D
Rabies
Bats, raccoons, skunks, and foxes are the most common infected animals. Bat contact causes at least 7 in 10 US human rabies deaths. Wash any bite or scratch with soap and water and get urgent care for treatment. Once signs appear, rabies is nearly always fatal (CDC).
Raccoon roundworm
Baylisascaris eggs in raccoon droppings become infectious after 2 to 4 weeks. People swallow them by accident. Symptoms come 1 to 4 weeks later and can include poor coordination and blindness. Wash your hands after outdoor work, and avoid contact with raccoon droppings (CDC).
Hantavirus
Spread by rodent urine, droppings, and saliva. The deer mouse is the most common carrier in the US. About 38 percent of people with breathing symptoms die. Seal gaps, trap rodents, and keep food stored (CDC).

White-tailed Deer and Chronic Wasting Disease

Disease
CWD is a fatal prion disease of deer, elk, moose, and their relatives. CDC says CWD has never been reported in people, but it is not yet known whether people can catch it.
Hunters
Do not shoot, handle, or eat a sick or odd-acting deer, or one found dead. Wear latex or rubber gloves. Avoid the internal organs, especially the brain and spine. Do not use kitchen knives or tools. Strongly consider testing the animal before eating it, and do not eat meat from one that tests positive (CDC, MDC). MDC says deer taken on opening weekend must go to a mandatory CWD sampling station; check MDC's maps for where this applies.

Gardening Notes

This region spans many USDA zones, from cold northern Great Lakes winters to mild southern Ozark and Tennessee Valley winters. The USDA map uses the average yearly lowest winter temperature, in 10°F zones with 5°F half zones. Look up your ZIP code on the USDA map and ask your extension service for local frost dates. Warm, humid summers raise fungal disease: bee balm gets powdery mildew and rust, so avoid overhead watering (NC State). Black walnut roots and leaves give off juglone, which harms tomatoes, apples, blueberries, azaleas, and peonies. Keep your garden away from walnut trees. Do not plant garlic mustard.

Legal and Cultural Notes

Legal note

Each state and province sets its own hunting, fishing, and plant rules. Check the wildlife agency for your area, such as the Missouri Department of Conservation, your state department of natural resources, or Ontario's natural resources ministry. Missouri requires hunter education to buy a firearms permit, and deer taken on opening weekend must go to a CWD sampling station (MDC). National parks bar plant collecting except what each park's rules allow. A 2010 report said ramp gathering was barred in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, even for Cherokee members (National Parks Traveler). A 2016 National Park Service rule (36 CFR 2.6) now lets parks make gathering agreements with federally recognized tribes. In 2019 the Smokies signed one with the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians for sochan, a traditional spring green, for enrolled members with permits (NPS). It does not cover ramps or anyone else. Rules change, so ask the park. Ask national forests, such as Mark Twain National Forest (about 1.5 million acres in Missouri), about permits before collecting. Ramp rules differ across Canada and in some parks, so check before you pick any.

This land is the homeland of many nations, among them the Cherokee, Haudenosaunee, Anishinaabe, Shawnee, Lenape, Osage, and Quapaw. They have cared for these forests and know their plants far better than this chapter can. Sugar maple was a main sweetener for early Native peoples and settlers. Native people also used dried spicebush fruit as a spice and its leaves for tea, and used jewelweed sap for itching (NC State). This chapter lists plant uses only as common knowledge. It does not teach sacred or ceremonial practice, which belongs to the communities that hold it. Learn from tribal cultural programs, and take little.
