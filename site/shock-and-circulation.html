Shock & Circulation

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026
On this page
  1. What Shock Is
  2. Early and Late Signs, and Tracking Trends
  3. Field Management of Shock
  4. Fainting vs. Shock
  5. Chest Injuries
  6. Heart Attack and Stroke in the Field
  7. Abdominal Injuries and Acute Abdominal Pain
  8. When Evacuation Is Days Away
  9. Sources

Shock means the body is not getting enough blood flow to keep its organs alive. It can follow bleeding, a failing heart, a severe allergy, infection, or an injured chest or belly, and it can kill even when the wound looks small. This chapter teaches you to spot shock early, track it over time, care for chest and belly injuries, recognize a heart attack or stroke, and keep a patient alive when no hospital is near. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course teaches these skills far better than any page.

Order of care

Check for danger, then airway, breathing, and severe bleeding (see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection). Call for help early (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). Nobody can cure true shock in the field. Evacuation is the real treatment. Your job is to slow the decline and get the patient to care.

What Shock Is

MedlinePlus defines shock as blood flow that is too low, so cells and organs do not get enough oxygen. Picture a pump, a set of pipes, and the fluid inside them. Shock happens when the pump fails, the pipes get too wide, the fluid runs low, or something blocks the flow (Figure 1).

TypeWhat goes wrongField examples
HypovolemicNot enough fluid in the systemHeavy bleeding, inside or out; long vomiting or diarrhea; dehydration; large burns
CardiogenicThe heart cannot pump wellHeart attack, heart failure
DistributiveVessels widen and pressure falls (wilderness texts also say vasogenic)Anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction), sepsis (infection that harms the whole body), spinal cord injury
ObstructiveSomething blocks blood moving through the chestTension pneumothorax (below)

The American Alpine Club primer names only three groups (hypovolemic, cardiogenic, vasogenic). The Merck Manual uses all four groups and lists tension pneumothorax as a cause of obstructive shock. Most backcountry shock is hypovolemic. For anaphylaxis, see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning. For sepsis, see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection.

Early and Late Signs, and Tracking Trends

The Red Cross lists these signs: a fast, weak pulse; fast breathing; pale, gray, cool, or clammy skin; restlessness, confusion, or irritability; nausea or vomiting; and unusual thirst. At first the body fights back. Heart and breathing rates climb, and skin vessels narrow to save blood for the brain and heart. This is the early, or compensated, stage. When the body runs out of tricks, the brain gets less blood and the person grows confused and drowsy (Figure 2).

Treat anyone with a serious mechanism of injury, such as a hard fall, a crash, or a blow to the chest or belly, as at risk even if they look fine. NOLS advises assuming shock until you can rule it out, because early signs look like a stress reaction. A stress reaction fades quickly; the American Alpine Club primer puts it at about 15 minutes. If the fast pulse and pale skin hang on, assume shock.

Trends beat single numbers

One pulse reading means little. A pulse that keeps rising and weakening, breathing that speeds up, skin that grows colder and damper, and alertness that slips are the real warning. The primer suggests checking vital signs every 5 to 15 minutes in a shock patient. Use the same wrist and the same method each time, and write every result down (Figure 3). Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs shows how to take each measurement and explains why capillary refill is only one clue.

Field Management of Shock

  1. Treat life threats first: airway, breathing, severe bleeding.
  2. Stop the cause. Control bleeding, splint broken bones, and cool burns (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection). Treating the injury treats part of the shock.
  3. Keep the patient warm and off the ground. Slip a pad, pack, or branches under the body and cover them from above (Figure 4). Remove wet clothing (see Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies). If they are hot, take a layer off instead (Red Cross).
  4. Position them. Lay them on their back. The Red Cross says to raise the feet about 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) only when there is no sign of injury, and to let them choose another position if it helps breathing. MedlinePlus says to keep the legs down if raising them hurts or if a head, neck, back, or leg injury is possible. NOLS notes nobody knows for sure that raising the legs returns blood to the heart, but it is harmless until shock is ruled out.
  5. Loosen tight clothing and reassure. The Red Cross notes that fear and pain make shock worse.
  6. Recheck breathing at least every 5 minutes (MedlinePlus) and log vital signs.
  7. Drowsy or vomiting? Turn them on their side. If a spinal injury is possible, roll the whole body as one unit (MedlinePlus; see Part 0 › Red Pages).
  8. Start the evacuation now. NOLS says to evacuate anyone whose vital signs do not stabilize or improve, and to hurry if alertness drops.

Fluids by mouth: sources disagree

The Red Cross and MedlinePlus both say to give no food or drink to a person in shock, because of the risk of vomiting and inhaling it. Those pages assume an ambulance is coming. For long delays, wilderness sources differ. NOLS lists oral fluids as part of shock care if the patient can swallow. The Wilderness First Responder scope of practice signed by the major U.S. wilderness medicine schools allows oral fluids only for patients with normal mental status. The American Alpine Club primer agrees, at no more than about 1 liter (1 quart) per hour. Even for simple dehydration, the 2024 AHA and Red Cross guidelines advise drinks only when there is no shock, confusion, or trouble swallowing. A search-and-rescue protocol (not a medical guideline) says to give fluids to anyone more than a couple of hours from a hospital unless they may choke, may need surgery within about two hours, or may have a stalled gut (ileus). This guide follows the cautious rule:

Small sips only if all are trueNothing by mouth if any is true
Awake, alert, swallows easilyDrowsy, confused, or unresponsive
Hospital care is hours or days awayVomiting, or help will arrive within a few hours
No injury or severe pain in the bellyBelly injury or severe belly pain (surgery or a stalled gut is possible)
Clean water (see Part 2 › Water), with salt or sugar if you have itTrouble breathing, or a head injury with falling alertness

Stop if vomiting starts. Dark or scant urine means more fluid is needed. The rehydration drink is in Part 7 › Illness in the Field.

Do not

  • Do not give alcohol, or anything by mouth to a drowsy or vomiting patient.
  • Do not raise the legs after a head, neck, back, or leg injury, or if it hurts or worsens breathing.
  • Do not leave the patient alone or wait for worse signs before calling for help.

Fainting vs. Shock

A faint is a short loss of consciousness. MedlinePlus lists common causes: dehydration, low blood sugar, a brief fall in blood pressure, and reflexes set off by pain, fear, or straining. Heart or nerve problems can also cause it.

Simple faintShock
Brief, usually about a minute or lessConfusion or drowsiness that lingers
Improves after lying flatDoes not improve, or slips back
Pulse and color return toward normalFast, weak pulse and pale, cool, damp skin persist
Clear trigger: standing, heat, fearCause such as bleeding, injury, burns, illness

This table is the guide's own summary; real cases blur it. If unsure, treat it as shock. For a faint, ease the person to the ground, lay them flat, and raise the feet about 12 inches (30 cm). If breathing stops, start CPR. Give nothing by mouth until fully awake (MedlinePlus). MedlinePlus says to get emergency help if the person stays out more than about a minute, was hurt in the fall, has diabetes or seizures, is pregnant or over 50, or wakes with chest pain, an uneven heartbeat, trouble speaking, vision trouble, or weakness. In the field, any of these means evacuation.

Chest Injuries

A hard blow, fall, or penetrating wound can break ribs, tear a lung, or let air or blood build up where it should not. Check the chest in your head-to-toe exam (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Look for wounds, bruises, and uneven movement, count breaths, and ask about pain with each breath.

Broken ribs

The Merck Manual says rib fractures usually follow a strong blow, though older people can break one in a small fall. The main symptom is pain worst on a deep breath. Shallow breathing to avoid the pain raises the risk of pneumonia. Broken ribs can bruise or collapse a lung, and broken lower ribs can injure the liver or spleen.

  • Let the patient find the least painful position, often sitting or half-sitting.
  • Do not tape or wrap a single broken rib. A search-and-rescue protocol says taping raises the risk of complications.
  • Have the patient breathe deeply and cough on a schedule while awake. Merck advises about once an hour to prevent pneumonia. Holding a pillow or jacket against the sore spot eases the pain.
  • For pain, ibuprofen or acetaminophen may help; use as directed on the label. This guide's cautious rule: skip ibuprofen and aspirin if internal bleeding is possible.

Flail chest

When several neighboring ribs break in more than one place, a loose piece of chest wall moves opposite to the rest of the chest. Breathing becomes hard and tiring, and the lung beneath is usually bruised (Merck). In a hospital it often needs a ventilator, sometimes surgery. In the field, let the patient pick a position, support the area gently with a hand or pad if that eases pain, watch breathing closely, and evacuate fast. This guide found no verified field splint for flail chest, so it teaches none.

Pneumothorax and tension pneumothorax

A pneumothorax is air between the lung and the chest wall. MedlinePlus lists chest injuries such as rib fractures and stab wounds among the causes, along with lung disease and pressure changes from diving or high-altitude travel. Symptoms are sharp chest or shoulder pain worse with a deep breath and shortness of breath. Larger ones can cause blue skin, a racing heart, and shock.

If air keeps leaking in and cannot escape, pressure builds. This is a tension pneumothorax. TCCC teaching suspects it in a person with major chest trauma and worsening trouble breathing, fast breathing, weak or absent breath sounds on one side, or shock. A SIRIUSMEDx summary adds grey or bluish skin, agitation or drowsiness, and a rapid weak pulse, and calls a shifted windpipe a late, uncommon sign, so do not wait for it.

The only cure is to release the trapped air. Needle decompression is a procedure for trained, equipped people, and a WMS magazine article says success varies widely even with training. This guide does not teach it. Your steps are:

  1. If a chest seal is on a wound, lift one edge for a moment, or remove it, to let air out (below).
  2. Sit the patient up if that eases breathing. Otherwise keep them on their back or in the recovery position (TCCC).
  3. Call for rescue and evacuate by the fastest way.

Open (sucking) chest wounds

A penetrating wound can open a hole in the chest wall, and air may move through it with each breath. Guidance has changed. Older courses taught a plastic square taped on three sides. Since 2013 the military TCCC guidelines call for a vented chest seal on any open chest wound, a non-vented seal only if no vented one is available, and close watching for tension. In a lab model, a vented seal prevented tension and a non-vented one did not. The 2024 AHA and Red Cross first aid guidelines go further for lay rescuers: leave the wound open to the air, or cover it with a clean, dry dressing that is not airtight (such as gauze), or use a vented chest seal. Any dressing can trap air, so they say to loosen or remove it if breathing gets worse. A 2015 search-and-rescue protocol still teaches three-sided tape. Because sources differ, this guide follows the AHA and Red Cross (Figure 6):

  1. Expose the wound and wipe away blood. Dry the skin if you can, because seals stick poorly to wet skin.
  2. Place a vented chest seal with the vent over the wound. Press the edges flat. A pair belongs in a serious med kit (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).
  3. With no vented seal, leave the wound open to the air or cover it loosely with clean, dry gauze. Do not tape plastic or foil airtight over it unless you are trained to (TCCC allows a non-vented seal for trained responders who can watch and burp it).
  4. Check the back and sides for a second wound, and seal it too.
  5. Watch the breathing every few minutes. If it worsens, lips turn blue or gray, or the patient grows confused, lift one corner of the seal or remove it to let air escape, then press it back on (TCCC; SIRIUSMEDx).
  6. Sit the patient up if that is easier. Evacuate fast.

Breathing trouble: evacuate now

After a chest injury, any of these means rescue or the fastest evacuation you can arrange: breathing that is faster or harder; blue or gray lips or skin; a chest wall that moves oddly; an open chest wound; signs of shock; confusion; or pain that is growing. Stay with the patient, keep them in the position that eases breathing, and recheck every few minutes.

Heart Attack and Stroke in the Field

You cannot treat either one in the backcountry. You can recognize it, start help early, and avoid making it worse.

Heart attack

MedlinePlus describes chest discomfort like pressure, squeezing, or fullness, often in the center of the chest. It can spread to the jaw, shoulder, arms, back, or stomach, and it lasts several minutes or comes and goes. Other signs are cold sweat, lightheadedness, nausea, indigestion, vomiting, shortness of breath, and unusual weakness. Signs can be subtle in women, older adults, and people with diabetes. The American Heart Association (AHA) notes that women more often have symptoms besides chest pain.

  1. Get help moving at once: call 911 where you have a signal, or start a rescue call (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). Do not let the person talk you out of it.
  2. Have them sit, rest, and stay calm. Loosen tight clothing. Do not let them walk it off or drive themselves out, because the AHA says a heart attack can trigger a dangerous heart rhythm.
  3. If they have a heart medicine prescribed to them, such as nitroglycerin, help them take it as their doctor directed.
  4. Aspirin. The 2024 AHA and Red Cross first aid guidelines say that, while waiting for help, you may encourage an alert adult with chest pain that is not from an injury to chew and swallow aspirin. Skip it if they are allergic to aspirin, a health care provider has told them not to take it, or they cannot chew and swallow. Use as directed on the label. Never delay the call for help to find aspirin. Give no other drugs.
  5. If they become unresponsive and are not breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED if you have one (see Part 0 › Red Pages).

Do not

  • Do not leave the person alone except to get help, and do not wait to see if the pain passes.
  • Do not give food or drink, other than their prescribed heart medicine or aspirin as above (MedlinePlus).

Stroke

A stroke is a blockage or bleed in the brain. The AHA and Red Cross teach the F.A.S.T. check. The CDC adds B for balance and E for eyes, making BE-FAST (Figure 7).

  1. Call 911 right away, or start a rescue call. The CDC says not to drive the person yourself where an ambulance is available, because treatment can start on the way.
  2. Note when symptoms began. The CDC says the most effective treatments are possible only if the stroke is recognized and diagnosed within 3 hours of the first symptoms.
  3. Keep the person calm and comfortable, and watch breathing and alertness. If they are drowsy, drooling, or struggle to swallow, put them in the recovery position (Red Cross). If they stop responding and are not breathing normally, start CPR.
  4. Give nothing to eat or drink, because a stroke can make swallowing hard (St John Ambulance).
  5. Do not give aspirin. The American Stroke Association says some strokes are bleeding in the brain, and aspirin could make those worse. Only a hospital scan can tell the types apart.

Signs that fade within minutes may be a TIA, or mini-stroke. The CDC says it is still a serious warning and needs prompt care. If help is far away, a stroke patient needs the fastest evacuation you can arrange.

Abdominal Injuries and Acute Abdominal Pain

The belly holds the liver, spleen, stomach, and bowel. A blow can tear the liver or spleen, which bleed inside with little to see. A penetrating wound can open the gut. The Red Cross warns these injuries are hard to spot, so suspect one whenever a person has several injuries.

Blunt injury and hidden bleeding

The Red Cross lists these signs of a serious belly injury: severe pain; an open wound or stuck object; visible organs; heavy bleeding; a belly that is tender, swollen, rigid, or bruised; nausea or vomiting, sometimes with blood; and signs of shock. A search-and-rescue protocol adds sustained lightheadedness or new shoulder pain after even a minor belly injury as signs of internal bleeding, and says to evacuate immediately.

  1. Record a baseline pulse, breathing rate, and alertness, then repeat (Figure 3). Internal bleeding shows up as a trend.
  2. Have the patient lie on their back with the knees bent, unless that hurts (Red Cross). If a head, neck, or back injury is possible, leave them as found.
  3. Keep them from getting too warm or cold, and treat for shock.
  4. Give nothing by mouth while the belly is injured or very painful.
  5. Evacuate. A bleeding belly cannot be fixed in the field.

Exposed organs (evisceration)

If bowel or other organs push out of a wound, the Red Cross steps are:

  1. Remove or cut away clothing around the wound.
  2. Do not push the organs back in, and do not press on them.
  3. Soak a clean dressing in clean warm tap water or saline and lay it loosely over the wound. Keep it moist.
  4. If you have it, cover the dressing loosely with plastic wrap or foil.
  5. Treat for shock, watch breathing and alertness, and evacuate.

Sources differ. A TCCC article (PubMed 34969144) says a trained responder may briefly try to return the bowel if the abdominal wall is mostly intact and the bowel is not bleeding or leaking, stopping after 60 seconds and never forcing it. A search-and-rescue protocol says to gently replace contents if evacuation will take over an hour. The Red Cross says not to push, and a lay rescuer should follow it. Open belly wounds also need antibiotics from a clinician (TCCC article), another reason to evacuate.

Do not

  • Do not pull out a stuck object. Pad around it so it cannot move (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  • Do not press on exposed bowel, and do not give food or drink.

Belly pain with no injury (acute abdomen)

Sudden belly pain from illness can be a surgical emergency. Appendicitis is the classic example. A NOLS case study shows how it misleads. A young man had two days of feeling unwell, a night of severe cramps, and then a dull ache. It looked like stomach flu, but a tap on his right foot caused sharp pain in the lower right belly. He declined evacuation, and his appendix had probably already ruptured. He recovered after a long hospital stay.

The NOLS guidance in that case calls for rapid evacuation when there is:

  • Pain lasting 12 hours or more, especially if constant, in one spot, or worsening
  • Guarding (tensing against touch), tenderness, swelling, or rigidity
  • Pain that worsens with movement, jarring, or foot strikes
  • Blood in urine, vomit, or stool
  • Fever above 102°F (39°C), or signs of shock

It calls for ordinary evacuation if stomach illness lasts or worsens past 24 hours, brings high fever, bloody diarrhea, or dehydration, if fluids will not stay down for more than 24 hours, or if pregnancy is possible. For routine stomach illness, see Part 7 › Illness in the Field.

When Evacuation Is Days Away

Sometimes rescue is not coming soon (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming). Then you are the care team. The goals are to slow the decline, avoid new harm, and keep moving toward help. See also Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions and Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care.

  1. Start the clock. Write down the time of injury and every check (Figure 3).
  2. Send for help early. Send two people if you can, with a note listing the injury, the time, the trend, and your location (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication). Do not send a lone traveler into bad terrain or darkness.
  3. Make a stable, dry, insulated bed (see Part 2 › Shelter). Cold makes shock worse.
  4. Check on a schedule. Every 5 to 15 minutes while unstable (the primer's range), less often once stable. Look at every wound, seal, and dressing each time.
  5. Fluids. Follow the fluids table above. If sips are allowed, give them often. Track drinks and urine.
  6. Chest patients: keep them propped up, coughing and breathing deeply on a schedule, with the breathing rate logged. Burp or re-seal a chest seal if breathing worsens.
  7. Belly patients: hold food and drink at first. If they are alert and thirsty, tiny sips are a judgment call; stop at any vomiting or growing pain. Recheck the belly for hardness and swelling.
  8. Push harder to evacuate if the pulse climbs, breathing speeds up, alertness falls, the belly hardens, blood shows in vomit or stool, or fever starts.
  9. Share the watch in shifts, and eat, drink, and rest yourself.

Know the limits. A patient with ongoing internal bleeding, tension pneumothorax, or a heart attack who cannot reach care has a poor outlook. Your best gifts are warmth, comfort, and a clear record. WFA or WFR training gives practice with all of this.
