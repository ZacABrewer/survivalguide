Great Plains & Prairies

The Great Plains are a huge, open, windy land of grass, big rivers, and fast weather changes. Water is scarce in the west and often tainted by farming in the east. Wood is scarce almost everywhere. This chapter covers the weather, water, plants, animals, and rules of the plains and prairies of the central US and the Canadian Prairie Provinces, with a special focus on the look-alikes that can hurt you.

At a Glance

Area covered
From the Rocky Mountain foothills east to the prairie-forest edge in Missouri, Iowa, and Minnesota. North to south it runs from Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba through Montana (east of the mountains), North and South Dakota, Wyoming (east), Nebraska, Colorado (east), Kansas, Oklahoma, and the Texas panhandle.
Overlaps
NA-08 (Eastern Deciduous Forest, Appalachians, Great Lakes & Ozarks) to the east: the Missouri, Iowa, and Minnesota edge is a mosaic of prairie and woods, so use both chapters there. NA-05 to the west: the foothills and Rocky Mountain slopes. NA-06 to the southwest: the dry southern Plains of the Texas panhandle and eastern New Mexico blend into desert grassland.
Climate (Köppen)
Cold semi-arid (BSk) in the west; humid continental (Dfa, and the cooler Dfb in the far north) farther east; humid subtropical (Cfa) in the southeast. Kansas alone has all three of BSk, Dfa, and Cfa (NASA). Rain rises from west to east.
Biomes
Shortgrass prairie (west, driest), mixed-grass (middle), tallgrass (east, wettest). Also sandhills, river woods, and northern prairie potholes.
Elevation
About 1,000–2,000 ft (300–600 m) along the eastern edge of the Great Plains, rising westward to about 5,500–6,000 ft (1,700–1,800 m) or a little more at the foot of the Rockies. The mountain front is lower in Canada and higher in the south. Sources differ on where they draw the edges.
Major rivers
Missouri, Platte, Kansas, Arkansas, Red, Canadian, Saskatchewan, Assiniboine, and Red River of the North.
Major aquifer
The High Plains aquifer covers about 174,000 square miles (450,000 km²) in eight states: Colorado, Kansas, Nebraska, New Mexico, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Texas, and Wyoming (USGS). Its best-known part is the Ogallala.

Seasons and Weather

Cold, dry Arctic air meets warm, wet Gulf air over open land, and nothing blocks the wind. Weather can turn within hours, so keep a weather radio or phone alert on.

SeasonTypical conditionsMain hazards
Winter (Dec–Feb)Cold, dry, windy. The north is far colder than the south. Snow cover varies.Blizzards, ice storms, wind chill, getting lost in blowing snow, carbon monoxide in stuck vehicles.
Spring (Mar–May)Fast swings between warm and cold. Storms increase in the south first, then move north.Tornadoes, large hail, flash floods, late freezes, grass fires on dry, windy days.
Summer (Jun–Aug)Hot days, often humid in the east. Storms often form in late afternoon.Heat illness, lightning, derechos, tornadoes (especially in the north), flash floods, drought.
Fall (Sep–Nov)Mild days, cool nights. Often calmer than spring. (About 40 percent of the year's rain and snow falls in June–August.)Early blizzards in the north, prairie wildfire in dry, windy weather, hunting-season accidents.

This is a broad summary. Check your local National Weather Service (NWS) office or Environment Canada for local normals.

Tornadoes

NWS says the best place in a home is a basement, a safe room, or an inside room away from windows. A mobile home or tent is not safe; get to a sturdy building right away. In a vehicle, drive to the closest shelter. If you cannot reach one, NWS gives two choices: get down in the car and cover your head, or leave the car and lie in a low spot such as a ditch or ravine.

Blizzards and winter storms

NWS advises staying in a stuck vehicle, because wind-driven snow quickly disorients you. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour, crack a window, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow to prevent carbon monoxide poisoning. Tie bright cloth to the antenna. Outdoors with no shelter, build a windbreak, lean-to, or snow cave, and melt snow before you drink it. See Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Lightning

NWS says you can usually hear thunder about 10 miles (16 km) from a strike, and lightning can strike that far from the storm. Sound travels about a mile every 5 seconds. If you hear thunder, you are in range. On open prairie you may be the tallest thing around, so move to a building early.

Derechos

A derecho is a widespread, long-lived windstorm from a fast-moving band of thunderstorms. Iowa's emergency agency says gusts of at least 58 mph (93 km/h) along a damage path longer than about 240 miles (400 km) may qualify, and winds have topped 100 mph. Go to a basement or an inside room on the lowest floor, get under sturdy furniture if you can, and stay away from windows. Outdoors, get into a sturdy building and away from trees. Campers, hikers, and people in cars are at risk from falling trees and power poles. Afterward, stay away from downed lines.

Heat

NWS notes that children, older adults, people with chronic conditions, and pregnant women are most at risk. It is never safe to leave a child, disabled person, or pet in a parked car. Work early or late, rest in shade, and drink water. See Part 7 › Environmental Emergencies.

Flash floods

Dry creek beds fill fast from storms far upstream. NWS says about 6 inches (15 cm) of fast water can knock an adult down, about 12 inches (30 cm) can sweep away most cars, and about 2 feet (60 cm) can carry away SUVs and trucks. More than half of flood drownings happen when someone drives into floodwater. Turn Around Don't Drown: never drive or walk into floodwater.

Prairie wildfire

Dry grass burns fast in wind. Leave early when officials tell you to, and plan more than one way out. If fire traps you on foot, get to the biggest patch of ground with nothing to burn, such as bare dirt, gravel, or ground that has already burned. If there is none, use the spot with the least fuel, such as a low spot, and shield yourself from the heat behind rocks or a bank. Cover your face with a cotton cloth. One UC Cooperative Extension guide says a grass fire front usually burns past a spot in about 10 minutes, and University of Wyoming Extension says trained crews work a grass fire from ground that has already burned. See Part 9 › Natural Disasters.

Water

Surface water is rare in the west and often farm-polluted in the east. Treatment steps are in Part 2 › Water.

Rivers and lakes

The big rivers carry silt, farm runoff, and town waste. The Missouri, Platte, Kansas, Arkansas, and Red Rivers in the south, and the Saskatchewan and Assiniboine in the north, cross the region. Small prairie streams often dry out in late summer, and warm reservoirs can grow harmful algae.

The High Plains (Ogallala) aquifer

USGS calls the High Plains aquifer the principal water source for one of the major farming areas of the US, covering about 174,000 square miles (450,000 km²). A USGS report also calls it a highly stressed supply.

Finding water

  • Look for lines of cottonwoods and willows. They mark creeks, springs, and shallow groundwater.
  • Look for windmills with stock tanks, farm ponds, and old homestead wells. Treat water from all of them.
  • Dig at the outside bend of a dry creek bed and let the hole seep.
  • Collect rain in tarps and containers.

Farm nitrates

Fertilizer and manure can put nitrate into wells and streams. The EPA limit is 10 mg/L as nitrate-nitrogen. Nitrate can cause "blue baby syndrome," and infants under one year are at highest risk. You cannot see, taste, or smell it. Boiling does not remove it. Boiling raises the concentration. What works is distillation, reverse osmosis, or ion exchange. Carbon filters and normal softeners do not remove it.

Warning

Do not mix infant formula with water you suspect has high nitrate. Use another source or bottled water.

Harmful algal blooms in ponds and lakes

Cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae") can form in warm, still, nutrient-rich water such as livestock ponds and reservoirs. CDC says blooms can look like foam, scum, mats, or paint on the water and can turn it green, blue, brown, or red. Some grow underwater where you cannot see them. People can be harmed by touching or swallowing the water. Animals can get very sick or die within hours.

  • Stay out of water that looks discolored, has scum, or smells bad. EPA's rule: when in doubt, stay out.
  • CDC says boiling will not make it safe and can concentrate the toxin. No source we checked says camp filters or tablets remove these toxins, so do not count on them. Choose another water source.
  • Keep pets and livestock out. If you or an animal touch bloom water, rinse off with clean water right away, before a pet can lick its fur.
  • If you feel sick, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or see a health care provider. For animals, call a veterinarian.

Fire and Shelter Materials

Open prairie has almost no firewood. Trees grow along rivers, in draws, and around old farmsteads, so gather fuel before you leave them. See Part 2 › Fire and Part 2 › Firewood & Woodlots.

Tinder and friction-fire woods

  • Cottonwood (Populus deltoides): dry, soft root and branch wood is a classic hand-drill and bow-drill wood. Fluffy seed fibers make fine tinder in early summer.
  • Willow (Salix): dry, light wood works for drills and fireboards. Dry willow fluff also makes tinder.
  • Yucca (Yucca glauca): the dry flower stalk is a traditional hand-drill spindle in the drier plains. Dead leaves also make cordage fiber.
  • Cattail fluff, dry grass seed heads, bird nests, and shredded dry bark of cottonwood or cedar are all tinder.
  • Dry bison or cattle dung ("chips") is old plains fuel. Wash your hands afterward.

These are traditional choices, and how well a wood works depends on how dry it is. Test and practice before you need it.

Wind, terrain, and shelter

  • Use dry creek banks, cutbanks, and the downwind side of ridges and hills for windbreaks.
  • Do not camp in dry creek beds or the bottom of draws. Flash floods can arrive from rain miles away.
  • In winter, NWS suggests a lean-to, windbreak, or snow cave.
  • Build fires on bare ground, never in dry grass, with water ready.

See Part 2 › Shelter.

Medicinal Plants

These plants have long traditions, but modern evidence is thin. None replaces medical care. See Part 7 › Herbal Medicine & Health Tonics and Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines. Badge: ◇ M.

PlantRatingTraditional useEvidence and caution
Purple coneflower Echinacea angustifolia◇ MPlains peoples used the root for toothache, coughs, sore throats, and burns; today, colds.NCCIH says echinacea may slightly lower your chance of catching a cold; it is unclear whether it shortens one. It is related to sunflowers, daisies, and ragweed, and some people have severe allergic reactions. USDA says wild stands are dwindling; do not dig wild roots.
Yarrow Achillea millefolium◇ MNative Americans used it for fever, rashes, bleeding, and stomach aches.Little modern evidence. NC State advises gloves because it can cause contact dermatitis. Safety in pregnancy is not established, so avoid it then. Never use it instead of firm pressure on a bleeding wound.
Willow bark Salix◇ MPain, fever.Contains salicin, the compound aspirin was derived from. Memorial Sloan Kettering says trials suggest it helps low back pain; it has not been tested for fever. Do not give it to children (Reye syndrome risk). Avoid it with aspirin or salicylate allergy, blood thinners, other NSAIDs, stomach ulcers, or asthma.
Plantain Plantago major◇ MLeaves on bites, stings, and cuts.Long folk use, little proof. Clean wounds first. NC State says eating large amounts can lower blood pressure.
Wild bergamot / bee balm Monarda fistulosa◇ MLeaf tea.Aromatic mint-family herb with square stems and pink to lavender flower heads; NC State lists the leaves as edible and used in teas. Little human evidence for any medical benefit.
White sagebrush / prairie sage Artemisia ludoviciana◇ MBurned as smudge in many Plains traditions.Not the same plant as garden sage. Many uses are sacred and are not ours to teach. Its safety as a tea or medicine is not established, so do not drink it.
Wild rose hips Rosa◇ MVitamin C tea or food.USDA says the hips are a source of vitamin C and are dried for teas and jellies (see Part 6 › Vitamin C & Scurvy). Each hip holds many small seeds; strain tea through cloth.

Warning

For any suspected plant poisoning, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 and follow instructions. Do not make yourself vomit unless told to. See Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.

Fungi

River bottoms and farm edges grow some mushrooms. Missouri Conservation says true morels appear only in spring, have a honeycomb cap of pits and ridges, and are completely hollow when sliced from cap tip to stem base. False morels have lobed, folded, or wrinkled caps and are chambered inside; treat every false morel as toxic. Slice every puffball top to bottom: the inside must be pure white and smooth, with no sign of a developing cap or stem, because young deadly Amanita mushrooms can look like puffballs. Poison Control warns that mushroom poisoning symptoms can be delayed for many hours and that cooking does not make a poisonous mushroom safe. Never eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has identified it. See Part 4 › Mushrooms & Fungi.

Animals

Game and food animals

Common game includes white-tailed and mule deer, pronghorn, rabbits and jackrabbits, squirrels, ducks and geese, pheasants, grouse, wild turkeys, and fish such as catfish, bass, walleye, and carp. Hunting and fishing seasons and limits are strict (see the legal section). Methods are in Part 4 › Hunting, Part 4 › Trapping, Part 4 › Fishing, and Part 3 › Animal Processing & Hides. Cook all game to a safe temperature (see Part 5 › Food Safety).

Warning

Diseased animals. Do not handle or eat any animal that looks sick, acts strangely, or was found dead. Wear gloves when field-dressing. See the cards for rabbits and deer below.

Prairie Rattlesnake Crotalus viridis

▲ T⚠ L
Size
About 31–57 in (79–145 cm) (Nebraska Game and Parks).
ID
A rattle on the tip of the tail. Greenish-gray to brown, with 30–55 dark blotches on the back that turn into dark bands near the tail. A rattling rattlesnake usually holds its tail up (Montana Department of Agriculture).
Where
Shortgrass and mixed-grass prairie, rocky canyons, and prairie dog towns. In Kansas and Nebraska it lives in the western half of the state; in Montana it is found statewide below about 6,400 ft (1,950 m). Active about April–October in Kansas.

Look where you step and reach, especially around rocks, burrows, and woodpiles. Two more venomous snakes live on the eastern side of the region. In Kansas, the small western massasauga (usually 18–30 in, with a small rattle) lives across much of the eastern two-thirds of the state, in grassy wetlands and rocky prairie hillsides. The copperhead (no rattle; light-edged crossbands that are narrow on the back and wide on the sides) lives in rocky woods and woods edges in eastern Kansas. Ranges differ by state, so ask your state wildlife agency which species live near you.

If bitten: Mayo Clinic advises calling 911 or your local emergency number at once, without waiting for symptoms. Move away from the snake. Stay calm and still. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing before swelling starts. Clean the bite with soap and water and cover it loosely. Keep the limb still, at about heart level. Mark the edge of swelling and the time. Do not use a tourniquet or ice, cut the bite, try to remove venom, drink alcohol or caffeine, take aspirin or ibuprofen, or try to catch the snake. See Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.

Bullsnake Pituophis catenifer

Size
The largest snake in Kansas: usually 37–72 in (94–183 cm); Nebraska lists up to 100 in (254 cm).
ID
Yellowish to light tan with 33–73 large dark blotches. The tail has yellow and black bands and ends in a point, with no rattle. Many have a dark band below the eye.
Behavior
When threatened it hisses loudly, flattens its head, takes a striking pose, and shakes its tail in dry leaves to sound like a rattle (NPS; Nebraska Game and Parks).
Where
Grassland, fields, woods edges, and rocky outcrops. Statewide in Nebraska; mostly west of the Flint Hills in Kansas.

K-State notes many bullsnakes are killed after being mistaken for rattlesnakes. They eat large numbers of rodents. Do not handle any snake you cannot identify.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Check the pupils or head shape to tell a harmless snake from a rattlesnake. Fact: You would have to be within striking range to see a pupil, and a bullsnake flattens its head to look like a viper. A buzz does not prove a rattlesnake either. Judge from a distance by the tail tip and the pattern, and if you are not sure, treat the snake as venomous.

Plains Bison Bison bison

Where
Parks, refuges, and ranches across the plains.
Danger
Yellowstone National Park says bison have injured more people there than any other animal, and they can run about three times faster than you. During the rut (late July–August), bulls bellow and fight; give them extra room.
Distance
Stay at least 25 yards (23 m) away, the Yellowstone rule. Other parks and refuges may post their own rules; follow whichever is farther.

Never walk up to a bison for a photo, even one that looks calm. If one charges, run away. Drive slowly and carefully around bison on roads.

Ticks (lone star, American dog, blacklegged)

▲ T
American dog tick
K-State: common in Kansas grasslands and forest edges, March–September. Unfed females are about 1/8 in (3 mm), brown with white mottling on the back. CDC: widely found east of the Rockies; spreads Rocky Mountain spotted fever and tularemia. Its bite can also cause tick paralysis, which usually goes away soon after the tick is removed (K-State).
Lone star tick
Females have one white spot on the shield. K-State: common in eastern Kansas and found as far west as Mitchell County. CDC: spreads ehrlichiosis, tularemia, and Heartland and Bourbon viruses, and is linked to alpha-gal syndrome.
Blacklegged tick
Adult males are almost black; females have a dark shield over the front third and an orange-brown rear. Unfed adults are about 1/16 in (2–3 mm). K-State says it is becoming more important across eastern Kansas. CDC: spreads Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, and Powassan virus, among others.
Rocky Mountain wood tick
CDC: found in the Rocky Mountain states at about 4,000–10,500 ft, so watch for it on the western foothill edge. Spreads Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Colorado tick fever, and tularemia.

Alpha-gal syndrome. CDC says growing evidence suggests the bite of a lone star or blacklegged tick may trigger an allergy to alpha-gal, a sugar in red meat. Symptoms commonly begin 3 to 6 hours after eating red meat and can include hives, vomiting, trouble breathing, a drop in blood pressure, and severe stomach pain. Most reported cases are in the southeastern US. Seek emergency care for a severe reaction.

Prevention and removal. Avoid tall grass, wear light clothes, tuck pants into socks, use repellent as directed on the label, and check your body after outdoor time. CDC says to grip the tick as close to the skin as you can with fine-tipped tweezers and pull straight up with steady, even pressure, without twisting or jerking. Do not crush it with your fingers. Wash the bite and your hands. Do not use petroleum jelly, heat, or nail polish; they can make the tick push infected fluid into the skin. If a rash or fever starts in the next several weeks, see a doctor and mention the bite.

Rabbits, Hares, and Tularemia

▲ T
Risk
CDC: tularemia (Francisella tularensis) infects rabbits, hares, and rodents, and many die in outbreaks. People catch it from tick and deer fly bites, skin contact with infected animals, drinking contaminated water, and breathing contaminated dust. It can be life-threatening.
Prevention
CDC: wear gloves when handling rabbits, hares, prairie dogs, and other rodents. Cook game meat thoroughly. Use insect repellent. Do not drink untreated surface water. Do not mow over sick or dead animals.

Do not take rabbits that act slow or look sick (see Part 5 › Food Safety). If you get a fever after handling rabbits or rodents, see a doctor and mention it. CDC says most infections can be treated successfully with antibiotics.

Deer and Chronic Wasting Disease

▲ T
Risk
CDC: CWD is a fatal prion disease of deer, elk, and moose. As of January 2026 it had been reported in wild deer in 36 states, and it may be in others. It has never been reported in people, but CDC still advises the steps below.
Hunter steps
Check your state's rules and testing advice before you hunt, and consider having the animal tested before you eat it. Do not eat meat from an animal that tests positive. Do not shoot, handle, or eat an animal that looks sick or acts strangely, or one found dead. Wear latex or rubber gloves when field-dressing. Avoid handling organs, especially the brain and spine. Do not use your kitchen knives or tools.

Rules vary by state; read your state agency's CWD page before you hunt.

Deer Mouse and Hantavirus

✖ D
Risk
CDC: in the US, hantavirus pulmonary syndrome is most often spread by deer mice. People catch it mainly from rodent urine, droppings, or saliva, often when fresh droppings or nests are stirred up into the air. Symptoms begin 1 to 8 weeks after contact: fatigue, fever, and muscle aches, then cough and shortness of breath 4 to 10 days later. There is no specific treatment, and CDC says 38% of people who develop lung symptoms may die.
Prevention
Seal holes, trap mice, and keep food put away. See a doctor right away if you are sick after rodent exposure.

Closed sheds and cabins are risky places to sleep. Before you clean one, follow CDC's steps:

  1. Open all doors and windows and leave the building for 30 minutes.
  2. Put on rubber or plastic gloves.
  3. Do not sweep or vacuum droppings, urine, or nests; that throws virus into the air.
  4. Spray them until very wet with a household disinfectant or a fresh bleach solution: 1 part bleach to 9 parts water, about 1.5 cups of bleach per gallon (350 mL per 3.8 L).
  5. Let it soak 5 minutes, or as long as the disinfectant label says.
  6. Wipe up with paper towels and put them in a covered trash can. Double-bag dead mice and nests.
  7. Mop or sponge hard surfaces with disinfectant.
  8. Wash your gloved hands, take off the gloves, then wash your hands with soap and warm water.

For a heavy infestation, CDC advises much more protection, including a respirator with a HEPA filter.

Other animal hazards

  • Spiders: CDC says brown recluses live in the Midwest and South, and black widows live throughout North America, most commonly in the South and West. Shake out boots, clothes, and bedding, and wear gloves when moving woodpiles or debris. For a bite, wash it with soap and water and apply a cool, damp cloth. Get medical care for a black widow bite, for spreading pain, or for a blister or sore that grows. See Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.
  • Mosquitoes and West Nile virus: CDC says most infected people have no symptoms, but each year more than 1,300 develop severe illness and more than 130 die. No vaccine exists. Use repellent and cover up.
  • Rabies: CDC says bats, raccoons, skunks, and foxes are the wild animals that most often carry rabies. Never touch a wild animal acting oddly. Wash any bite or scratch right away with soap and water, then get medical care urgently: once symptoms appear, rabies is nearly always fatal. See Part 7 › Bites, Stings & Poisoning.

Gardening Notes

  • Hardiness zones: The USDA map is based on the average annual extreme minimum winter temperature, in 10°F (5.6°C) zones and 5°F half zones. NDSU says most of North Dakota is in zones 3b to 4b; the southern plains are several zones warmer. Check your ZIP code on the 2023 map. Canada uses its own hardiness map.
  • Wind: constant wind dries soil and tears plants. Plant windbreaks and stake tall crops.
  • Drought and season: frost-free seasons are short in the north and long in the south, so use local frost dates. Mulch heavily, water deeply, collect rain, and favor hardy crops such as beans, squash, and sunflowers.

Legal and Cultural Notes

Legal note

Laws differ by state and province. Before hunting, fishing, trapping, or foraging, check with the state or provincial wildlife agency, such as Kansas Department of Wildlife and Parks, Nebraska Game and Parks, South Dakota Game, Fish and Parks, North Dakota Game and Fish, Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks, Wyoming Game and Fish, Colorado Parks and Wildlife, Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation, Texas Parks and Wildlife, Iowa DNR, Missouri Department of Conservation, Minnesota DNR, or the Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba provincial agencies. Most places require licenses, tags, and set seasons.

  • Private land: most plains land is private. Ask permission before you enter, hunt, or gather, and close gates.
  • Public land: national parks restrict collecting plants, animals, and artifacts. National grasslands, state wildlife areas, and provincial lands each set their own rules for gathering and campfires. Check posted rules and ask the local office before you gather anything.

Respect for Plains nations

This land is the home of many nations, including the Lakota, Dakota, Pawnee, Osage, Cheyenne, Kiowa, Comanche, Blackfoot, Cree, Arapaho, Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Omaha, Ponca, Assiniboine, and Métis peoples, among others. People have lived on these plains for thousands of years, and Plains nations hold deep knowledge of prairie plants and animals. Prairie turnip is one example. SDSU lists its Dakota name as tipsina and its Lakota name as thíŋpsila and calls it an important food for many Plains tribes. The KU American Indian Health and Diet Project describes it as a staple with strong spiritual meaning, dug in June or July and eaten fresh or dried and pounded into flour.

Credit these people, learn from them with permission, and do not treat sacred uses (such as ceremonial smoke and medicines) as how-to material.
