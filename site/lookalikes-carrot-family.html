Deadly Look-Alikes: The Carrot Family

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 11, 2026
On this page
  1. Why This Family Is So Dangerous
  2. The Species, One by One
  3. Side-by-Side Comparison
  4. Reading the Features
  5. Skin Safety: Wild Parsnip, Giant Hogweed, and Cow Parsnip
  6. If Someone May Have Eaten One
  7. The Clear Takeaway
  8. Sources

The carrot family (Apiaceae) holds carrots, parsley, celery, dill, and parsnips. It also holds poison hemlock and water hemlock, two of the deadliest plants in North America. Their leaves, flowers, and roots look alike, and a single mistake can kill. This chapter shows you how the main members differ, and why the safest rule for a beginner is to eat none of them from the wild.

Why This Family Is So Dangerous

Most plant families have a few bad actors. The carrot family mixes kitchen staples and killers in the same look. Almost every member makes small white flowers in umbrella-shaped clusters called umbels. Almost every member has lacy, divided leaves. A forager who sees "something like wild carrot" can be looking at a deadly plant.

The numbers are grim. A CDC report on a 1992 case notes that water hemlock looks like parsnips, and that it causes most of the deaths blamed on mistaking a poisonous plant for an edible one. A Cornell weed profile says poison hemlock poisonings usually involve children playing with the stems or adults who mistake it for an edible plant. An Illinois Extension article says hemlock has poisoned many foragers who took it for wild carrot or parsley.

Warning

There is no antidote for hemlock or water hemlock poisoning. Doctors can only support breathing and control seizures. If you are not 100 percent sure what a carrot-family plant is, do not eat it, do not chew it, and do not make tools from it.

Two other hazards hide in this family. Some members, like wild parsnip and giant hogweed, do not poison you when you eat them. Their sap burns your skin when sunlight hits it. You can be hurt just by brushing against them.

The Species, One by One

Rating badges: ✖ D deadly, ▲ T toxic, ☐ S skin hazard, ⚠ L dangerous look-alikes. None of these plants is recommended as food in this guide.

Wild Carrot / Queen Anne's Lace Daucus carota

⚠ L☐ S
Height
About 1 to 4 ft (0.3 to 1.2 m); one botanical guide gives up to 5 ft (1.5 m).
Stem
Green, hairy, hollow, and ridged. Hairs on stems and leaf stalks are an important clue, but some stems are only sparsely hairy.
Flower cluster
Dense, flat-topped white umbel. Often one dark red or purple floret sits in the center. A ring of leafy bracts sits under the umbel; each bract is usually split into thin, thread-like branches. As seeds form, the umbel curls inward like a bird's nest.
Leaves
Lacy, feathery, and finely divided, very much like poison hemlock's. The whole leaf tends to be narrower in outline than hemlock's triangular leaf.
Root
Slender, woody taproot with a carrot smell. It is tough and fibrous.
Habitat and season
Roadsides, fields, and disturbed ground. Blooms roughly May through October.
Hazard
Sap may cause a sun-triggered skin rash (Minnesota Extension). Its main danger is that people confuse it with poison hemlock.

This is the plant most often blamed in hemlock mix-ups. A hairy stem, shorter height, and a carrot-smelling root point toward wild carrot, but none of them proves it. The dark floret is not a test either. Arkansas Extension warns that wild carrot's purple or pink center dot is not always present and is not a reliable way to identify the plant.

Poison Hemlock Conium maculatum

✖ D⚠ L
Height
Flowering plants in their second year reach about 2 to 10 ft (0.6 to 3 m); many are 6 to 10 ft.
Stem
Smooth, hairless, hollow, and often waxy, with purple spots or blotches. Very young plants can have hairs on the stem (Arkansas Extension).
Leaves
Fern-like, finely divided, glossy green, and more triangular in overall shape than wild carrot's.
Smell
Crushed plant gives off a mouse-like or unpleasant odor.
Flowers
Small white flowers in looser, more rounded umbels, June to August. A botanical guide describes a few short bracts under the umbel that are not branched.
Root
White taproot. People have mistaken it for wild parsnip (USDA ARS).
Habitat
Roadsides, ditches, fences, and stream edges across the United States.
Toxins
Coniine and related alkaloids. All parts are poisonous; seeds and dried plants stay toxic.
Symptoms
Mouth irritation, drooling, nausea, vomiting, tremor, and confusion. Muscle weakness then spreads upward. Death usually comes from respiratory failure. The Maryland Poison Center says stomach symptoms can start as soon as 20 minutes after eating; Arkansas Extension says symptoms usually appear within minutes. In severe cases, death can come in under 3 hours without treatment.
Dose
Symptoms can follow as little as 3 mg of coniine. One reported fatal dose is 6 to 8 fresh leaves. The exact human lethal dose is unknown, and alkaloid levels vary from plant to plant.
First aid
Call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 at once. Call 911 for seizures, trouble breathing, or unconsciousness. Bring a piece of the plant for identification.

Poison hemlock is a biennial. In its first year it makes only a low rosette of leaves. In its second year it shoots up and flowers. Michigan State Extension says touching it is generally not hazardous, but its sap can cause blisters after sun exposure. Arkansas Extension reports skin and breathing reactions in some people who touched or even smelled it. Wear gloves.

Children and hollow stems

The USDA Agricultural Research Service reports that whistles made from hollow poison hemlock stems have killed children. Utah State University Extension says whistles or snorkels made from these stems have sickened and killed children. Never let a child make a whistle, snorkel, pea shooter, or straw from any wild hollow stem.

Water Hemlock / Spotted Cowbane Cicuta maculata and relatives

✖ D⚠ L
Height
About 2 to 6 ft (0.6 to 1.8 m).
Habitat
Wet ground only: stream banks, marshes, wet meadows, pond edges, ditches, and springs.
Stem
Smooth and hollow, pale green with purple streaks or spots.
Leaves
Divided up to three times. Leaflets are lance-shaped with sharp teeth. The side veins run to the notches between the teeth, not to the tooth tips. A Texas A&M source says this does not occur in other members of the carrot family.
Root
The swollen base is divided into chambers that hold a poisonous liquid. Sources describe it as yellowish, turning reddish brown in air (Texas A&M), brown or straw-colored (USDA ARS), or yellow-orange (Norway). Older plants may have clusters of fleshy roots.
Flowers
White umbels, June through fall (June to October in Texas).
Toxin
Cicutoxin, a convulsant that acts on the nervous system. It is concentrated in the roots and lower stem. Young leaves are also hazardous. Treat every part as deadly.
Symptoms
Nausea, belly pain, drooling, then violent seizures and breathing trouble. In the CDC case report, mild poisoning shows within 15 to 90 minutes. Seizures started within about 30 minutes in one man and about 2 hours in his brother. Death can come within hours.
First aid
Call 911 and Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) at once, even if the person feels fine.

How toxic is water hemlock?

Water hemlock is one of the most toxic plants in North America. The CDC's report on a 1992 Maine poisoning calls it the most toxic native plant in North America and says it causes most deaths from plants mistaken for food, and the USDA Agricultural Research Service calls it the most violently toxic plant that grows here. The Maine Department of Agriculture and the Maryland Poison Center call it one of the most toxic plants in the United States, and the poison center notes deaths after only a few bites of root. Sources differ on the ranking, but they agree on the practical point: a very small amount can kill an adult.

The CDC case shows how fast a mistake happens. In 1992, two brothers digging wild ginseng in Maine ate pieces of a root. The younger man ate three bites and died about 3 hours later. His brother ate one bite and felt fine on arrival at the hospital, then began having seizures and delirium about 2 hours after eating. He was already under hospital care when the seizures began, and he survived. The report adds that there is no antidote.

Do not

  • Do not dig or taste any wild root that grows in wet ground, especially if it looks like a parsnip, carrot, or ginseng.
  • Do not wait for symptoms before calling for help.

Wild Parsnip Pastinaca sativa

☐ S⚠ L
Height
Second-year plants grow about 3 to 5 ft (0.9 to 1.5 m).
Stem
Deeply grooved and hollow.
Leaves
Compound, with about 5 to 15 leaflets. First-year plants form a low rosette.
Flowers
Yellow, in umbrella-shaped clusters. This is the quickest way to tell it from most white-flowered look-alikes.
Habitat
Roadsides, field edges, and unmowed disturbed ground in sun.
Hazard
Sap holds furanocoumarins. Sap plus sun can cause severe blistering.

Wild parsnip came to North America as a root crop. Its roots look like garden parsnips. The USDA and a clinical toxicology reference both list wild parsnip as a plant that hemlocks are mistaken for. No extension source checked for this guide says the wild root is safe to eat, so this guide does not recommend it.

Giant Hogweed Heracleum mantegazzianum

☐ S
Height
Often 12 to 15 ft (3.7 to 4.6 m); Minnesota's extension page says up to 15 to 20 ft (4.6 to 6 m).
Stem
Hollow, 1 to 4 in (2.5 to 10 cm) thick, with purple mottling and stiff hairs.
Leaves
Deeply lobed, up to 5 ft (1.5 m) wide.
Flowers
White umbels up to about 2.5 ft (0.75 m) across, late spring through mid-summer.
Hazard
Sap and sunlight cause chemical burns. Sap in the eyes can cause temporary or permanent blindness.

Do not touch a suspected giant hogweed or try to cut it down yourself. Minnesota Extension says it should be reported. Report it to your state's invasive-species program or extension office.

Cow Parsnip Heracleum maximum

☐ S⚠ L
Height
Sources differ. Maine lists 5 to 8 ft (1.5 to 2.4 m); NC State lists 4 to 15 ft (1.2 to 4.6 m) for flowering stems.
Stem
Stout, hollow, and grooved. It can be green with purple splotches, with soft hairs and white woolly hairs at the leaf bases.
Leaves
Three large, lobed leaflets, soft and velvety, with hairs along the veins beneath.
Flowers
Flat white umbels 6 to 12 in (15 to 30 cm) across, June to July. Blooms several weeks before giant hogweed.
Habitat
Moist meadows, marsh edges, and streamsides.
Hazard
Sap holds furanocoumarins and can cause skin damage in sunlight. NC State rates the plant's general toxicity as low.

Cow parsnip is the closest look-alike to giant hogweed. It is shorter, has softer hairs, and has smaller flower heads. Because the two are easy to confuse, treat any large, white-flowered, coarse plant like this as a burn hazard until proven otherwise.

Fool's Parsley Aethusa cynapium

▲ T⚠ L
Height
Sources differ: 6 to 24 in (15 to 60 cm) in a Norwegian poison-center guide; up to 4 ft (120 cm) in a New Zealand reference.
Stem and leaves
Finely lined or striped stem. Soft, glossy, dark green leaves that look like parsley.
Flowers
White. Long, narrow bracts hang down beneath the small flower clusters, giving a bearded look.
Smell
Repulsive when fresh, according to the New Zealand source.
Habitat
Fields, road verges, waste ground, and weedy spots in towns.
Toxin
Alkaloids, including aethusin, in leaves, stem, and roots.

Fool's parsley is an annual that gets mistaken for garden parsley (New Zealand reference), and a clinical reference lists it among plants confused with poison hemlock. Older writing describes severe poisoning and deaths, but the Norwegian guide says no modern cases appear to be recorded. Treat it as toxic: "poorly studied" does not mean safe.

Reading the Features

No single feature settles an identification. Check several at once, and expect exceptions. Appearance shifts with sun, shade, and moisture.

Stems: hairy or smooth?

Stem hairs are a quick first check, nothing more. Arkansas Extension says wild carrot stems are hairy at all growth stages, though one botanical guide notes some are nearly hairless. Mature poison hemlock stems are hairless, but very young plants can have hairs.

Do not stop at the blotches. Water hemlock stems can also have purple streaks, and some other plants have spotted stems. Check hairs, height, habitat, leaf shape, and root together.

Flower clusters

Wild carrot makes a dense, flat-topped umbel, often with a dark floret in the middle and a ring of bracts underneath that are split into thin, thread-like branches. Poison hemlock makes a looser, more rounded cluster of smaller clusters; a botanical guide describes its bracts as short and not branched. The dark floret is not reliable: Arkansas Extension says it is not always present.

Leaf shapes

Wild carrot and poison hemlock leaves are both lacy and fern-like. That likeness is the whole danger. Wild carrot leaves tend to be narrower in outline, with hairy stalks; poison hemlock leaves are glossier and more triangular, with smooth, often spotted stalks. Water hemlock leaflets are long, narrow, lance-shaped, and sharply toothed. Giant hogweed and cow parsnip leaves are broad, deeply lobed, and can be huge.

Water hemlock: veins and root

Two features are special to water hemlock. First, the side veins of each leaflet end at the notches between the teeth. In most other toothed leaves, veins end at the tooth tips, and Texas A&M says the notch pattern is not found in other carrot-family plants. Second, the swollen root base is divided into hollow chambers that hold a yellowish to brownish liquid. Each feature appears in at least two sources (Texas A&M, Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station, USDA ARS, Norway's poison center).

Do not

  • Do not use the chambered root as a field test. The USDA says the root and tubers are the most dangerous part.
  • Do not rely on smell. Some sources describe a carrot-like or celery-like scent from poisonous plants, and tasting to check is how people die.

Skin Safety: Wild Parsnip, Giant Hogweed, and Cow Parsnip

These plants cause phytophotodermatitis, a skin injury triggered by plant chemicals plus sunlight. It is not an allergy, so anyone can get it. The chemicals are furanocoumarins. When sap sits on your skin and ultraviolet light hits it, the chemicals damage skin cells. Symptoms range from redness like sunburn to large blisters. Blisters can later leave dark patches of skin.

How to protect yourself

  • Wear long sleeves, long pants, closed shoes, and gloves that keep sap off your skin around any of these plants. Add eye protection and a face and neck cover if you must cut one.
  • Do not break or crush stems and leaves. Sap on clothing, tools, and gloves can spread to your skin later.
  • Keep children and pets away from them.

What to do after contact

  1. Get away from the plant and out of direct sun. Remove any sap-covered clothing carefully and bag it for washing.
  2. Wash the skin with soap and water as soon as you can.
  3. Cover the area and keep it out of sunlight. Minnesota advises at least 48 hours; the Poison Control site says 1 to 2 days. Use the longer time. Cloudy days still count.
  4. If sap got in your eyes, rinse with water for at least 15 minutes, then get medical care.
  5. Watch the skin for a day or two. Call a health care provider for large blisters, a severe reaction, or any eye exposure. A provider may prescribe a steroid cream.
  6. Call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 with any question. The service is free and open all day and night.
  7. Wash contaminated clothes separately in warm water with detergent. New York's health department advises sunscreen on the area while it stays sun-sensitive, which can last several months.

Tip

Minnesota and Michigan State Extension say wild carrot and poison hemlock sap can also cause a sun-triggered rash or blisters, so gloves are smart for these plants too.

If Someone May Have Eaten One

  1. If the person is having seizures, trouble breathing, confusion, or is hard to wake, call 911 now.
  2. Otherwise, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 right away. Do not wait for symptoms. In the 1992 water hemlock case, one man had no symptoms on arrival and began to seize about 2 hours later.
  3. If the plant could be water hemlock, get the person to emergency care even if they feel fine. Norway's poison center says any water hemlock ingestion needs hospital assessment at once.
  4. Keep the person calm and still. Be ready to start CPR if breathing stops (see Part 7 › Wilderness First Aid, and take a CPR course before you need it).
  5. Save a sample of the plant, including leaves, stem, and root if possible, in a bag. Poison center and hospital staff use it to identify the plant.

Warning

Early vomiting may protect some people, because many throw up the root. Do not count on it. Treatment is supportive and must be done by professionals.

The Clear Takeaway

Do not

  • Do not eat any wild carrot-family plant unless a qualified local expert has checked that exact plant in person and confirmed what it is.
  • Do not use a phone app, a photo match, or a single feature to decide. An Illinois Extension article says plainly not to trust apps or AI tools for this.
  • Do not make whistles, straws, or toys from hollow stems.

Beginners should leave every wild carrot-family plant alone. That means the wild "carrots," "parsnips," "celery," "parsley," and "ginseng" you may see in a ditch or marsh. The deadly members look too much like the edible ones.

If you want to learn, find a local expert, a native plant society, or an extension office, and learn in person, one plant at a time. Even then, have an expert confirm each plant in person before anyone eats it. In a survival situation, plenty of safer food choices exist (see Part 4 › Foraging Principles). A hungry day is better than a fatal meal.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: If it smells like carrot, it is safe. Fact: Wild carrot roots smell like carrot, but the USDA describes a carrot-like smell in water hemlock's toxin, and the Norwegian guide describes a celery-like scent. Smell is not a safety test.
