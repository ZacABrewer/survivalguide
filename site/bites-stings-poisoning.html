Bites, Stings & Poisoning

Researched & fact-checkedChecked Nov 16, 2026
On this page

  4. Scorpions
  5. Ticks
  6. Bees, Wasps, Ants, and Anaphylaxis
  7. Marine Stings and Bites

  11. Poison Control and No-Phone Plans

Most bites, stings, and poisonings are not deadly, but a few are, and some of the worst start slowly. This chapter covers snakes, spiders, scorpions, ticks, stinging insects, sea creatures, rabies, poisonous plants and mushrooms, and carbon monoxide, including what to do when help is days away. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course teaches these skills far better than any page.

Order of care

Check the person first: breathing, bleeding, and signs of shock (see Part 0 › Red Pages and Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). Move away from the animal or the fumes. Then give the first aid for the bite. Start planning how to reach care at the same moment, because antivenom, rabies vaccine, and most treatments for serious poisoning exist only in a clinic or hospital (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions and Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

Venomous Snakes of the World

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that snakebites kill about 81,000 to 138,000 people a year, mostly in rural Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Asia has up to 2 million envenomings a year. Venom can cause paralysis that stops breathing, bleeding disorders, kidney failure, and severe tissue damage. Antivenom is the main treatment, and WHO warns that supply is failing in parts of Africa and Asia.

Many bites from venomous snakes inject little or no venom, but you cannot tell which kind you got, so treat every bite from an unknown snake as serious.

Which snakes live where

This table is a guide, not a field key. A snake you cannot name is handled the same way every time: keep still, get care.

RegionMain dangerous snakesPoints to know
North AmericaRattlesnakes, copperhead, cottonmouth, coral snakesPit vipers: no pressure bandage. Coral snakes: the bite may not hurt at first, and serious signs can take hours.
Central and South AmericaFer-de-lance (Bothrops), bushmaster, rattlesnakes, coral snakes, palm pit vipersRattlesnakes range from southern Canada to northern South America.
EuropeAdder and other vipers (asp, nose-horned, Ottoman)Viper bites. Get care.
AfricaPuff adder, Gaboon viper, black mamba, other mambas, cobras, boomslang, saw-scaled vipersAntivenom can be scarce in many areas.

Avoid bites the simple way: watch where you step and reach, wear boots and long pants, carry a light at night, and never handle a snake, even a dead one. A dead snake or severed head can still bite by reflex.

Snakebite First Aid

Current guidance from the Wilderness Medical Society (as taught by NOLS), the CDC, and WHO agrees on the main points. The goal is to keep the person calm and still and to get them to antivenom. The Red Pages have a short card for this (see Part 0 › Red Pages › Snakebite).

  1. Get away from the snake. Do not try to catch or kill it. A photo from a safe distance is enough for experts to identify it.
  2. Keep the person calm and still. Fear and movement do not help, and not every bite injects venom.
  3. Take off rings, watches, and tight items before swelling starts.
  4. Keep the bitten limb still, with the bite at about heart level (see Regional differences below). Do not raise it high.
  5. Cover the bite with a clean, dry dressing. Wash it first only where local guidance says to (see Regional differences below).
  6. Mark the edge of swelling or tenderness on the skin with a pen and write the time next to it. Repeat as it spreads. This helps the doctor.
  7. Start moving toward care now. Do not wait for symptoms, which can be delayed. The CDC says not to drive yourself. Carry or transport the person and keep them from walking if you can, because MedlinePlus says to avoid exertion.
  8. If you can, call ahead to the hospital so antivenom is ready. In the US, Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) can also advise.

Do not

  • Do not cut the bite or suck out venom with your mouth or a suction pump. NOLS says these do not work, and WHO's South-East Asia guideline calls cuts and suction useless or harmful.
  • Do not use a tight tourniquet. WHO warns it can starve the limb of blood and cause lasting damage.
  • Do not use ice, or soak the bite in water. NOLS lists ice among unproven or discredited treatments, and the CDC says not to use it.
  • Do not use electric shock, car batteries, jumper cables, "venom stones," or herbal pastes. None have been shown to work. Some are dangerous.
  • Do not drink alcohol. The CDC says not to take aspirin, ibuprofen, or naproxen, and MedlinePlus says to give no pain medicine at all unless a health worker advises it.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: You can cut the bite and suck the poison out. Fact: This does not work and adds a wound. Antivenom is the treatment, so the best first aid is calm, stillness, and fast transport.

Pressure-immobilization bandage: only for some snakes

Some venoms spread through the lymph system. A firm wrap over the whole limb plus total stillness slows that spread. This helps with venoms that mostly paralyze, but may do harm with venoms that destroy tissue.

  • Use it for: bites from Australian snakes (including sea snakes) and North American coral snakes. NOLS names these two cases. Australia's first-aid guideline (ANZCOR) also uses it for funnel-web spiders, blue-ringed octopus, and cone shells, and for no other bites or stings.
  • Do not use it for: North American rattlesnakes, copperheads, and cottonmouths (pit vipers). NOLS says it is not recommended for India. A 2018 review says it may worsen local damage with tissue-destroying venoms.
  • Other elapids (kraits, cobras, mambas, and similar): guidance differs by region. WHO's South-East Asia guideline (2010) says trained rescuers who have the right bandage may consider pressure immobilization unless an elapid bite can be ruled out. A 2024 UN staff snakebite protocol (a reputable secondary source) suggests it for kraits and purely nerve-acting cobras when transport will be long, but not for the many cobras whose venom destroys tissue, or for vipers. India's protocol does not recommend it. So: if you are trained, sure the snake was a krait or sea snake, and care is hours away, a pressure bandage is reasonable. If you cannot tell a cobra from a viper, or local guidance says no, do not wrap tightly. Splint the limb, keep the person still, and move to care.
  1. Move the person away from the snake and have them lie down. Take off rings and bracelets. Do not take off clothing, because moving the limb can spread venom.
  2. Use a broad elastic bandage, about 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm) wide, if you have one. Torn clothing, stockings, or a towel will do.
  3. Wrap firmly over the bite first.
  4. Then wrap the whole limb, starting at the fingers or toes and going up as high as you can. Leave the fingertips or toes showing so you can check them.
  5. Make it about as firm as you would wrap a sprained ankle. It should be snug, not cutting off blood flow.
  6. Bind a splint (a stick, a rolled newspaper, a spade handle) along the limb to keep it from bending. Australian sources differ on slings for arm bites: ANZCOR uses one, but the Melbourne venom unit warns that bending the elbow into a sling can act like a tourniquet. A straight splint avoids that problem.
  7. Keep the whole person still. Carry them out if you can. Do not remove the wrap. Hospital staff decide when to take it off.

Regional differences

  • Washing the wound. NOLS and the CDC say to wash it in North America to lower infection risk. In Australia, do not wash it: venom left on the skin helps identify the snake. NOLS says washing is not recommended in India, and WHO's South-East Asia guideline says to avoid rubbing, vigorous cleaning, or massage.
  • Position. NOLS and MedlinePlus say heart level; the CDC says a comfortable, neutral position. A 2018 review says the best position depends on the venom: below heart level may slow whole-body effects but can worsen local damage. For lay care, the safe middle is heart level.
  • Time limits. An Australian review (MJA 2013) says a pressure bandage applied more than 4 hours after the bite is unlikely to help.

When help is days away

Antivenom cannot be made in the field, so your job is to keep the person alive and moving toward care. Evidence for field care is thin. These steps come from the sources above plus basic first aid.

  • Watch for signs of envenomation. Spreading swelling, bruising or bleeding, drooping eyelids, blurred vision, trouble speaking or swallowing, weakness, or trouble breathing. Write down when each began.
  • Breathing comes first. Paralyzing venoms can stop breathing. If breathing stops, give rescue breaths and keep going until you reach care or the person breathes again (see Part 0 › Red Pages › CPR).
  • Fluids and warmth. NOLS says a conscious person may take small sips of water, should be kept warm, and may lie with legs raised if showing signs of shock.
  • Check a wrap, if one is on. Look at the fingertips or toes now and then. If they turn pale, cold, or blue, the wrap is too tight. The UN snakebite protocol says that on a long trip it may be loosened carefully to restore blood flow; re-wrap more gently rather than removing it.
  • Watch the wound. Swelling can be large. Later, watch for infection (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  • If one person must go for help, send two. Leave the patient still, warm, hydrated, and watched (see Part 0 › When Help Isn't Coming).

Spiders

Almost all spider bites are harmless. A few spiders have venom that can make a person very sick. Check the CDC's list for what lives near you, and shake out boots and bedding where these spiders live.

Widow spiders

Black widows (Latrodectus) are found across North America, most often in the south and west, in woodpiles, eaves, fences, and debris. The adult female has a red marking on the underside of a shiny black abdomen. The bite leaves two small marks. The venom affects nerves. Pain, cramping, and muscle twitching start near the bite and can spread to the chest, back, belly, or whole body. Death is extremely rare, but get care for any suspected widow bite, especially for a young child or an older person. In Australia, the redback spider has neurotoxic venom too. For a redback, do not use a pressure bandage, because it can make the pain worse. A cold pack may help.

Recluse spiders

Brown recluses (Loxosceles reclusa) live in the midwestern and southern US in dry, sheltered places: under logs, rocks, and leaf piles, and indoors in closets, shoes, and attics. They bite mainly when pressed against skin. The spider is brown with a dark violin-shaped mark on the front body section and six equal eyes (most spiders have eight). The bite may sting, with a small white blister. In some people the venom kills skin tissue, which needs a doctor. The Australian Museum notes that recluse spiders are native to the Americas and that recent studies found Australian spider bites do not cause tissue death.

Funnel-web spiders and wandering spiders

The Sydney funnel-web and its relatives live in eastern Australia. Severe poisoning has been reported from southern Queensland to southern New South Wales. Bites cause immediate pain and puncture marks. Bad cases bring sweating, drooling, a racing or slow heartbeat, stomach pain, and sometimes drowsiness or coma. Apply a pressure-immobilization bandage, keep the person still, and get them to a hospital fast. Antivenom is the main treatment. For help in Australia, call Triple Zero (000) or the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.

Large wandering spiders (Phoneutria) of Central and South America have a feared reputation. No verified specific first aid is given here. Use the general steps below and seek medical care.

First aid for any spider bite

  1. Move away from the spider. If it is safe, note what it looked like. Do not pick it up bare-handed.
  2. Wash the bite with soap and water.
  3. Put a cold, damp cloth or ice wrapped in cloth on it to ease swelling, and raise the area if you can (CDC).
  4. For itching, use an over-the-counter antihistamine or hydrocortisone cream as directed on the label.
  5. Get care for any widow or recluse bite, any child or frail person, spreading pain or cramps, or any bite that turns purple, blisters, or breaks down. Watch for infection: pus, red streaks, spreading redness.
  6. Check your tetanus status (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Do not

  • Do not cut the bite or try to remove venom.
  • Do not use a pressure bandage or tourniquet except for funnel-web bites (Australia).

Scorpions

Most scorpions are not deadly, but a few species cause serious illness and deaths, mostly in young children. A species name helps less in the field than watching the person closely.

RegionMedically important scorpions
US Southwest, Mexico, Central AmericaBark scorpion (Centruroides sculpturatus) and other Centruroides. MedlinePlus says the venomous US species live mainly in the southwestern deserts. Most Centruroides species are in Mexico and Central America.
South AmericaTityus species, especially in Brazil and nearby countries.
North Africa and Middle EastAndroctonus (fat-tailed scorpions) and Leiurus (deathstalkers); also Buthus in the Mediterranean.
India, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri LankaIndian red scorpion (Hottentotta tamulus).
Southern and East AfricaParabuthus (thick-tailed scorpions).

Severe cases often go unreported and species are often misidentified, so a mild sting does not prove the scorpion was harmless (NTNU list).

What to do

  1. Wash the sting with soap and water. Take off rings and tight items.
  2. Keep the stung area still. Put on a cold pack wrapped in cloth: 10 minutes on, 10 off. Use shorter periods if the person has poor circulation.
  3. For mild pain and itch, an over-the-counter antihistamine may help if the person can swallow. Use as directed on the label.
  4. Watch closely, especially for the first 2 to 4 hours. MedlinePlus says symptoms that worsen fast in that window point to a poor outcome, and symptoms can last for days. Severe signs: trouble breathing, drooling, muscle spasms, twitching, jerking eyes, convulsions, a racing or very slow heartbeat, vomiting, restlessness.
  5. Children aged 6 and younger are at the highest risk. Get any child stung by a scorpion in a dangerous region to medical care. In the US, call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222).

Do not

  • Do not use a pressure bandage. Australian guidance limits it to snakes, funnel-webs, blue-ringed octopus, and cone shells.
  • Do not cut, use suction, or apply a tourniquet.
  • Do not put ice directly on the skin.

Shake out boots and clothing in scorpion country, and use a flashlight at night.

Ticks

Ticks climb onto you in brush and tall grass and then crawl to a warm, hidden spot. They feed for days. The risk of disease rises the longer one stays attached, so check your whole body at the end of each day: scalp, behind the ears, armpits, waist, groin, and behind the knees.

Removing a tick (CDC method)

  1. Remove it as soon as you find it. Do not wait for a clinic.
  2. Grip it with fine-tipped tweezers as close to the skin as you can. Regular tweezers or fingers also work.
  3. Pull straight out with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk it, and do not squeeze its body.
  4. If mouthparts stay in the skin, try to lift them out with tweezers. If they do not come out easily, leave them. Skin usually pushes them out as it heals.
  5. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol, or hand sanitizer.
  6. Get rid of the tick. Seal it in a container, wrap it tightly in tape, drop it in alcohol, or flush it. Do not crush it with your fingers.
  7. Check for more ticks. Write down the date and where you think you picked it up.

After the bite

  • Watch for a rash or fever for several weeks. See a doctor if either appears, and tell them about the bite.
  • The CDC does not recommend testing the tick, since a negative result can give false comfort. If you get sick, do not wait for results to start care.
  • If you can reach a doctor, ask early whether the bite needs treatment. Tell them when the bite happened and where you likely picked up the tick.

Tick-borne diseases by region

The CDC lists 16 tick-related conditions in the US, including Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, babesiosis, tularemia, Powassan virus, and alpha-gal syndrome. All tick-borne diseases can cause fever. Many also cause headache, fatigue, and aches.

  • Lyme disease is most common in the US Northeast, mid-Atlantic, and upper Midwest. The rash (erythema migrans) typically starts 3 to 30 days after the bite, on average about 7 days. It expands slowly over days and can reach 12 inches (30 cm) or more. It is often not a bull's-eye. About 7 in 10 to 8 in 10 infected people get it. Without treatment, later signs can include facial droop, heart rhythm problems, and swollen joints.
  • Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever is found in Africa, the Balkans, the Middle East, and parts of Asia south of 50 degrees north. The Hyalomma tick is the main carrier. The virus can also spread through contact with infected animal blood or tissue, such as when slaughtering livestock, and from person to person through blood and body fluids.
  • Other regions have their own tick diseases. Ask local health workers what is common where you are.

Tick paralysis

Tick paralysis is thought to come from a toxin in the saliva of an attached tick. It causes weakness or paralysis that creeps up the body and can look like Guillain-Barré syndrome or botulism. Find and remove every tick, checking the scalp and hidden places carefully. The CDC says movement typically returns within 24 hours of removal. If weakness reaches the breathing muscles, support the person's breathing and get help.

Tip

Wear light-colored long sleeves and pants so ticks show. Use an approved repellent on skin and clothes. WHO also advises avoiding tick-heavy areas in high season.

Bees, Wasps, Ants, and Anaphylaxis

Honeybees, wasps, hornets, yellow jackets, and fire ants can cause severe allergic reactions in sensitized people. For most people, a sting is painful but harmless. For some, it can become a life-threatening emergency within minutes.

Normal stings

  1. Get away from the nest or the ants. Fire ants swarm, and each ant can sting several times.
  2. If a honeybee left its stinger, scrape it off with a fingernail or a card. Do not pinch it or use tweezers, which can squeeze in more venom.
  3. Wash with soap and water. Put a cold pack, wrapped in cloth, on the spot.
  4. For itch or swelling, use an over-the-counter antihistamine or hydrocortisone cream as directed on the label.
  5. Fire ant stings bring itchy bumps and then blisters. Keep blisters clean and do not pop them.
  6. A large local reaction (swelling over about 3 inches, or 7.5 cm) peaks in 2 to 3 days. It is not life-threatening but may need a doctor.

Recognizing anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a whole-body allergic reaction. It usually starts within seconds or minutes. Look for any of these, especially after a known exposure:

  • Hives, itching, or flushing away from the sting
  • Swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat; trouble swallowing
  • A hoarse or whispery voice, or noisy breathing
  • Wheezing, cough, chest tightness, trouble breathing
  • Belly cramps, vomiting, diarrhea
  • Dizziness, a weak fast pulse, fainting, loss of consciousness

If anaphylaxis is suspected

  1. Use the person's own epinephrine auto-injector (or nasal spray) right away, as prescribed and as the device instructions direct. Read the label. Each brand works a little differently. MedlinePlus says there is no reason to avoid epinephrine in a severe reaction.
  2. Call emergency services, or send someone to. ACAAI says to get care even if the person improves after one dose, and advises people at risk to carry two doses, since one may not be enough.
  3. Have them lie flat with legs raised about 12 inches (30 cm) and keep them covered. Do not do this if they are vomiting, in discomfort, or have a head, neck, back, or leg injury. Do not put a pillow under the head of someone struggling to breathe.
  4. Give nothing by mouth if they have trouble breathing.
  5. If they stop breathing, start rescue breathing or CPR (see Part 0 › Red Pages › CPR).
  6. Pass the used device to medical staff.
  7. Keep the person under observation and get them to care. Do not let them go off alone.

Warning

Antihistamine pills are for itching and mild reactions, not anaphylaxis. Epinephrine is the immediate treatment. Anyone with a known severe allergy who travels far from help should carry two devices and know how to use them (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).

Marine Stings and Bites

Get the person out of the water first, so they do not drown. Then match the first aid to the animal; no single trick works for all of them.

Jellyfish and similar stingers

  • Box jellyfish (Chironex, tropical Australia) and Irukandji: pour on plenty of vinegar right away, then remove tentacles. Vinegar stops unfired stinging cells from firing. If you have no vinegar, rinse with seawater. Australian guidance uses ice packs for the pain. These stings need hospital care. Be ready to resuscitate if the person collapses (see Part 0 › Red Pages › CPR). Vinegar is also commonly used for the Hawaiian box jellyfish, but an AAFP review found no studies that test it there.
  • Bluebottles (Australia): do not use vinegar. Rinse with seawater, pick off tentacles, then soak the area in hot water at about 113°F (45°C) for 20 minutes, or stand under a hot shower. Test the water first, as below.
  • US East Coast sea nettles and lion's mane jellyfish: vinegar can make stinging cells fire, so do not use it. Rinse with seawater and remove tentacles.
  • Portuguese man-of-war elsewhere: the AAFP review says vinegar stops its stinging cells, but Australian guidance says no vinegar for the same animal. Since sources conflict, rinse with seawater, remove tentacles, and use hot water for pain.

Common rules for any sting: scrape off tentacles with a plastic edge or gloved hand (not bare fingers), and rinse with seawater, never fresh water. For pain (after vinegar, where vinegar applies), the AAFP review found that heat beat cold packs in trials; it lists hot water at about 109 to 113°F (43 to 45°C) for about 20 minutes. Use a cold pack if you have no safe hot water. Do not use urine or alcohol.

Stonefish, stingrays, and spiny fish: hot-water immersion

Their venom is damaged by heat, and heat also eases pain. Stingray wounds may hold a broken spine, and a stab to the chest or belly can be fatal.

  1. Get out of the water. Rinse the wound with salt water. Control bleeding with firm pressure.
  2. Soak the limb in hot water, about 113°F (45°C), for up to 90 minutes. For scorpionfish, MedlinePlus gives 100 to 113°F (38 to 45°C), as hot as can be tolerated, for 30 to 90 minutes. Test the water on the person's unhurt limb or your own hand first. It must be hot, not scalding.
  3. Keep the water hot. Add more as it cools.
  4. Stop if the limb goes numb. Heat on numb skin can burn it, which the Australian Prescriber article warns about.
  5. Gently wash with mild soap and clean water. Get a doctor to check for a spine left in the wound and for infection.
  6. Get urgent care for severe pain, chest or belly wounds, breathing trouble, a racing heart, or weakness.

Cone snails and blue-ringed octopus

Cone snails and blue-ringed octopuses carry nerve venom, and the danger is paralysis that stops breathing. Australian health services give a pressure-immobilization bandage as first aid for blue-ringed octopus and cone shell stings, with the limb splinted and the person kept still. The Australian Prescriber article stresses early breathing support in severe cases. Call for help, keep the person still, and breathe for them if they stop (see Part 0 › Red Pages › CPR). Do not handle live cone shells or small octopuses. Cone-snail steps for regions outside Australia are not verified here, so follow local guidance there.

Do not

  • Do not use vinegar on stonefish or stingray stings. Do not use a pressure bandage on jellyfish, stonefish, or other fish stings.
  • Do not rub the sting or rinse with fresh water.

Mammal Bites and Rabies

Rabies is a virus that spreads in the saliva of infected mammals, mostly through bites and scratches. Once symptoms appear, it is almost always fatal. Before that point, treatment works. WHO estimates about 44,000 deaths a year, mainly in Asia and Africa, and says dogs cause up to 99% of human cases. In the Americas, where dog rabies is mostly controlled, bats are now the main source. In the US, the CDC says bats are tied to at least 7 in 10 rabies deaths. Raccoons, skunks, and foxes also carry it. Any mammal can.

What to do after any bite or scratch

  1. Get away from the animal safely. Do not try to catch it.
  2. Wash the wound with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes, right away. This flushes out the virus. Both WHO and the CDC give this step.
  3. Cover it loosely. Stop heavy bleeding with pressure (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  4. Get medical care as soon as you can. Do not wait to see if the animal looks sick.
  5. Treatment is called post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). WHO describes three parts: thorough wound washing, a course of rabies vaccine, and rabies immune globulin or antibodies in the wound when needed. The CDC says PEP is nearly 100% effective if it is started after exposure and before symptoms begin, and works best the sooner it starts.
  6. If it can be done safely, tell health workers about the animal, and have it tested if possible.

Bats and "I'm not sure"

Bat teeth are tiny, and a bite can go unnoticed. The CDC says to ask the health department about treatment if you are unsure whether you had direct contact with an animal that might be rabid. Treat contact with a bat, or a bat found near a sleeping person, as a reason to call a health worker right away. Avoid any animal that is hurt, sick, or acting oddly, or any normally nocturnal animal seen by day.

Timing and when help is far away

The incubation period is usually 2 to 3 months but can range from one week to one year (WHO). That does not mean you can wait: the vaccine must be started before symptoms, and you cannot know how much time you have. Early signs are flu-like illness, fever, and tingling, prickling, or itching at the bite. Once symptoms begin, the disease is nearly always fatal. If you are weeks from a clinic, wash the wound at once, tend it as for any bite, and head for a clinic that stocks vaccine as soon as you can. There is no field substitute.

Warning

Do not skip care because the animal "seemed fine," the bite was small, or a pet bit you. Report any bite to a health worker.

Poisoning: The General Response

Poison control experts give the same first steps for nearly any swallowed, inhaled, or skin-contact poisoning. They apply to plants, mushrooms, medicines, chemicals, and fuels.

  1. Collapse, seizure, trouble breathing, or cannot be woken? Call emergency services now.
  2. Get away from the source. For fumes, move to fresh air and ventilate.
  3. Otherwise call a poison center (in the US, 1-800-222-1222; see Poison Control, below), even if the person looks fine.
  4. Skin: remove contaminated clothing and rinse with plenty of room-temperature running water for at least 15 minutes. Mild soap can help.
  5. Eyes: rinse with room-temperature water for at least 15 to 20 minutes, blinking often. Remove contact lenses. If pain or vision problems last an hour after rinsing starts, get an urgent eye exam.
  6. Swallowed: do not make the person vomit. Poison Control says ipecac syrup is no longer recommended. A small amount of water or milk is okay only if the product burns or irritates, and the person is awake, not convulsing, and can swallow.
  7. Save the container, the plant, or the mushroom. Note the time, the amount, and the symptoms. Write them down.

Do not

  • Do not try to "neutralize" a poison with vinegar, lemon juice, or other agents unless poison control tells you to.
  • Do not wait for symptoms. Many poisons, including some deadly mushrooms, act late.

Poisonous plants

Many plants cause harm if eaten, and some also burn or blister skin. Never eat a plant you cannot identify with certainty (see Part 4 › Foraging Principles and Part 4 › Deadly Look-Alikes: The Carrot Family). If someone has eaten one, follow the general steps above. Save a sample and note what it looked like, and keep any vomit that contains plant bits. For sap or plant juice on skin, rinse as above, and get care for blisters or burns (see Part 4 › Deadly Look-Alikes: The Carrot Family › Skin Safety).

Mushrooms and delayed poisoning

Edible and deadly mushrooms can look alike, even to experts, and they grow side by side. Medical sources advise treating every wild mushroom as potentially poisonous unless an expert has identified it (see Part 4 › Mushrooms & Fungi).

Time to first symptomsWhat it may mean
30 minutes to 3 hoursSeveral kinds: stomach upset, drooling and sweating (muscarine), hallucinations or agitation, or flushing after alcohol. Usually less dangerous, but still get advice.
6 hours or more (usually 6 to 24)The danger sign for amatoxin mushrooms (death caps and relatives, Amanita phalloides). Severe vomiting, belly pain, and diarrhea. The CDC and a CMAJ review give 6 to 24 hours; the Missouri Poison Center says about 6 to 12. Treat any symptoms that start 6 or more hours after eating as possible amatoxin poisoning.

Amatoxin poisoning moves in stages. After the vomiting and diarrhea, many people seem to get better for a day or so. This false recovery, lasting about 24 to 48 hours, is when the liver is being damaged. Some people never have it and just keep getting sicker. Liver and kidney failure follow, often 3 to 5 days after eating, and death can come 4 to 9 days after (CDC). There is no proven antidote. Care is in a hospital: fluids, monitoring, and sometimes a liver transplant. Hospitals may try drugs such as silymarin (Missouri Poison Center), though a CMAJ review says none is proven to improve outcomes. Call poison control right away, and do not wait for the symptoms to pass.

Warning

Vomiting hours after a wild-mushroom meal is a medical emergency, even if others who ate it feel fine. Get to care, take any leftover mushrooms, and tell everyone who ate them. If you are days from care, give fluids by mouth in small sips for as long as the person can drink, keep them resting, and evacuate (see Part 7 › Illness in the Field).

Carbon Monoxide

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas you cannot see or smell. It comes from burning fuel. The CDC lists sources such as portable generators, cars and trucks, small engines, charcoal grills, stoves, lanterns, fireplaces, gas ranges, furnaces, and water heaters. The CPSC adds camping heaters, lanterns, and stoves used inside tents, campers, and vehicles. Never run a generator, engine, or grill in an enclosed space.

Signs are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion. These look like the flu. Several people feeling sick together near a fuel-burning device, or feeling better once outside, are key clues. The CDC says people who are asleep or intoxicated can die before they notice any symptoms. High levels of CO can build up quickly and linger for hours after a generator shuts off (EPA).

If you suspect CO

  1. Get everyone into fresh air at once. Do not stop to pack.
  2. If it is safe, shut off the device and open doors and vents. Do not go back into a space that may be full of gas to rescue someone. Get help.
  3. Check breathing. Give rescue breaths or CPR if needed.
  4. Call emergency services, or a poison center. Symptoms can fade once you are in fresh air, but anyone who had symptoms or lost consciousness needs medical care.
  5. Keep the device off until it is checked.

Staying safe in camp or in a shelter

  • The US Consumer Product Safety Commission warns never to use fuel-burning heaters or lanterns while sleeping in tents, campers, or vehicles, and says the risk is higher at altitude. Treat a snow cave or cabin the same way. Do not run a stove, lantern, heater, or charcoal there.
  • Cook outdoors. Never leave a fuel burner running while people sleep, since sleeping people can die before they notice symptoms.
  • Install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms near every sleeping area in cabins and homes. Check the batteries each spring and fall. Replace alarms as the maker says, or every 5 years.
  • Have heating systems, water heaters, and other fuel-burning appliances serviced, and chimneys checked, every year.

For heat and cooking without fuel risk, see Part 2 › Fire, Part 2 › Shelter, and Part 5 › Off-Grid Cooking Methods.

Poison Control and No-Phone Plans

Finding a poison center

  • United States: Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222. It is free, confidential, and open 24 hours a day from anywhere in the US and many territories. Your call goes to the poison center that serves your area, and interpreters are available (HRSA). America's Poison Centers and the federal HRSA Poison Help program both list this number. For simple questions you can also use an online triage tool such as webPOISONCONTROL (triage.webpoisoncontrol.org).
  • The 1-800 number still works everywhere in the US. You may read that "Poison Control closed its call center." That was one regional center, the National Capital Poison Center (poison.org), which stopped taking phone calls on March 31, 2025, and now offers only its online tool. Calls from the area it served (Washington, DC, and nearby Maryland and Virginia) now go to neighboring centers through the same 1-800-222-1222 number, with no break in service. For collapse, seizure, trouble breathing, or a person who cannot be woken, call 911.
  • Australia: Poisons Information Centre, 13 11 26. For emergencies call 000.
  • Elsewhere: WHO keeps a world directory of poisons centres on its chemical safety site. Many serve the public as well as health workers. Numbers for other countries are not verified here, so check the WHO directory, a clinic, or the national health ministry before you travel.
  • Save the number for your destination, and the local emergency number, in your phone and on paper.

What to have ready

Age and weight, what the person took or touched, how much, when, any symptoms, and where you are. Keep the container or the plant in hand.

With no phone

  1. Do the first aid steps above, and keep a written log with times: what was taken, symptoms, and what you did.
  2. Go to care if you can. Use any signal you have (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).
  3. For slow poisons such as mushrooms, leave early. Start out before symptoms appear.
  4. Keep the person warm, resting, and drinking small sips if they can. Watch breathing and responsiveness (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs and Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).

Tip

Take a Wilderness First Aid or Responder course for hands-on practice with bites, anaphylaxis, and long evacuations. Make sure your group knows where any prescribed allergy medicine is and how to use it.
