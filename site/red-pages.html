These cards are for the worst moments. Each gives you short steps and the mistakes that kill. Read them now, before you need them, and take a hands-on first aid and CPR class.

Call first, then act

In a life-threatening emergency, call for help or send someone to call before you start care. Alone? Use speakerphone and keep working.

No signal, or no 911 at all?

These cards assume help can reach you. If you have no cell service, or emergency services don't exist anymore, do the steps here first, then go to When Help Isn't Coming for what changes when you are the rescue and the hospital.

S.T.O.P. and Survival Priorities

When: you are lost, stranded, hurt, or your plan has fallen apart.

  1. Stop. Sit down. Breathe. Panic is your biggest enemy.
  2. Think. Recall how you got here and what landmarks you passed. Do not move without a reason.
  3. Observe. Check your location with a map, compass, or phone. Check injuries, weather, light, and gear.
  4. Plan. Pick one option and think it through. If you are not confident in the route, hurt, tired, or it is almost dark, stay put.
  5. Fix threats in this order: treat life-threatening injuries first, then breathing and warmth, then shelter from wind, rain, and cold, then water, then food.
  6. Call for help (next card).

Rule of 3s (rough rule of thumb only): about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather, 3 days without water, 3 weeks without food. Real limits vary widely, and cold water, heat, injury, or illness can shorten them. Use it to rank problems, not to relax.

Do not

  • Do not wander "to see what's over the next hill." Moving makes you harder to find.

Getting Help

When: someone is badly hurt or sick, you are in danger, or you cannot get yourself out.

  1. Dial your emergency number: 911 (United States and almost all of Canada), 112 (free from any phone in all EU countries, and works in many others), or 999 (United Kingdom; 112 also works there). Check the number before you travel.
  2. Say where you are first. Give a street address, or trail name, landmark, and phone GPS coordinates if you have them.
  3. Give the phone number you are calling from, what happened, and the person's injuries or symptoms.
  4. Stay on the line. Many centers coach you through CPR, choking, and bleeding control.
  5. No voice call? Try again from higher ground or an open area. Where it is offered, text to 911, but call if you can. If texting is not offered, you get an automatic bounce-back message.
  6. No signal at all? A registered 406 MHz personal locator beacon (PLB) sends your distress to search and rescue when you switch it on by hand. Its battery lasts at least 24 hours. Newer iPhones (14 or later) can connect to emergency services by satellite in supported countries if you are outside with a clear view of the sky and horizon.

Do not

  • Do not hang up early, even after a mistaken call. Explain it to the call-taker.
  • Do not count on text-to-911 or satellite features without checking them first. Coverage and rules vary.

Severe Bleeding

When: blood spurts, flows without stopping, pools, or soaks through cloth. Losing about half a soda can of blood is serious.

  1. Call for help. Put on gloves if you have them. Do not delay if you do not.
  2. Press hard with a clean cloth or dressing directly on the wound. Keep pressing.
  3. If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top. Do not lift off the first layer.
  4. Arm or leg, still bleeding? Put a tourniquet 2 to 3 inches (5 to 8 cm) above the wound, between the wound and the heart. Never on a joint or on the wound. If that spot lands on the knee or elbow, go just above the joint (Figure 1).
  5. Pull the strap tight, then twist the rod until the bleeding stops or it will not turn further. It will hurt. Lock the rod in its clip. If bleeding continues, put a second tourniquet above the first.
  6. Neck, shoulder, armpit, groin, or a wound a tourniquet cannot reach? Push gauze or clean cloth into the wound until it is filled, then press hard on it with both hands until help arrives.
  7. Write the time on the person or the tourniquet and tell the responders. Keep the person warm and treat for shock.

Do not

  • Do not loosen or remove a tourniquet. Only medical staff should.
  • Do not stop tightening because the person is in pain.
  • Do not pull out an embedded object. Pad around it, and put any tourniquet above it.

CPR and AED (Adults)

When: an adult or teen collapses suddenly, will not respond, and is not breathing or only gasps.

  1. Shout and tap. If there is no response and no normal breathing, call 911 or have someone call. Send someone for an AED.
  2. Lay the person on their back on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside them.
  3. Put the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone. Put your other hand on top (Figure 2).
  4. Keep your arms straight with your shoulders over your hands. Push down at least 2 inches (5 cm), but not more than about 2.4 inches (6 cm), at 100 to 120 pushes per minute. Let the chest rise fully between pushes.
  5. As soon as an AED arrives, switch it on and follow its voice. Bare the chest. Put one pad on the upper right chest and one on the lower left side, a few inches below the armpit. Say "clear," make sure nobody touches the person, and press shock if told to.
  6. Start pushing again right away after the shock. Keep pauses under 10 seconds. Continue until emergency responders take over or the person clearly wakes up.

Hands-only is right for a teen or adult who suddenly collapses. Breaths matter most for infants, children, drowning, drug overdose, or a collapse caused by a breathing problem. If you are trained, give 30 pushes, then 2 breaths. If you cannot give breaths, keep pushing. Child and infant CPR differs in hand position and depth. Learn it in a class.

Do not

  • Do not be afraid of doing it imperfectly. Imperfect CPR is better than none.
  • Do not rely on this card alone. Get certified through the American Heart Association or the Red Cross.

Choking (Adult)

When: a person cannot speak, cough, or breathe. They may clutch the throat, look panicked, or turn pale or blue.

  1. Ask them to cough. If the cough is strong, encourage it and stay close. If the cough is weak or absent, act and have someone call 911.
  2. Stand to the side and slightly behind. Hold across the chest with one arm and lean them forward.
  3. Give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  4. Still stuck? Stand behind them. Make a fist with the thumb side against the belly, just above the navel. Cover it with your other hand. Pull sharply inward and upward 5 times.
  5. Repeat sets of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or the person stops responding.
  6. If they become unresponsive, lower them to the ground and start CPR. Look in the mouth after each set of compressions. Remove an object only if you can see it.

Pregnant, or too large to wrap your arms around? Give chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts. Infants are different: hold the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the body. Give 5 back blows, then turn the baby face up and give 5 chest thrusts in the center of the chest, just below the nipple line. The AHA (2025) teaches the heel of one hand for these thrusts; the Red Cross teaches two fingers. No abdominal thrusts on infants.

Older AHA courses taught abdominal thrusts alone. Since 2025 the AHA and the Red Cross both teach back blows first.

Do not

  • Do not sweep a finger blindly in the mouth. You can push the object deeper.
  • Do not use abdominal thrusts on an infant.
  • See a doctor afterward, even if the object came out.

Shock

When: after bleeding, burns, severe injury, or illness: fast weak pulse, fast breathing, pale or gray cool clammy skin, confusion or restlessness, unusual thirst, nausea.

  1. Call 911. Shock can be fatal.
  2. Fix the cause if you can, starting with severe bleeding.
  3. Have them lie flat on their back. If there is no sign of trauma or injury, raise their feet about 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) (Figure 3). If they breathe better or feel better in another position, let them stay in it.
  4. Keep them warm with a blanket or jacket, and insulate them from the cold ground. If they are hot, remove a layer.
  5. If they are drowsy or vomiting, or you must leave them, roll them into the recovery position (Figure 4).
  6. Calm them. Recheck breathing often.

Do not

  • Do not give food or drink. Vomiting can block the airway.
  • Do not raise the legs after an injury or any trauma.

Hypothermia

When: cold, wet, or windy conditions plus shivering, clumsy hands, slurred speech, or confusion. Severe: shivering stops, the person becomes drowsy or unresponsive, or breathing and pulse slow.

  1. Get them out of wind, rain, and water. Call for help if signs are more than mild.
  2. Handle gently, especially if confused or not shivering. Keep them lying flat. Rough handling can trigger heart rhythm problems.
  3. Insulate from the ground first: a pad, pack, rope coil, or thick layer of branches. Cover the head. Wrap the whole body, including a waterproof outer layer.
  4. Wet clothing: once you are in a warm, sheltered place with dry layers ready, remove it gently (cut it off if needed) and dry the skin. Until then, leave it on and wrap the whole person in insulation and a waterproof layer.
  5. Warm the core first: chest, armpits, back, neck, and groin. Use warm (not hot) bottles or packs wrapped in cloth, or skin-to-skin contact under dry covers. Check the skin for burns every 20 to 30 minutes.
  6. Only if they are fully alert and can swallow: give small sips of a warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drink.
  7. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED. Check breathing and pulse for up to a full minute, because a slow pulse is hard to feel. Keep going until help arrives.

Sources differ: the Red Cross and CDC say to remove wet clothing. The Wilderness Medical Society says to wait until the person is somewhere warm. Step 4 follows the more cautious advice.

Do not

  • Do not give alcohol or caffeine, and do not give anything by mouth to someone drowsy.
  • Do not rub, massage, or warm the arms and legs first. This can send cold blood to the core and make things worse.
  • Do not let a moderately or severely hypothermic person walk or exert themselves.

Frostbite

When: fingers, toes, nose, or ears are numb and look white, gray-yellow, waxy, or feel hard.

  1. Get to shelter. Treat hypothermia first if present.
  2. Remove wet or tight clothing and rings. Keep the person warm and dry.
  3. Can it refreeze? If there is any chance the part will freeze again, do not thaw it. A thaw-refreeze cycle is worse than staying frozen. Protect it, and get to care.
  4. Thaw it in the field only if it will stay warm and medical care is more than about 2 hours away. Soak the part in water at 98.6 to 102°F (37 to 39°C) for about 30 minutes, until it turns red or purple and feels soft. Use a thermometer if you have one. If not, the water should feel warm, not hot, to an uninjured hand. It will hurt as it thaws.
  5. Air dry or blot gently. Pad loosely with dry bulky dressing. Raise the limb if you can.
  6. Get medical care as soon as possible. The Wilderness Medical Society also lists ibuprofen for field use, if you can safely take it. Follow the label.

Do not

  • Do not rub or massage frostbitten skin.
  • Do not warm it with a fire, stove, heater, heating pad, or electric blanket. Numb skin burns easily.
  • Do not walk on frostbitten feet unless you must.

Heat Exhaustion and Heat Stroke

When: hot weather or hard exertion. Exhaustion: heavy sweat, cool clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea. Stroke: confusion, slurred speech, seizure, fainting, or a body temperature above about 103°F (39.4°C). Skin may be hot and dry or damp. This is an emergency.

  1. Suspect heat stroke? Call 911 now. Start cooling while you wait. Do not wait for the ambulance.
  2. Get the person out of the sun. Take off extra clothing.
  3. Fastest cooling: immerse them up to the neck in cold water (35 to 60°F, 2 to 15°C) and stir it. Keep them in for about 15 minutes, or until they are no longer confused.
  4. No tub or stream? Use cold showers or hose water, soaked towels, and ice packs on the neck, armpits, and groin. Keep this up for 20 minutes, or until they improve or EMS takes over.
  5. Heat exhaustion only? Move to shade or air-conditioning. Loosen clothes. Apply cool wet cloths. Give slow sips of cool water or a sports drink if they are alert.
  6. If an exhausted person vomits, gets worse, or does not improve within an hour, treat it as an emergency and get medical help.

Fans: the Red Cross suggests misting and fanning. The NWS warns fans can backfire when the heat index is very high (high 90s °F or more). Then rely on immersion and cold wet cloths.

Do not

  • Do not give drinks to someone with signs of heat stroke, or to anyone confused or unconscious.
  • Do not give salt tablets.
  • Do not give fever medicines for heat stroke. They do not work for it.

Snakebite

When: bitten by any snake you cannot clearly identify as harmless.

  1. Move away from the snake. Do not try to catch it or kill it. A photo from a safe distance helps.
  2. Call 911 and get to a hospital fast. Antivenom is the treatment. Do not drive yourself, because the bite can cause fainting.
  3. Keep the person calm and still. Take off rings and watches before swelling starts.
  4. Splint the bitten limb loosely to keep it still. Do not wrap it tightly.
  5. Wash with soap and water if you can, and cover with a clean, dry dressing.
  6. Mark the edge of the swelling on the skin and write the time next to it. Update the mark as it spreads.

Limb position (sources differ): the Wilderness Medical Society says keep it at heart level. CDC/NIOSH says a neutral, comfortable position, and an older CDC disaster page says below the heart. All agree: never raise it above the heart. About heart level is the safe middle.

Do not

  • Do not use a tourniquet, tight pressure bandage, ice, or electric shock. For North American pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths), the Wilderness Medical Society advises against all of them. Pressure bandaging is used only for certain snakes elsewhere, such as in Australia, so follow local guidance there.
  • Do not cut the bite or suck out venom. Do not use a "suction kit."
  • Do not drink alcohol. Avoid aspirin, ibuprofen, and naproxen.

Stings and Allergic Reactions

When: stung by a bee, wasp, hornet, or ant; bitten by a spider; stung by a scorpion; or any sudden allergic reaction.

  1. Scrape or pull out any stinger quickly with a fingernail or tweezers. Speed matters more than technique.
  2. Wash with soap and water. Put a cold pack, wrapped in cloth, on the sting.
  3. Watch for anaphylaxis: swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or neck; hives; trouble breathing; dizziness; weak fast pulse; fainting.
  4. If it happens, use their prescribed epinephrine now. Auto-injector: remove the cap and press it firmly against the middle of the outer thigh, at a right angle. Hold it for 3 seconds after the click (or as the label says), then rub the spot for 10 seconds. Nasal spray: put the tip in one nostril and press the plunger.
  5. Call 911 even if they improve. Alone with a phone? Call on speaker while you give care. No phone? Give the epinephrine first, then go for help. If symptoms continue after 5 to 10 minutes and help has not arrived, give a second dose from a new device.
  6. If they show signs of shock, have them lie down. Otherwise help them sit comfortably. Give the used injector to the paramedics.
  7. Spiders and scorpions: wash the bite and apply a cool, damp cloth. Get medical care for any scorpion sting and any black widow or brown recluse bite. Call 911 for trouble breathing, seizures, rigid muscles, drooping eyelids, heavy sweating, or numbness.

Do not

  • Do not wait to "see if it gets worse" before using epinephrine.
  • Do not use a tourniquet, cutting, suction, or electric shock on scorpion stings or spider bites. Do not put ice straight on the skin. A cold pack or ice wrapped in cloth is fine.

Poisoning and Carbon Monoxide

When: someone swallowed, inhaled, or touched a plant, mushroom, chemical, or medicine. Or several people have headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion near a fuel-burning device.

  1. Collapse, seizure, trouble breathing, or cannot be awakened? Call 911.
  2. Otherwise call Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222 (United States only; free, open 24 hours a day). Call even if the person looks fine.
  3. Have ready: age, weight, what and how much, when, symptoms, and your location.
  4. Skin: remove contaminated clothes and rinse with water for at least 15 minutes. Eyes: rinse with room-temperature water for 15 to 20 minutes.
  5. Keep the container, the plant, or the whole mushroom. If the person vomits after eating a plant, save some of the vomit. Experts use these to identify the cause.
  6. Carbon monoxide (CO): get everyone into fresh air now. Open doors, switch off the device, and leave. Call 911. Even those who feel better need a doctor.
  7. Wild mushroom: symptoms can be delayed many hours. Call right away.

Do not

  • Do not make the person vomit unless poison control tells you to.
  • Do not try to "neutralize" a poison with vinegar, lemon juice, or anything else unless poison control tells you to.
  • Do not enter a gas-filled space to rescue someone. Alert others and get help first.
  • Do not use grills, generators, camp stoves, or gas ovens for heat in a closed space or vehicle.

Fastest Safe Water

When: your only water is a stream, a pond, a flooded supply, or an unknown tank.

  1. If it is cloudy, let it settle. Then pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter.
  2. Boil: hold a rolling boil for 1 minute. Above 5,000 feet (1,500 m), boil for 3 minutes. That is the EPA's line. The CDC uses 6,500 feet (2,000 m). This card uses the lower, safer number.
  3. No fire? Use plain bleach (regular, unscented, labeled for disinfection, and less than a year old) at the dose in the table. Stir.
  4. Wait 30 minutes. It should smell slightly of chlorine. If not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
  5. Not sure if your bleach is 6% or 8.25%? Use the CDC dose for any 5–9% bleach: 8 drops per gallon.

Do not

  • Do not use scented, "color-safe," or cleaner-added bleach.
  • Do not rely on bleach or iodine alone for Cryptosporidium. Boil or use a proper filter.
  • Do not drink water that smells of fuel or chemicals. Boiling does not remove them.

Fastest Reliable Fire

When: you need warmth, a signal, or boiled water, and you have a lighter or matches.

  1. Choose a dry spot out of the wind. Clear to bare dirt. Do not build under a tree that holds snow.
  2. Collect all three fuels first, in three separate piles. Gather far more than you think you need.
    • Tinder: bone-dry, fine material that catches a spark, such as shredded bark, dry grass, pine needles, or wood shavings.
    • Kindling: small dry twigs and thin splits.
    • Fuel: dry dead branches. The inside of wet dead wood is often dry. Split it.
  3. Build a low pyramid of tinder and kindling with a small gap to light through. Put a rock or log wall behind it to cut wind and reflect heat toward you.
  4. Shield the flame with your body. Light the low windward side. Add small pieces first, then bigger ones.
  5. Keep the fire small. Never leave it unattended. Put it out with water, and stir the ashes until cool.

Carbon monoxide kills

Never burn wood, charcoal, or a stove inside a closed shelter, tent, snow cave, or vehicle. Carbon monoxide has no smell or color. Keep fires outside or in a properly vented fireplace.

Do not

  • Do not use gasoline to start a fire.
  • Do not start a fire during a burn ban or high fire danger unless your life is at risk.

Signaling for Rescue

When: you cannot reach help by phone and search aircraft or ground teams may be near.

  1. Think in threes. Three whistle blasts, three flashes, or three fires are widely read as a call for help.
  2. Whistle: three blasts, a pause, then repeat. It saves energy compared with shouting.
  3. Mirror: flash it toward aircraft, the horizon, or ground searchers, even if you hear nothing. A glass signal mirror can be seen for miles in sun. A metal one is far weaker.
  4. Fire and smoke: build three fires in a triangle (Figure 5). At night, flames show. By day, add green vegetation for thick smoke. Do this only if wildfire risk allows.
  5. Ground symbols: in an open area, lay out the code in Figure 6. Make each symbol at least 10 feet (3 m) tall, and as big and contrasting as you can. On snow, stamp, drag, or shovel trenches. They look dark from the air.
  6. If you are unsure which symbol to use, make an SOS. A pilot acknowledges your message by rocking the wings side to side.

Do not

  • Do not move far from your last known position to signal. Signal from a visible clearing near your shelter.
