Cordage, Knots & Lashings

Researched & fact-checked3AChecked Nov 11, 2026
On this page
  1. Rope Basics
  2. Making Cordage
  3. Essential Knots
  4. Lashings
  5. Safety, Inspection, and Retiring Rope
  6. Sources

Rope and cord let you build shelters, hang gear, lash poles, set snares, and rescue gear from a cliff or a river. This chapter covers how rope works, how to make your own cord from plants, 18 working knots, and the lashings that turn sticks into frames. It also says plainly where a page stops and real training must start.

Never trust your life to a knot learned from a page

Do not use anything here for climbing, rappelling, rescue, or hauling a person. A knot tied slightly wrong can look right and still fail. Learn life-safety systems in person from a qualified instructor, and have them check your work. The figures in this chapter are simplified drawings, so tie each knot with real rope while you follow the steps and compare it with a trusted video or instructor.

Rope Basics

Parts of a rope

Knot instructions use a few fixed words. Learn them once and every set of steps gets easier to follow.

  • Standing part: the long, unworked length of rope, usually the part that carries the load.
  • Working end (tail): the end you use to tie the knot.
  • Bight: a U-shaped bend in the rope that does not cross itself.
  • Loop: a bend where the rope crosses itself and closes a ring.
  • Turn and round turn: a turn is one pass around an object. A round turn goes all the way around, so both parts leave on the same side.

Natural and synthetic fiber

Natural rope comes from plant fibers such as manila (from abaca), hemp, cotton, jute, and agave. A US Navy training manual notes that manila is weak in salt water and shrinks when wet. Synthetic rope has replaced it in most uses. The same Navy sources list nylon, polyester, polypropylene, and aramid fibers.

  • Nylon: strongest for its size, stretches (up to about 40%), can be stored wet.
  • Polyester: less strong and stretchy than nylon; can chafe.
  • Polypropylene: floats and is cheap, but weakens in sunlight and can cut skin.
  • Manila and other natural fibers: do poorly in salt water, shrink when wet, and rot if stored damp.

Knots weaken rope

Every knot bends and crushes the fibers, so a knotted rope is weaker than the same rope straight, and it usually breaks at the knot. Exact numbers vary with the fiber, how the rope is made, whether it is wet, and how well the knot is tied.

Lab tests reported by a rescue-gear maker (CMC Rescue) on half-inch nylon and polyester rescue rope found common knots keeping only about 49% to 82% of the rope's strength. The double fisherman's kept about 80%, the figure-8 loop about 54% to 80%, and the bowline about 58% to 75%. Treat these as rough figures for new, clean rope pulled in a lab. Old, worn, dirty, or badly tied rope can do worse.

Plan with a big safety margin

One knot site says to assume even brand-new rope will perform at no more than half its rated breaking strength once you tie it, and it cites industry guidance of 5:1 to 12:1 design factors, with 12:1 for live or critical loads. For camp work, pick rope much stronger than you think you need.

Care of rope

  • Dry rope before you store it. Keep it clean, coiled, and out of the way.
  • Protect it from abrasion and sharp edges. Replace worn or frayed line.
  • Keep polypropylene out of the sun when you can, since sunlight damages it.
  • Whip the ends (bind them with thin cord) or melt synthetic ends so they do not unravel.
  • Keep rope away from chemicals. A climbing-gear maker warns that acid, such as from a car battery, can ruin a rope without leaving any visible sign.

Making Cordage

You can make strong cord from many plants. The part you want is usually the bast: the fibrous inner bark or the stringy fibers in a stem or leaf. They must be separated from the woody core, either by retting (letting microbes break down the glue between fibers) or by stripping dry stalks by hand.

Fiber plants by region

Fiber plants can hurt you

Dogbane and milkweed contain cardiac glycosides and are poisonous if eaten. Dogbane sap can blister skin, and milkweed sap can irritate skin and eyes. Nettle stings. Agave sap can cause severe rash, and agave and yucca have needle-sharp leaf tips. Wear gloves, wash up afterward, and never put fiber in your mouth. Confirm plant identity with an expert (see Part 4 › Foraging Principles).

Common milkweed Asclepias syriaca

Part used
Fibers from the dry stalks.
Hazard
Cardiac glycosides are present in all parts of milkweeds, and they are poisonous to people and livestock. NC State Extension says the milky sap can cause skin rash and eye irritation.

A USDA guide says to gather stalks in late fall or early winter after they dry, split them, remove the bark, rub the fibers loose, and roll them into cord on your thigh.

Stinging nettle Urtica dioica

When
Late summer to early fall, while stalks are full-grown and still strong.
Hazard
Stinging hairs on leaves and stems. Nettles can also take up pollutants from soil.

A Washington State education guide says to wear gloves, cut the longest stalks, strip the leaves, split each stem lengthwise, flatten and beat it, and pull out the outer fibers. Dry them before use, since they shrink a lot. Soak dried stalks first. Harvest away from roads and sprayed fields.

Basswood, linden Tilia americana

Part used
Inner bark (bast).

A USDA guide says people used the inner bark fiber for rope, mats, fish nets, and baskets, and ate the young leaves. A US Forest Service page notes that knots tied in wet basswood twine tightened as it dried. Strip bark only from a tree you may cut.

Yucca and agave Yucca, Agave

☐ S
Part used
Leaf fibers.
Hazard
Sharp leaf tips and spines; yucca leaf tips have caused eye injuries. Sap from many agaves can cause severe dermatitis, and a few people react to yucca on the skin.

Banana yucca (Yucca baccata) was preferred for fiber strength. The Forest Service says leaves were soaked, pounded, and rinsed to free the strands, which were twisted into thread and rope. Agave leaves give sisal and henequen. Trim spines first and wear gloves.

Other fiber sources. The Forest Service lists western red cedar (Thuja plicata) bark fibers as material for rope and mats. Flax and hemp are the best-known cultivated bast fibers, and growing flax gives a steady supply of cord. Dry stalks are retted (softened by moisture and microbes), then broken and combed to free the fiber. Check local rules before planting any fiber crop.

Preparing the fiber

  1. Gather mature, dry stalks or strips of inner bark, wearing gloves.
  2. Soak dry material in water until it bends without snapping.
  3. Split stalks lengthwise. Flatten them with a smooth stick or stone.
  4. Break and peel the outer fibers away from the woody core. Keep them as long as you can.
  5. Dry the fibers fully before you twist them. They shrink as they dry, and cord made from damp fiber loosens.
  6. Sort the fibers by length and thickness. Pull out any bits of woody core.

The reverse-wrap (two-ply) method

The reverse wrap is the standard way to make strong cord by hand. The trick is two opposite twists. Each strand is twisted one way, and the two strands are wrapped around each other the other way. The opposite twists lock against each other, so the cord does not untwist.

  1. Take a small bundle of fiber about as long as your forearm. Pinch it in the middle with your left hand, or pinch one end for a long start.
  2. Split the bundle into two equal strands.
  3. With your right hand, twist the near strand clockwise until it kinks back on itself.
  4. Twist the second strand the same way.
  5. Lay the first twisted strand over the second, turning the other way (counterclockwise). The strand that was on top is now below.
  6. Repeat the twist and cross, moving your pinch point along as the cord grows. Keep the same direction every time.
  7. Keep both strands equal in thickness and keep the tension even.

Adding new fiber (splicing)

When a strand thins, lay new fiber alongside it so they overlap by several inches, and twist them together. Stagger the joins so both strands never splice at the same place. Finish the end with an overhand knot.

Braiding and simple rope making

For flat, soft cord, braid three strands: right strand over the middle, then left over the new middle, and repeat. Add fiber as a strand thins. For thicker rope, make several two-ply cords, twist each one way, then twist them all together the other way. A helper at the far end saves time. Hand-made natural cord is weaker than synthetic cord of the same thickness, so make it thicker than you think you need and test it with a small load.

Essential Knots

Learn a few knots very well instead of many knots poorly. Each entry says what the knot is for, how to tie it, how to check it, and where it fails. Dress every knot (make the strands lie neat and parallel), pull it snug, test it, and leave a long tail on any knot you rely on, and a much longer one for anything critical.

Stopper knots

Overhand knot

For: keeping a rope end from slipping through a hole. Tie: (1) Make a loop. (2) Pass the end through it. (3) Pull tight. Weakness: it jams hard. A Navy seamanship guide says to use it sparingly.

Figure-8 knot

For: a bigger, more stable stopper. It does not bind, even when jammed. Tie: (1) Pass the tail over the standing part to make a loop. (2) Bring the tail under and around the standing part. (3) Pass it down through the loop. (4) Pull tight. Check: it should look like a neat 8. Weakness: it can fall undone if shaken, so leave a tail.

Binding knots

Square (reef) knot

For: light jobs such as tying a bundle or bandage. Tie: (1) Cross the left end over the right and wrap it under. (2) Cross the right end over the left and wrap it under. (3) Pull both ends. Check: the two bights lie flat and interlocked, and each end lies beside its own standing part, with both ends on the same side of the knot. If the ends stick out crosswise, you tied a granny. Untie it and start again.

Do not use a square knot to join ropes under load

  • It can slip, come undone, or jam, and it spills if one rope is pulled sideways.
  • The classic Ashley Book of Knots, as cited by Animated Knots, warns that using it as a bend (rope joiner) has probably cost more lives than the failure of any half dozen other knots combined.
  • A granny is even weaker.

To join ropes, use a sheet bend or double fisherman's.

Constrictor knot

For: closing a sack neck, binding a bundle, or whipping a rope end. Tie: (1) Take one turn around the bundle, crossing the end over the standing part to make a diagonal (the riding turn). (2) Take the end around behind the bundle again. (3) Pass it over the standing part, then tuck it under the riding turn, so both ends come out between the two turns. (4) Pull both ends hard. Weakness: it often cannot be untied, so plan to cut it.

Joining knots (bends)

Sheet bend

For: joining two ropes, especially of different thickness. Tie: (1) Make a bight in the thicker rope. (2) Pass the thinner rope up through the bight. (3) Take it behind both legs of the bight. (4) Tuck it under its own turn. (5) Pull tight. For ropes very different in size, take the thinner rope around twice. Check: both tails lie on the same side. Weakness: for critical loads the tails must be much longer, and both ropes must be slack while you tie it.

Double fisherman's bend

For: joining ropes of similar size, and tying a cord into a loop. Tie: (1) Overlap the two ends. (2) Wrap one end twice around both ropes and tuck it back through its own turns. (3) Do the same with the other end. (4) Pull the ropes apart so the two knots seat against each other. Weakness: it can jam so tight it is hard to undo, and it can fail if tied wrong, so inspect it closely.

Loop knots

Bowline

For: a loop at the end of a rope that does not slip and unties easily when unloaded. Tie (the rabbit story): (1) Make a small loop in the standing part. This is the rabbit hole. (2) Pass the working end up through the hole. (3) Take it behind the standing part, the tree. (4) Take it back down through the hole. (5) Pull the standing part to snug it. Check: the tail lies inside the loop. Weaknesses: it can work loose when slack or when the load comes and goes, it can slip when pulled sideways, and it cannot be untied while the standing end is loaded. For climbing, one knot site warns it is too easily tied wrong and calls for a backup knot in the tail. In lab tests it kept only about 58% to 75% of the rope's strength.

Figure-8 loop

For: a strong fixed loop. Tie (on a bight): (1) Double the rope end. (2) Tie a figure-8 using the doubled end as one strand. (3) Dress it and pull tight. To tie around an object, tie a figure-8 with a long tail, wrap the tail around the object, and follow the knot back through itself. Check: parallel strands that do not cross. Weakness: it can be hard to untie after a heavy load.

Alpine butterfly

For: a loop in the middle of a rope that can be loaded from the loop or either end. It can also isolate a damaged section. Tie: (1) Wrap the rope twice around your open hand. (2) Lift the turn nearest your fingertips over the other two turns. (3) Tuck it under and slide the knot off your hand. (4) Pull the loop and both ends tight. Weakness: it is fussy to set.

Hitches

A hitch ties a rope to another object, such as a post, ring, or another rope.

Clove hitch

For: a quick, temporary tie to a post. Tie: (1) Pass the end around the post. (2) Bring it back over itself to form an X. (3) Pass it around again and tuck it under the X. (4) Pull both ends. Weaknesses: it can slip or spill if the standing part is pulled the wrong way, and it works loose when the load comes and goes. Do not rely on it alone for anything important; back it up with two half hitches.

Round turn and two half hitches

For: tying to a ring or tree under steady pull. Tie: (1) Take a full round turn around the object. (2) Make a half hitch around the standing part. (3) Make a second half hitch the same way. (4) Pull snug. The round turn takes the strain while you tie the half hitches. Tie both half hitches in the same direction.

Taut-line hitch

For: an adjustable loop on tent and tarp lines. Tie: (1) Pass the end around the stake and back alongside the standing part. (2) Make a half hitch around the standing part. (3) Add a second turn inside the loop. (4) Finish with a half hitch outside the loop, in the same direction. (5) Snug it and load it slowly. Slide it away from the anchor to tighten, or toward it to loosen. Weakness: it may not hold on slick synthetic cord.

Trucker's hitch

For: cinching a load, tarp, or ridgeline with extra pull. Tie: (1) Tie a fixed loop in the standing part. (2) Pass the free end around the second anchor. (3) Pass it back through the loop and pull it back toward the second anchor. (4) Hold the tension and finish with two half hitches just below the loop. In theory it gives a 3:1 pull, but one knot site says rope friction may cut that to about 1.6:1. Weakness: the finishing half hitch can jam under big loads.

Timber hitch

For: dragging or lifting a log or pole. Tie: (1) Pass the rope around the object. (2) Take the end around the standing part. (3) Wrap the end around its own part (the part that circles the log) at least three times, keeping the turns against the wood. (4) Pull the standing part. Use extra turns on slick synthetic rope. Weakness: it holds only under tension and can roll on a long object. A half hitch added in front makes a killick hitch, which is steadier for towing.

Rolling hitch

For: tying a small line to a larger rope or pole so it grips under a lengthwise pull. It is one of the few knots you can tie and untie under load. Tie: (1) Pass the end around the main rope and make a half hitch. (2) Go around again, crossing over the first turn. (3) Tuck the end between the main rope and the first turn, then tighten. (4) Finish with a half hitch. Weakness: the pull must line up with the main rope, and it may slip on slick rope.

Prusik hitch

For: a cord loop that grips a rope when loaded and slides when loose. Tie: (1) Tie 5 or 6 mm cord into a loop with a double fisherman's. (2) Pass the loop around the rope. (3) Pull the far end through the loop. (4) Make three turns in all, side by side. (5) Pull tight and test near the ground. Weakness: the cord must be clearly thinner than the main rope. Petzl says to match the cord size to the rope's diameter and keep a real difference between them. Test it near the ground on your own rope, and learn it from an instructor before you trust it.

Cleat hitch

For: tying a line to a boat or dock cleat. Tie: (1) Take the line to the far horn. (2) Wrap it once around the base. (3) Make a figure-8 across the top and around the opposite horn. (4) Finish with a half hitch turned under. A Navy guide says extra wraps are not needed because they slow casting off.

Highwayman's hitch

For: tying to a pole or rail so you can free it instantly by pulling the tail. It is a quick-release hitch. Tie: (1) Hold a bight of rope against the pole. (2) Wrap a second bight around the first, behind the pole. (3) Pass a bight of the tail through the first loop. (4) Tighten. Pull the tail to release. Weakness: never use a quick-release hitch for a life load or a rappel line, because a sudden slip can cause a fatal fall.

The netting knot

Nets are made from repeated sheet bends tied around a gauge that sets mesh size. See Part 3 › Hunting & Fishing Tools for steps and mesh layouts.

Lashings

A lashing binds poles together with rope, turning sticks into tripods, frames, rafts, and racks. The methods below come from Scouting pioneering manuals.

Terms and rope size

  • Wrap: a turn around both spars. A square lashing usually has three.
  • Frap (frapping turn): a turn between the spars, around the wraps, that pulls them tight. A square lashing usually has two.
  • Rope length: add the two spar thicknesses in inches and multiply by three to get feet. Two spars totaling 6 inches (15 cm) need about 18 feet (5.5 m).
  • Rope size: a pioneering guide uses quarter-inch (6 mm) manila when the spars total 6 inches or less, and three-eighths-inch (10 mm) above that.
  1. Keep tension on the rope as you make each wrap.
  2. Pull frapping turns as tight as you can before the final knot.
  3. Do not rush. A loose lashing is worse than none.

Square lashing

For: joining two poles at right angles, such as a ledger across an upright.

  1. Tie a clove hitch on the vertical spar, just below where the crossing spar will sit. Tuck the short end under the wraps.
  2. Hold the crossing spar against the vertical spar.
  3. Make three tight wraps around both spars with the long end.
  4. Make two frapping turns between the spars, around the wraps. Pull them as tight as you can.
  5. Finish with a clove hitch on the horizontal spar.

Check: the spars should not rack or slide. Weakness: it fails if the wraps are slack or the rope is too small for the load.

Diagonal lashing

For: poles that cross but do not touch, such as the X brace of a trestle.

  1. Tie a timber hitch around both poles where they cross, to pull them together.
  2. Make three tight, parallel wraps along one diagonal.
  3. Make three tight wraps along the other diagonal, across the first set.
  4. Take two frapping turns between the poles, around both sets of wraps.
  5. Finish with a clove hitch on one pole.

Shear lashing and the A-frame

For: two poles held side by side that can be spread apart at one end, such as the legs of an A-frame or a pair of poles that make a gate.

  1. Lay the two spars side by side. Tie a clove hitch on one spar about six inches (15 cm) from the end.
  2. Make 8 to 10 wraps around both spars. More wraps make a stiffer lashing.
  3. Take two tight frapping turns between the spars.
  4. Finish with a clove hitch on the other spar.
  5. Spread the legs into an upside-down V.

Wraps that are too tight can resist the spread and strain the rope. Some builders leave the wraps a little loose so the lashing tightens as the legs open. Add a crossbar with a square lashing at matching points on each leg to finish the A-frame, then stand it up and shake it to test it.

Tripod lashing

For: a three-legged stand for cooking, hanging gear, drying racks, or a lookout.

  1. Lay three poles side by side with the butts even. A pioneering guide uses about 12½ feet (3.8 m) of quarter-inch (6 mm) rope for Scout staves.
  2. Tie a clove hitch around one outside pole about six inches (15 cm) from the tips.
  3. Make 6 to 8 wraps around all three poles. The tighter and more numerous, the stiffer the legs.
  4. Take two frapping turns between the middle pole and one outside pole.
  5. Take two more frapping turns between the middle pole and the other outside pole, the opposite way.
  6. Finish with a clove hitch (two half hitches) on the other outside pole.
  7. Cross the two outside legs under the middle leg, then stand it up so the feet form an equilateral triangle.

Round lashing

For: joining two poles end to end. Overlap them (about 10 inches, 25 cm, for 5-foot staves). Tie a clove hitch around both, wrap tightly 8 to 10 times with turns flush together (no frapping turns), and finish with a clove hitch. Make a second lashing on the other side of the overlap.

Floor lashing

For: binding floor poles to the supports of a platform or raft, so each floor spar is held at two points on each support.

  1. Tie a clove hitch around the support, inside the first floor spar.
  2. Make a bight in the running end and pass it over the first floor spar on the inside of the support, then under the support, then back over the spar on the outside of the support.
  3. Pull the running end between the first and second floor spars to tighten both loops.
  4. Repeat for each spar, then finish with tight half hitches on the other end of the support.

A 6-foot (1.8 m) platform takes about 50 feet (15 m) of quarter-inch rope. Test any platform with weight low to the ground first.

Safety, Inspection, and Retiring Rope

Warning

Rope can kill. Never use knots learned from this page for climbing, rappelling, rescue, or hauling people. Take a hands-on course with a qualified instructor. Rope injuries include burns and crushed fingers.

Handling rules

  • Never stand inside a loop or bight of rope that could tighten suddenly, such as an anchor line or tow line.
  • Keep fingers and limbs clear of coils and small loops. A line wrapped around a finger can cause serious injury under sudden strain.
  • Do not wrap a rope around your hand for grip. A heavily loaded line should be controlled with two or more turns around a post, winch, or cleat, not bare hands.
  • A rope that stops a falling object takes many times that object's weight.
  • Test every knot with a firm, controlled pull before you rely on it. Never test a knot by taking a fall on it or hanging your weight on it off the ground.

Inspecting rope

Run the whole rope through your hands before each use. Look and feel for:

  • cuts, flat spots, or hard or stiff areas;
  • unusually thick, thin, or soft spots;
  • a visible core through the sheath;
  • discoloration from sun or chemicals;
  • fuzzy patches (a little is normal, but watch them).

Climbing-gear makers add a loop test: bend the rope into a loop. A rope in good shape shows resistance and bounce, and a rope that collapses is too damaged to trust.

When to retire a rope

  • Retire any rope with damage in the middle. Damage near an end can be cut off.
  • Retire a rope after a serious fall or extreme load, or after acid exposure.
  • Retire an old rope even if it looks fine. One retailer gives rough limits for climbing rope: weekly use, a year or less; monthly, 4 to 5 years; never used, about 10 years. Follow the maker's limits for your rope. Natural cordage rots, so inspect it often.
  • Mark a retired rope clearly or cut it up so nobody uses it for anything important. Reuse it for low-stakes jobs such as tie-downs.
