## Writer's weak spots: how each was resolved

1. **WMS 2025 burns guideline.** Opened the original at journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10806032251345768 (Bitter et al., Wilderness & Environmental Medicine 36(4), first online 19 June 2025). PubMed, doi.org and the plain SAGE DOI URL were blocked; the `/doi/full/` URL loaded, and the WMS guideline list links to it. What it says:
   - Cooling: 20 min of cool running water at 12-18 C (53-64 F) for burns under 10% TBSA. The recommendation box says 20 min; the body text says 20-30 min. Still works if started within 60 min. Soaking is OK if there is no running water. Drinkable water is preferred. No ice, and no snow on the skin (snow may chill the water). Suggests not cooling at all when hypothermia is a risk.
   - Fluids: oral fluids under 20% TBSA. IV fluids, if available, at 20% or more. Simple ORS recipe: 6 tsp sugar and 1/2 tsp salt in 1 L.
   - Evacuation: most burn patients need it. Triggers include full-thickness over 5%, partial-thickness over 10-20%, hands, feet, face, genitals, circumferential, inhalation, infected, uncontrolled pain, and chemical, electrical or lightning burns. Joints and infants are listed for faster evacuation.
   - Dressings: antibiotic ointment or honey for deep partial-thickness burns when care is delayed; none needed if care is under 12-24 h away. Any clean, dry cloth will do. Leave most blisters intact. No preventive oral antibiotics.
   - Palm method overestimates, especially over 10% TBSA. Use a checked method such as the rule of nines.
   - Remove jewelry. OTC pain relievers first.
   The chapter now cites the original. The blog summary is gone from the text and the Sources list.
2. **Burn cooling duration.** The disagreement is still stated: Red Cross 5-20 min, MedlinePlus at least 5-30, AAD about 10, WMS 20 (20-30 in its text). The advice is now "cool a small burn for about 20 minutes" (follows WMS). I added a "Large burns are different" paragraph: WMS limits cooling to burns under 10% and says not to cool when hypothermia is a risk, MedlinePlus says not to put a severe burn in cold water, and the Red Cross says to watch for hypothermia. Cover the burn and keep the person warm.
3. **Wound irrigation and closure.** The WMS guideline list (wms.org CPG page) confirms that the 2014 wound guideline is still the newest. The chapter now gives the WMS recommendation of 6-12 psi and notes that its definitions table says up to 15, and that over 15 psi can damage tissue and push bacteria deeper (WMS). Figure 3 is updated to match. The closure table's "leave open" row now says older than about 6 h (10 h on face and scalp), matching the shorter WMS window. Before, it said "6 to 12 hours". The table's 8-12 h (24 h face) is still described as the inconsistent figure.
4. **Tourniquets.** TCCC 2016 re-opened and confirmed: 2-3 in above the wound; at least 3 min pressure with hemostatic gauze; convert within 2 h only if the person is not in shock, the wound can be watched, and the limb is not amputated; do not remove after 6 h without monitoring and lab support; mark the time. New finding: the TCCC Change 25-2 *proposal* (JSOM, April 2026) would let trained non-medics convert only within the first 2 h, leave conversion after 2 h to medical personnel, discourage field conversion after 6 h, and says a failed attempt does not reset the clock. I added it, labeled as a proposal. The conversion paragraph now limits conversion to trained people within 2 h. The untrained rule is unchanged: leave it on, mark the time, get care.
5. **Fishhook string-yank.** Checked against AAFP 2001 and EMRA. The steps now include: rest the part on a firm, flat surface; wrap the string around the middle of the bend and hold the ends tight; press down on the **eye** so the shank lies flat (this frees the barb); jerk **parallel to the shank**; bystanders out of the flight path. The treble-hook and bait-removal steps are confirmed by AAFP. The cautions now match the sources: an eye hook gets a rigid cup and evacuation (AAFP); a hook that may reach bone, tendon, a vessel or a nerve needs medical evaluation first (AAFP; EMRA for bone and vessels); never on an earlobe or other loose part (AAFP). Figure 6's label changed from "Not for eye, face, joints, tendons" to "Not near the eye or on an earlobe". The "face" ban had no source behind it.
6. **Tetanus.** Matches the CDC clinical table: clean/minor needs a booster at 10 years or more, dirty/major at 5 years or more, unknown history gets a vaccine, and TIG for dirty wounds. Incubation 3-21 days (average 8), lockjaw, about 1 in 10 fatal in the US (CDC About Tetanus). CDC advises against antibiotics to prevent tetanus. Added the CDC line on when to see a provider about a shot (deep, puncture, dirt, soil, feces, saliva, dead tissue).
7. **Rabies.** CDC confirmed: wash with soap and water for 15 min; raccoons, skunks, bats and foxes; post-exposure treatment is highly effective if given early; people who need it and don't get it before symptoms "will most likely die". Added: seek medical care urgently and contact the health department (CDC).
8. **Rule of palms and rule of nines.** StatPearls: palm 0.5%, whole hand with fingers 1%; adult rule of nines is head 9, each arm 9, front trunk 18, back 18, each leg 18, perineum 1. Minnesota Dept of Health: the patient's palm with fingers is 1%; use the patient's hand; its front and back breakdown sums to the same values. Figure 8's percentages are correct. Only superficial burns are excluded from the count (StatPearls).
9. **Amputated parts.** Merck: damp gauze or cloth, a dry sealed plastic bag, then a bag or container of ice water; no dry ice; not directly in water. MedlinePlus Traumatic Amputation: never directly on ice, no dry ice; an uncooled part stays viable about 4-6 h and cooling extends that. The "could not verify a time limit" line is replaced with the MedlinePlus figure.
10. **OTC products.** Only "use as directed on the label" (ibuprofen and acetaminophen). No prescription doses.
