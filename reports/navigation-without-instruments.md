## Facts I could not verify, or where sources disagree
- **Watch method error by time of day.** The task asked for the large error "away from noon". No source I opened gives numbers for time-of-day error. The chapter says so plainly and gives only sourced figures: the NPS paper's "about 70 degrees" at Big Bend, the Army saying it is less accurate near the equator, and the TC advice to recheck with a shadow clock after about an hour. The NPS paper also has a second error figure for the tropics (about 135 degrees), but the OCR text was garbled ("1J5"), so I left it out.
- **Daylight saving handling.** The sources agree on the 1 o'clock substitution (Army GTA 05-02-013 and TC 3-25.26) and on "use standard time" (FM 21-76 text). The NPS paper does not discuss DST. The chapter presents the two ways as alternatives.
- **Southern Cross distance multiple.** Sources disagree: 4.5 times the Cross's length (land-navigation.com), 4 times (EarthSky), about 5 times (Army FM 21-76 text, whose wording on which stars are the "pointers" is muddled). The chapter states the disagreement. The pointer-bisector method (EarthSky) is offered as the cross-check. Hadar and Rigil Kentaurus are named as the pointers (land-navigation.com).
- **Shadow-tip accuracy.** The Army calls it "simple and accurate". The only numeric figure ("errors usually within 15 degrees; can fail near solstices") comes from the old NPS training paper, which credits it to an author's own work. The chapter attributes it and says to treat it as a guide.
- **Shadow-tip in the southern hemisphere.** The Army page gives the method without hemisphere notes. The claim that it works in both hemispheres is geometric reasoning (the sun moves east to west everywhere, so the tip moves west to east), and the chapter flags it as the guide's own reasoning.
- **Pace definition.** The Army treats a pace as one natural step (about 30 in; 116 paces per 100 m level, 148 cross-country, from GTA 05-02-013). NC State Extension defines a pace as two steps. The chapter states both and says to pick one and stay consistent. The Army documents use pebbles, knots, and notebooks. Ranger-bead details come from Wikipedia (secondary).
- **Dead reckoning.** I found no authoritative page that defines it in the exact form I wrote. The definition is a plain-language one. Straight-line technique (backsights, short legs, detours) comes from secondary sources (MapTools, SectionHiker, Princeton OA).
- **Moon crescent method.** Only secondary sources (IFLScience; Natural Navigator, which describes a different moon method and gives no accuracy). The chapter rates it poor and says why. The Army text gave the lit-side rule (rises before sunset: lit side west; after midnight: east).
- **Orion.** EarthSky places only Mintaka on the celestial equator ("rises due east, sets due west"). The chapter says the other belt stars are approximate. No error figure was found.
- **Natural signs, lower-confidence items:** stump growth rings (the Army suggests it, and I found no independent confirmation, so it is rated poor); satellite dishes hemisphere rule (the "equator side of the sky" statement is the guide's reasoning from geostationary satellites; the only sourced detail is the UK, roughly southeast); southern-hemisphere reversal of slope-aspect snow melt (inferred, flagged as such); the Natural Navigator UK-only clues (wind from southwest, weathering of buildings).
- **Polaris:** altitude equals latitude is "very nearly" (ESO) and "roughly" (land-navigation.com). The land-navigation page did not confirm a 0.7 degree figure, so I used only the Army's "less than 1 degree". The Army's "too high to be useful above 70 degrees latitude" and "visible only in the northern hemisphere" are quoted from TC 3-25.26.
- **Big Dipper "never sets" (Army page)** is true only at higher northern latitudes. The chapter does not repeat it.
- **Stay-or-go advice** relies on the US Forest Service page and the Smithsonian/Gooley article. I did not re-open the NPS or American Hiking Society pages cited in the Signaling chapter.
