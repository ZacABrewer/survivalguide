## Changes made (reason → source)

### Edible plants table (top priority): every row re-checked
Each row is now backed by an extension, USDA, or university page for where it grows, which parts are edible and when, any preparation, and look-alikes. The writer's line "only 6 of 21 were checked" was replaced with a statement that every row was checked.
1. **Groundplum milkvetch: row removed.** No authoritative source confirmed safe use, and the row already said to skip it. Locoweed and milkvetch warnings stay in the dangerous-plants table.
2. **Purslane: contradiction fixed.** The old text said "do not eat any plant with milky sap", yet dandelion, which has milky sap, is listed as edible. The row now says spotted spurge forms flat mats in the same places and has milky sap that can blister the mouth, so milky sap means "not purslane". An oxalate caution was added. → NC State purslane and spotted spurge pages; NDSU Prairie Fare.
3. **Wild grape seeds.** "2–6 round seeds" was changed to "one to several flat, egg-shaped seeds (sources give 1–4 or 2–6)". Unsourced "young leaves" edibility was removed, and "sour until frost" was added. → NC State Vitis riparia; UWGB moonseed page.
4. **Mulberry.** Now red mulberry, with plains states confirmed. Added that unripe fruit and the milky sap can cause stomach upset and hallucinations, and a ▲T badge. → NC State Morus rubra.
5. **Wild plum.** Fruit color corrected to red–purple with yellow flesh. Season is July–September. The cyanide warning now covers leaves, stems, and pits, plus "pit before blending". → USDA plant guide; NC State Prunus americana.
6. **Chokecherry.** Removed "never use wilted leaves" (that warning is about black cherry; it is not in the chokecherry sources). The row now says pick dark, fully ripe fruit (NDSU: toxin falls as fruit turns dark purple). → USDA fact sheet; NDSU.
7. **Cattail.** Season and size of young shoots added. The iris look-alike is now sourced: toxic yellow flag iris has sword leaves in the same wetlands. The unsourced line "plants absorb pollutants" became a plain "avoid polluted water". → USDA plant guide; NC State Typha and Iris pseudacorus.
8. **Lambsquarters, amaranth, dandelion, plantain.** IDs were rewritten from NC State. Lambsquarters: cook it (saponins, oxalic acid). Amaranth: nitrates, seeds Aug–Oct. Dandelion: its milky sap is normal and can irritate skin. Plantain: young leaves raw, old ones cooked. → NC State; NDSU.
9. **Wild onion.** NC State's rule added: harvest only plants that smell of onion. Added: wash hands between plants so the smell doesn't carry over (this is my own precaution, not from a source).
10. **Jerusalem artichoke.** "Can cause gas" and "river bottoms" (unsourced) were removed. Harvest is Oct–Dec, raw or cooked, native to prairies. → NC State.
11. **Sunflower.** "Pick when back of head turns brown" (unsourced) was removed. Seeds raw or roasted and boiled buds are now sourced. → USDA plant guide.
12. **Prairie turnip.** The ⚠L badge was removed because no look-alike is documented. A caution about toxic pea-family plants (locoweeds) was added. Depth now reads 1.5–4 in (4–10 cm), imperial first. Peel-and-cook and the flour use were added. → SDSU; KU AIHD; UNL locoweed.
13. **Elderberry.** NC State says unripe fruit is much more toxic, so a ▲T badge was added. Plains occurrence was confirmed. → OSU; NC State.
14. **Sumac.** Now smooth sumac only; staghorn reaches only Iowa and Minnesota. The drink is sourced (NC State: soak fruit in water). The poison sumac note was corrected: drooping whitish fruit, smooth-edged leaflets, bogs, and very rare here (its range lists MN and TX but not IA, MO, KS, or OK). → NC State Rhus glabra, Rhus typhina, Toxicodendron vernix.
15. **Buffaloberry.** Added USDA's saponin caution (can irritate the gut; small amounts are low risk). Ripening is late summer, the fruit stays on the shrub, and it is sweeter after a light frost. → USDA plant guide.
16. **Serviceberry, hackberry, strawberry, black walnut.** Seasons and IDs were corrected from sources: serviceberry June–Aug; hackberry Sept–Oct, warty bark, crushed or pounded by Native Americans; strawberry range reaches Alberta and Oklahoma; walnut ripens in October, is native west to North Dakota and Texas, and its husks stain. The unsourced "thin sweet flesh / do not bite hard" was removed. → USDA plant guides; NC State.
17. **Prickly pear.** Glochid handling now follows UNR: singe the fruit, cut the needle beds off pads, and rinse only afterward. → UNR Extension; OSU (plains range).
18. **Foraging calendar** was brought into line with the corrected seasons. A death camas smell-check was added in March.

### Medicinal table
19. **Willow bark** is now sourced (MSKCC): salicin, evidence for low back pain, no trials for fever. Do not give to children (Reye syndrome). Avoid with salicylate allergy, blood thinners, NSAIDs, ulcers, or asthma. The first-person note "I could not open..." was removed.
20. **Echinacea:** NCCIH wording confirmed. The USDA note that wild stands are dwindling now supports "do not dig wild roots".
21. **Yarrow:** traditional uses and the contact-dermatitis/gloves advice now come from NC State. The pregnancy line became "safety not established; avoid", which is conservative and unsourced.
22. **Bee balm:** the tea use is sourced. The unsourced "avoid concentrated oil" was removed.
23. **Prairie sage:** names corrected to white sagebrush / prairie sage. Smudge use is sourced (NC State). The unsourced "wormwood relatives toxic in quantity" was replaced with "safety not established; do not drink".
24. **Rose hips:** the vitamin C claim is now sourced (USDA). The unsourced claim that hairy seeds irritate the gut became "strain tea through cloth".

### Dangerous plants table
25. **Jimsonweed.** Symptoms were changed to match the sources: dilated pupils, thirst, hot dry skin. The ✱X psychoactive badge and the unsourced "delirium, hallucinations" were removed, and the badge legend was updated. ID now comes from Missouri. → Missouri G4970; Arkansas FSA3025.
26. **Nightshades.** Symptoms corrected to vomiting, weakness, slowed breathing, death possible (Missouri). Arkansas data on bittersweet added: red berries, purple flowers, about 10 berries is a hazardous dose.
27. **Locoweed.** Symptoms corrected to the UNL list: weakness, loss of coordination, convulsions, abortions. "Nerve damage" is not in the source.
28. **Pokeweed, buckeye, white snakeroot, larkspur, moonseed, water and poison hemlock, death camas.** Details were aligned with Missouri, Arkansas, NC State, and MSU. Additions: berries mistaken for chokecherry (UNL); a walnut-sized root kills a cow (Missouri); moonseed fruit, leaves, and roots are toxic. Larkspur's unsourced flower color was removed. Poison hemlock's "no antidote" was dropped because no source opened here supports it (it is verified in the carrot-family chapter, which this row points to).
29. **Poison ivy:** "never burn it" is now backed by NC State (urushiol smoke can inflame the lungs).

### Fungi
30. The "no mushroom source opened" paragraph was rewritten from MDC morels and giant puffball pages and Poison Control. The old text said morel caps are "attached all the way down". That is wrong for half-free morels, so it now says hollow from cap tip to stem base. Added: treat every false morel as toxic; puffballs must be white with no sign of a cap or stem; symptoms can be delayed; cooking does not make a poisonous mushroom safe.

### Animals
31. **Rattlesnake vs. bullsnake.** The ID now uses tail and pattern features from state and federal agency pages:
    - Rattlesnake: rattle; blotches turn into bands near the tail; tail usually held up when rattling.
    - Bullsnake: pointed, banded tail with no rattle; dark band below the eye; hisses, flattens its head, and shakes its tail in leaves.
    - Pupils and head shape are no longer offered as ID features. A myth/fact callout says pupils are not a safe field test and bullsnakes flatten their heads.
    - Sources: Montana Dept. of Agriculture; NPS Bighorn Canyon; Nebraska Game and Parks; CNAH Kansas.
    - Bullsnake size is now 37–72 in (K-State/CNAH), up to 100 in (Nebraska).
32. **Figure 6 redrawn.** Pupils were removed. It now shows the rattle with the tail raised, the bands near the tail, and the bullsnake's pointed banded tail and eye band. The bottom note no longer crosses the divider line.
33. **Massasauga and copperhead.** Kansas ranges added from CNAH: western massasauga, 18–30 in, across much of the eastern two-thirds of the state; copperhead in eastern Kansas, with its ID. Range for prairie rattlesnake: western half of KS and NE, statewide in Montana below 6,400 ft.
34. **Snakebite first aid:** checked against Mayo. Added: don't wait for symptoms; no alcohol or caffeine; don't try to catch the snake.
35. **Bison.** Added the NPS Yellowstone rule (at least 25 yards / 23 m), "most injuries of any animal", "about 3× faster than people", the rut timing, and "if one charges, run away". The unsourced warning signs (snort, paw, raised tail) were removed, and so was the ▲T "Toxic" badge (wrong for bison).
36. **Ticks.** Disease lists were corrected to CDC. American dog tick: RMSF and tularemia (plus tick paralysis per K-State). Blacklegged tick now also lists anaplasmosis, babesiosis, and Powassan. The Rocky Mountain wood tick was added for the western foothills (CDC). Alpha-gal wording now follows CDC. Tick removal now matches the CDC page: don't crush, wash, and see a doctor if a rash or fever appears within several weeks.
37. **Figure 7 redrawn.** Adult ticks were drawn with 6 legs; they now have 8. Mouthparts were added. The blacklegged shield is now a dark front shield, not a band. Size labels match K-State.
38. **Tularemia:** the CDC prevention page was added (cook game thoroughly; don't drink untreated surface water).
39. **CWD:** the number of states (36, as of Jan 21, 2026) was confirmed. "Risk is not ruled out" was replaced with CDC's actual wording. The CDC hunter steps were expanded, and "do not eat meat from a positive animal" is now in bold.
40. **Hantavirus.** CDC cleanup steps were added as a numbered list:
    - Air the building out for 30 minutes first.
    - Wear gloves. Don't sweep or vacuum.
    - Use bleach 1:9 (1.5 cups per gallon), not 1:10.
    - Soak 5 minutes, wipe up with paper towels, double-bag waste.
    - Wash gloved hands, then bare hands.
    Also added: 38% fatality once lung symptoms develop, and the respirator advice for heavy infestations. → CDC rodent clean-up page; CDC hantavirus about page.
41. **Spiders:** ranges now come from CDC NIOSH (brown recluse in the Midwest and South; black widow throughout North America, most common in the South and West). First aid and prevention were added.
42. **Rabies:** now follows CDC: bats, raccoons, skunks, foxes; wash right away; get urgent care; nearly always fatal once symptoms appear. "Wash for several minutes" was unsourced and was replaced.

## Claims verified (brief)
- **NWS:** tornado, winter-vehicle, lightning (10 mi; 5 s per mile), heat-risk groups and parked cars, and flood depths.
- **Water:** CDC HAB appearance and boiling statement; EPA "when in doubt, stay out"; UNL nitrate (10 mg/L N; boiling concentrates it; RO, distillation, or ion exchange work; carbon filters and softeners don't); USGS aquifer facts.
- **CDC disease pages:** alpha-gal (3–6 h; southeast), tularemia, CWD (36 states), hantavirus (1–8 weeks; 38%), West Nile (>1,300 severe cases, >130 deaths per year), tick species and diseases, tick removal, rodent cleanup, spiders, and rabies.
- **Plant toxicity:** Mayo snakebite steps; MSU death camas (no odor, seeds most toxic, no treatment); Missouri and Arkansas poisonous-plant data; UNL locoweed; K-State poison ivy.
- **Snakes and ticks:** CNAH ranges and sizes; K-State tick descriptions.
- **Bison:** Yellowstone rule of at least 25 yards (confirmed).

## Still uncertain / notes
- **Dfb in the far-north plains** is standard Köppen climatology, but no source confirmed it; NASA confirmed only BSk, Dfa, and Cfa for Kansas.
- **Plantain:** occurrence in the plains rests on USDA PLANTS listing it as introduced across the lower 48 and NC State calling it a common lawn weed. No plains-specific page was found.
- **Cattail/iris:** the confusion is inferred from shared habitat and sword-shaped leaves. No source explicitly says foragers confuse the two. The advice to harvest only where last year's spikes stand is conservative.
- **Camp filters vs. cyanotoxins:** no authoritative source either way. The chapter tells readers not to rely on them.
- **Seasons table and calendar** are a broad summary. Timing for cactus fruit and some calendar entries is approximate.
- **CNAH** is a herpetology nonprofit, not a state agency. Its Kansas data agree with Nebraska Game and Parks and the Montana Dept. of Agriculture.
- **Length:** prose is now about 6,600 words (tables and captions included, SVG and sources excluded). That is well above the spec's 3,500-word guide for a normal chapter; the writer's draft was already about 4,800. The added text is safety detail (hantavirus steps, tick diseases, snake ID). If the coordinator wants it shorter, trim the calendar and the fire-materials section first, not the safety content.
- Ready.gov (wildfire), the Missouri Conservation purslane page, the USDA plant guides for grape, sumac, and Jerusalem artichoke, and MedlinePlus herbs all failed to load or are gone. Equivalent sources were used instead.
- **For another chapter:** the Missouri Conservation field guide loads for some species pages (morels, giant puffball) but not others (purslane). That may help the NA-08 verifier.
