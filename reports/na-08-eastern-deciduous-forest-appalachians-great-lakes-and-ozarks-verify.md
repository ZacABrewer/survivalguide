## Changes made (reason → source)

### Edible plants table
1. **Maple syrup ratio.** The draft had 32:1 from NC State only. It now says about 43 gallons of sap at 2 percent sugar, with sources ranging from about 32 to 50 depending on sweetness. The sap season (freezing nights followed by quick thaws, late winter to early spring) is now sourced. The calendar line was fixed too: sap runs on the warm days after freezing nights. → UMN Extension "Homemade maple syrup"; Ohio State F-36.
2. **Fiddleheads.** Health Canada's numbers are confirmed: boil 15 min in plenty of water, or steam 10 to 12 min, then throw out the water. Rub off the scales and wash in several changes of cold water first, and boil before sautéing or frying. I added the scientific name *Matteuccia struthiopteris*, "smooth, not fuzzy" stem, "picked 1 to 2 in above ground", and "not all fern fiddleheads are edible; bracken is carcinogenic". → Health Canada; UMaine 4198.
3. **Elderberry.** The row is now *Sambucus canadensis*, berries Aug–Sep. Always cook, because cooking removes the cyanide-making toxin. Unripe berries are much more toxic. Strip all stems. Drying or freezing does not make raw berries safe. Avoid red-berried elder (*S. racemosa*). Flower clusters are often confused with cow parsnip, with a cross-reference to the carrot-family chapter and an L badge. → NCCIH; NC State; Oregon State EM 9446.
4. **Pawpaw.** Added "raw fruit only in small amounts" and "never eat leaves or stems". → NC State.
5. **Persimmon.** Added the bezoar caution (verified). Tannin in unripe fruit can form a hard stomach mass that may block the gut, and risk is higher after stomach surgery. → Kement et al., World J Gastroenterol 2012 (peer-reviewed). NC State says nothing on bezoars.
6. **Acorns.** I removed the Farmers' Almanac source, its times (3–4 boils; 2–7 days in the fridge), the float test, and the unsourced "white oak milder" claims from the table, Figure 3 and the caption. The method now follows FM 21-76 Ch. 9: tannin is the cause, and you boil in several changes of water or crush and soak in running water. Sorting (holes, mold), drying, and "flush with cold water for hours" come from a symposium handout that lists a UC Cooperative Extension contact. The caption says neither source gives times, so keep tasting.
7. **Wild onion row.** It had no source. It is now *Allium canadense*: harvest only plants with a distinct onion odor, and not where herbicides were used. → NC State.
8. **Wild grape.** Added that it climbs with tendrils (NC State *Vitis vulpina*). Moonseed "can be fatal". Added Virginia creeper as a vine with toxic blue-black berries and 5 leaflets. → NC State moonseed and *Parthenocissus* pages.
9. **Cattail.** Added that poisonous yellow flag iris can be mistaken for cattail before flowering. → Wisconsin DNR.
10. **Sassafras.** The part column now reads "traditional root tea; dried leaves as filé". The callout now says 21 CFR 189.180 names sassafras tea as an example of an adulterated food, and the guide no longer says "don't drink regularly" but that it does not recommend the root tea. The citation moved from Cornell LII to the eCFR, which I opened. → eCFR; NC State.
11. **Dandelion.** "Ragweed-allergic people may react" was changed to "evidence is mixed", matching NCCIH.

### Ramps / lily of the valley / false hellebore (text and Figure 2)
12. **Habitat.** Penn State places ramps in moist drainages and floodplains, which overlaps false hellebore habitat. The labels changed from "Upland rich woods" and "Wet ground" to "Moist rich woods" and "Often wetter ground", and the caption now says never rely on habitat. → Penn State; Vermont DOH.
13. **Figure 2 drawing.** The lily-of-the-valley bells had been floating unattached beside the leaf. They are now drawn hanging on pedicels from an arching one-sided raceme (NC State: 6–16 nodding bells on a one-sided raceme). The ramp stem had used the red "danger" fill; it now uses a neutral fill with a "white or red-purple stem" label. The leaf label now reads "1 to 4 flat leaves" (Penn State).
14. **Ramps callout.** Added slowed breathing, 911 if the person passes out or has trouble breathing, and "Poison Help" naming. → Vermont DOH.

### Dangerous plants
15. **Mayapple.** The row now spells out that only soft, yellow, fully ripe fruit flesh is edible, in small amounts, never the seeds; every other part and the green fruit are toxic. In Figure 4 the fruit label reads "Fully ripe fruit (yellow, soft): flesh only, small amounts. Seeds are toxic." A rhizome labeled toxic was added in the soil, since roots were listed but not drawn. The caption adds that touching leaves or roots irritates skin and eyes. → NC State; UW-Madison Extension (12–18 in tall; one-leaf stems do not flower; one nodding flower at the fork).
16. **Yew.** "Planted evergreen" was wrong for the north: Canada yew is a native low shrub of the region. The row now names it, gives toxic parts and fatality (NC State English yew), and says to treat every yew as deadly.
17. **Virginia creeper** row added (D): NC State says the berries may be fatal. It is a grape look-alike.
18. **Jimsonweed** now adds convulsions and coma. **Ohio buckeye** adds muscle weakness and paralysis. **White snakeroot** adds "may be fatal" and the late-summer bloom. **Poison sumac** gives 7–13 leaflets (NC State) with Alabama's "up to 15", and yellowish-white fruit. **Poison ivy** ID now uses Alabama's wording (toothed or mitten-shaped; old vines hairy from rootlets) instead of "shiny, middle one longer". → NC State; Alabama Extension.

### Fungi (text and Figure 5)
19. The Wikipedia jack-o'-lantern source was replaced with the MDC Jack-o'-Lantern field guide. The text now says both mushrooms are orange and funnel-shaped. Jack-o'-lantern gills are sharp, crowded, true gills that also run down the stalk. It grows in clusters from stumps, tree bases or buried wood, July–October. A cluster that seems to rise from soil may be on buried wood. Chanterelles grow alone or scattered on the ground, never on wood (MDC). NAMA onset is 1–3 h.
20. **Figure 5 redrawn.** The chanterelle is now a wavy-edged funnel with forked (Y-branching) blunt ridges, where before it was a dome. The jack-o'-lanterns now grow from one fused base on wood, which before were three separate stems, and their gills taper down toward the stalk. The leader no longer cuts through a cap. Labels were updated.
21. **Amanita stages.** The draft said "feel fine 6–24 h while the liver and kidneys fail". It now gives NAMA's sequence: a 6–24 h symptom-free latency while damage begins, then violent GI illness, then apparent recovery, then liver and kidney failure. It also says to go to a hospital even if you feel well.

### Animals
22. **Black bear card.** Restructured by bear behavior per NPS. A still bear: talk low, move away slowly sideways, leave an escape route; a standing bear is usually curious. An approaching or charging bear: stand your ground, no running, screaming or climbing, look big, pick up children, keep pack on, most charges are bluffs, EPA-approved bear spray. An attack: don't play dead with a black bear, fight at face and muzzle, and fight any bear that stalks you or attacks a tent. Also added: travel in groups, and never get between a mother and cubs.
23. **Copperhead.** MDC's page now loads and was added: usually 24–36 in, max 53 in, colors, venom mild, very low fatality but always get care.
24. **Figure 6.** "Heavy, stocky body" was removed from the "more reliable" box. MDC describes water snakes as medium-sized and Clemson's body-build contrast is with slender snakes, so body build is not a safe copperhead-vs-water-snake test. The reliable box now reads: band shape; unsure means treat it as venomous; photo from afar; never handle, even dead. "Rattle (tails break; may be silent)" was added as a weak clue (MDC), and pupils stay in the weak list per the brief. The bands were filled solid ink so the "dark band" shapes read clearly. The caption notes broken or half-hourglass bands and that juvenile water snakes are the hardest to sort out (NJ Fish & Wildlife).
25. **Cottonmouth.** The draft's "30–48 in" became "usually 3–4 ft" (MDC). ID now includes the head much wider than the neck and the young's yellowish tail. The mix-up text uses MDC's cues: water snakes are less heavy, with a smaller head, no pit, and no eye-to-mouth light line.
26. **Timber rattlesnake.** "Bites mostly happen when people try to kill or handle it" was not in any source. It now uses MDC: usually escapes, coils and rattles if cornered, bite can kill, freshly killed snakes can bite by reflex.
27. **Snakebite steps.** Now match CDC NIOSH exactly: keep calm, neutral position, don't wait for symptoms, don't trap the snake. Added the don'ts: electric shock, soaking in water, and pain relievers (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen). One action per step.
28. **Ticks.** Added that blacklegged ticks are widespread in the eastern US. Added checking clothes, gear and pets, showering soon after, and removing attached ticks right away because delay raises Lyme risk (CDC). **Figure 7.** The tweezers had gripped the top of the tick's body; they now pinch the head at the skin, with a "grip close to the skin, not the body" label. The icons now have 8 legs (they had 6) and heads, and the labels were moved off the legs. Added "Rash or fever later? See a clinician" (CDC: within several days to weeks).
29. **CWD.** The wording now matches CDC: never reported in people, and it is not yet known whether people can be infected. The Missouri rule now matches MDC's page (opening-weekend deer must go to a mandatory sampling station; check MDC maps for where). It no longer states a blanket November-season rule.
30. **Brown recluse.** Now "get medical care right away", and save the spider even if crushed. → UKY.

### Water, weather, glance, legal and culture
31. **Algal blooms.** This resolves the writer's flag. CDC's prevention page says boiling won't make toxin-containing water safe and can concentrate toxins. Utah DEQ says no known recreational filter removes cyanotoxins and that boiling, chlorine and filter units do not remove them. The text now says the water cannot be treated in the field.
32. **Acid mine drainage.** Named the metals (copper, lead, mercury) from USGS.
33. **Heat exhaustion.** Added "get help if vomiting, worsening, or not better in 1 hour". → NWS.
34. **Köppen.** The codes were unsourced. They now come with USFS Bailey ecoregion pages (Warm Continental Dcb, Hot Continental Dca, Subtropical Cf, which are Trewartha codes), and the text notes the systems draw the lines differently.
35. **Elevation.** "Over 6,000 ft" became Mount Mitchell, 6,684 ft (2,037 m), highest point east of the Mississippi. → NC State Parks.
36. **National parks and Cherokee gathering.** Updated past the 2010 and 2015 articles. A 2016 NPS rule (36 CFR 2.6) allows tribal gathering agreements. The 2019 Great Smoky Mountains–EBCI agreement covers sochan only, for enrolled members with permits, and does not cover ramps. → NPS resource brief. "Plant collection generally prohibited" was softened to "bar collecting except what each park's rules allow".
37. **Culture and medicinal.** "The Oswego people" (not a nation name) was reworded: the name Oswego tea comes from Native people of the Oswego area of New York (NC State). "Jewelweed sap eased itch" became "was used for itching". "Proven" (urushiol washing) became "recommended". Unsourced "mint-family" and "daisy-family" allergy claims were removed. Yarrow use is attributed and the gloves advice was added. Slippery elm wording now notes twigs, per NC State.
38. **Map (Figure 1).** The St. Lawrence label had sat on the river line, and the Tennessee label was touching the region border. Both were moved.
39. **Sources.** Removed Farmers' Almanac, Wikipedia and LII. Added: MDC Jack-o'-Lantern, MDC Eastern Copperhead, CDC HAB Prevention, Utah DEQ, Oregon State EM 9446, World J Gastroenterol 2012, UMN maple, OSU F-36, Wisconsin DNR yellow flag iris, USFS Bailey 210/220/230, NC State Parks Mount Mitchell, NPS sochan brief, eCFR 21 CFR 189.180, the acorn handout, and NC State wild garlic, Canada yew and Virginia creeper. Vermont DOH: 25 cases in 2020 (over 4× usual), symptoms, flat vs pleated leaves, 1-800-222-1222, don't wait for symptoms.
- NC State: lily of the valley (paired leaves, cardiac glycosides, rated low; often confused with ramps), false hellebore (2–6 ft, high severity, often confused with ramps), mayapple, white snakeroot, pokeweed, buckeye, moonseed, jimsonweed, bittersweet nightshade (may be fatal), Jack-in-the-pulpit, wild ginger (aristolochic acid; do not consume), English yew, poison sumac, giant hogweed (20 ft; federal noxious weed), and every edible row (walnut 15–23 leaflets; beech small amounts; hazelnut confused with *Ostrya*; mulberry unripe fruit causes hallucinations; redbud flowers Mar–May; garlic mustard; cattail 4–16 in shoots; Jerusalem artichoke Oct–Dec; plantain blood pressure).
- 21 CFR 189.180 (eCFR). NCCIH elderberry and dandelion. Health Canada and UMaine fiddleheads.
- MDC: morels (hollow; late Mar–Apr; treat all false morels as toxic), chanterelles, jack-o'-lantern, timber rattlesnake (36–60 in; Apr–mid-Oct; reflex bites), copperhead, northern watersnake, cottonmouth (range; 4–5% of Missouri bites), hunting page (hunter ed required; opening-weekend CWD sampling; archery Sep 15; Nov portion Nov 14–24, 2026).
