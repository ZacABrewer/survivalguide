Most illness in the field is not dramatic. A sick gut, a fever, a sore eye, or a bad tooth can still sink a trip when help is days away. This chapter covers the common illnesses, how to spot the ones that are getting dangerous, and what you can do with water, rest, and careful watching. It is written at first-responder level and leaves out prescription medicines. A Wilderness First Aid or Responder course will teach you far more than a page can.

Limits of this chapter

You cannot diagnose most illness without tests. Treat this chapter as a guide to watching and comforting a sick person, and to deciding when to leave for care (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions). Take vital signs and write them down (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs). For medicines, use over-the-counter products only as directed on the label (see Part 7 › Medical Kits & Medicines).

Diarrhea, Vomiting, and Dehydration

Traveler's diarrhea is usually caused by bacteria such as E. coli or by viruses such as norovirus. Parasites are less common. Germs reach you through dirty water, food washed in it, ice, and unwashed hands (Merck Manual). Most mild cases clear up in about 3 to 5 days. The real danger is losing too much fluid, not the germ itself.

Prevent it by treating water (see Part 2 › Water), cooking food well (see Part 5 › Food Safety), and washing hands (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation).

How to tell someone is dehydrated

Watch urine first. Dark urine means mild dehydration. Little or no urine means it is getting worse. Check babies and small children often, and use the diaper and tear signs below.

Danger signs: get out or get help

  • Blood in the stool, even a small amount.
  • Fever with the diarrhea.
  • Signs of significant dehydration that do not improve with drinking (above).
  • Vomiting that will not stop, so the person cannot keep fluids down.
  • A baby or small child who is very sleepy, will not drink, or has not urinated for many hours.

The Merck Manual lists fever and blood in the stool as reasons to seek care. The Rehydration Project says to go to a clinic if diarrhea increases or vomiting continues. I could not verify the signs of cholera, so treat very watery, endless diarrhea with fast dehydration as an emergency. Keep the person drinking while you get help.

Do not

  • Do not give anti-diarrhea medicine such as loperamide to a child under 18 with acute diarrhea.
  • Do not use it if there is blood in the stool or fever. The Merck Manual advises against it in both cases, and also if the person recently took antibiotics.
  • Do not take leftover antibiotics. Many cases are viral, and antibiotics do not work on viruses.
  • Do not stop all drinking because the person is vomiting. Use small, frequent sips (below).

Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS)

Plain water replaces fluid but not the salts lost with it. Oral rehydration solution is water with the right amounts of sugar and salt. The sugar helps the gut pull the salt and water in. It does not stop diarrhea. It prevents the dehydration that makes diarrhea dangerous.

The packet (WHO / UNICEF reduced-osmolarity formula)

WHO and UNICEF recommend a reduced-osmolarity formula (245 mOsm/L). One 20.5 g packet holds 2.6 g sodium chloride, 13.5 g glucose, 1.5 g potassium chloride, and 2.9 g trisodium citrate (WHO/UNICEF statement, via the Rehydration Project). Mix one whole packet into exactly 1 liter (a bit over 1 US quart) of clean water, boiled and cooled if you are unsure of it. Do not boil the mix itself, because heat can burn the sugar.

The homemade recipe

If you have no packets, you can make a close substitute. Measure level spoonfuls:

Clean water
1 liter (a bit over 1 US quart)
Sugar
6 level teaspoons (about 25 g)
Salt
½ level teaspoon (about 2 g)

The 6-and-½ recipe appears in a WHO cholera task-force paper (as summarized by WikEM) and in the Rehydration Project's home guide. Sources disagree: another Rehydration Project page lists a stronger mix of 8 level teaspoons of sugar and 1 of salt. This guide uses the weaker one. Too much salt is the dangerous mistake. It can seriously harm a child and, in extreme cases, cause convulsions. Too little salt is less effective but not harmful. Too much sugar can worsen diarrhea.

How to give it

  1. Wash your hands with soap and water before you mix and before you serve.
  2. Offer small amounts often, using a clean cup or spoon. Let the person drink as much as they want.
  3. If the person vomits, wait about 10 minutes. Then start again with smaller sips.
  4. Keep it cool. Throw it out and mix a fresh batch after 24 hours.
  5. Add only water. Do not mix ORS with milk, juice, soup, or soft drinks, and do not add extra sugar.

The Rehydration Project guide is written for children and says to give no antibiotics or other drugs for diarrhea unless a trained health worker says so. For adults, the Merck Manual says to drink plenty of safe fluids.

Feeding through diarrhea

Do not starve a child with diarrhea. The Rehydration Project says to keep giving breast milk and, for children 4 months and older, to keep giving solid food along with ORS. For adults, this guide's practical rule is to eat small, plain, easy meals when appetite returns, while you keep drinking.

Fever

Mayo Clinic counts an oral temperature of 100°F (37.8°C) or higher as a fever. Fevers usually pass within a few days.

Measuring it

Oral and rectal thermometers are the most accurate. For infants, rectal is somewhat more accurate (Mayo Clinic). Without a thermometer, you cannot get a number. The person feels hot, flushed, or sweaty, and may have chills, headache, aches, and no appetite. A hand on the forehead only tells you something is wrong. I could not verify a reliable way to estimate a number by touch, so do not trust one. Write down what you see, how often they drink, and their pulse and breathing (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).

Comfort and cooling

  • Rest in a cool, shaded place, with light, loose clothing.
  • Drink plenty of fluids. Fever dries people out. Use ORS if there is diarrhea or vomiting.
  • A cool washcloth on the head or wrists feels good (UPMC).
  • Over-the-counter acetaminophen or ibuprofen can ease discomfort. Use as directed on the label. Read the label for children's limits.

Do not

  • Do not use ice baths. UPMC warns they can drop body temperature dangerously.
  • Do not rub alcohol on the skin. It can be harmful if it is absorbed.
  • Do not pile on blankets, which raises body heat further.

A high temperature with confusion after hard work in the heat may be heat stroke, not infection (see Part 0 › Red Pages).

Warning signs with a fever

Mayo Clinic and UPMC both say to get immediate care when a fever comes with any of these:

  • Stiff neck, especially pain when bending the head forward, or a severe headache.
  • Rash, bruising, or swelling.
  • Confusion, odd speech, or fainting.
  • A seizure.
  • Trouble breathing or chest pain.
  • Severe belly pain, repeated vomiting, or painful urination.

You cannot treat these in the field. Start the evacuation now (see Part 9 › Signaling, Rescue & Communication).

How long is too long

  • Adults: Mayo Clinic says to call a provider at 103°F (39.4°C) or higher. UPMC says a fever above 103°F for over 48 hours needs evaluation, and any fever over seven days needs a call.
  • Children: more than three days, or sooner if the child is listless, confused, or vomiting repeatedly (Mayo Clinic).
  • Babies under 3 months: a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher needs a provider (Mayo Clinic).

Respiratory Infections

Most coughs and sore throats are colds. They are miserable but mild. A person with a cold is up and about, drinks well, and breathes easily. Pneumonia is an infection deep in the lungs, and it is the one to catch early, especially in children.

Colds versus pneumonia

Likely a coldThink pneumonia / get care
Runny nose, sore throat, coughFast breathing for the person's age
Breathing is easy and quietLower chest pulls in with each breath (children)
Drinks and eats a littleCannot drink or feed; vomits everything
Alert, even if tiredVery sleepy, hard to wake, or confused
Lips and nails look normalBlue lips or fingernails

Counting a child's breathing

Fast breathing is the best simple sign of pneumonia in a child. Count when the child is calm, because crying and fussing speed up breathing (MSF). Count for one full minute. An IMCI-based training module says to use one full minute.

  1. Keep the child quiet. A sleeping or resting child is best. Lift or open clothing so you can see the chest and belly.
  2. Use a watch, or count slowly in your head to 60. Count each rise of the chest as one breath.
  3. Compare the count to the table below.
  4. If the count is high, count again after a few minutes. Look for the other signs in Figure 3.

Pneumonia danger signs in children

MSF lists these signs of severe disease: chest pulling in (clearly visible and constant, not just when upset), blue lips or fingernails, flaring nostrils, grunting on breathing out, a harsh noise on breathing in (stridor), being very sleepy or hard to wake, refusing to drink or feed, and any age under 2 months. Chest pulling in or a danger sign means the child needs a clinic or hospital. In a child under 5 who has only fast breathing, the WHO guide says the usual treatment is an oral antibiotic given by a health worker (a prescription medicine, so not covered here). Get care.

A harsh noise (stridor) when a calm child breathes in can mean a swollen airway and can be life-threatening. Get help at once.

Adults and older children

I found no verified breathing-rate limits for adults, so this guide gives none. Evacuate anyone with trouble breathing at rest, blue lips, chest pain, confusion, or weakness that grows each day. Field care is rest, fluids, and fever comfort measures. I could not verify any benefit from breathing steam, and it can scald, so this guide does not recommend it.

Eye Problems

Eyes heal poorly and fail fast. A good rule: if an eye hurts, vision changes, or you are not sure, treat it as serious. Never assume an eye injury is harmless (Prevent Blindness).

Chemical splash: flush first

This is the one eye problem where seconds count. Do not stop to look for a first aid kit. A tap or bottle of clean water is fine.

  1. Start flushing right away with clean water. Prevent Blindness says at least 15 minutes. Better Health Victoria says 15 to 20. Aim for 20, and go longer if the eye still burns.
  2. Tilt the head so the injured eye is lower than the good eye. Pour from the nose side outward (Figure 4).
  3. Hold the lids apart with your fingers. Have the person roll the eye around.
  4. If a contact lens is in, start flushing over it. The flow may wash it out. Remove lenses as soon as you can.
  5. Get urgent care. Bring the chemical's name or label. Say whether it was an acid or alkali, and a liquid or powder.

Specks and foreign bodies

  1. Do not rub the eye.
  2. Let tears wash the speck out. Or lift the upper lid out and over the lower lid while looking down. Or use an eyewash (Prevent Blindness).
  3. If it will not wash out, check under the upper lid. AAFP says always flip the upper lid to look for a hidden object, and says several vertical scratch lines on the upper cornea suggest one is there (Figure 5).
  4. Do not use tweezers or tools to pull anything out.

Scratched cornea

A scratch (corneal abrasion) causes pain, tearing, light sensitivity, and a gritty feel. AAFP gives these points:

  • Do not patch a simple scratch. Patching does not ease pain and may slow healing.
  • Do not use numbing drops at home. Repeated use can damage the cornea, delay healing, and hide a worsening problem.
  • Contact lens wearers are at higher risk. Their eyes can carry germs that infect a scratch fast and even punch a hole in the cornea. Take the lenses out and keep them out until symptoms are gone.
  • Get an exam if symptoms are worse or unchanged after 24 hours, if vision is blurry, or if a scratch has not healed in three to four days.

Cuts, punctures, and blows

If the eye is cut or something is stuck in it, do not wash it, press on it, or try to pull anything out (Prevent Blindness). Protect it with a rigid shield, such as the bottom half of a paper cup, taped to the brow and cheekbone so nothing touches the eye (Figure 6). Evacuate.

For a blow, hold a cold compress on it without pressure. Seek care for pain, blurry vision, a bulging eye, blood inside the eye, or a black eye (Prevent Blindness).

Pink eye (conjunctivitis)

Pink eye brings redness, a gritty or itchy feeling, watery eyes, and discharge that can crust the lids shut overnight (CDC, Mayo Clinic). It is most often viral and often comes with a cold. Viral and bacterial pink eye spread easily. Allergy and irritation pink eye do not.

  • Wash hands often. Do not touch or rub the eyes.
  • Do not share towels, pillowcases, eye makeup, or eye drops. Stop wearing contact lenses.
  • Get care for eye pain, light sensitivity, or blurred vision, or if it does not improve in 12 to 24 hours (Mayo Clinic). Any newborn with symptoms needs a doctor right away.
  • A painful red eye after an injury is not simple pink eye. Treat it as an injury.

Dental Emergencies

Knocked-out permanent tooth

This is a true dental emergency. A tooth put back within minutes has the best chance, and the odds fall the longer it stays out. A dental training source (Smiles for Life) says re-planting within about five minutes gives the best result, and that options remain after 60 minutes, though they are weaker. Mayo Clinic says a late re-plant is unlikely to work. Do it right away if you can.

  1. Find the tooth. Pick it up by the crown (the white chewing part). Do not touch the root.
  2. If it is dirty, rinse it very briefly. Smiles for Life says to use saline or milk. Mayo Clinic says to use milk or the person's saliva and to avoid tap water. Do not scrub it.
  3. Push it gently back into the socket, the right way around. If it will not seat fully, have the person bite gently on gauze or a clean damp cloth to hold it.
  4. If you cannot put it back, store it in cold milk or the person's saliva. Do not wrap it in tissue or cloth.
  5. Get to a dentist or emergency room as soon as you can.

If bleeding from the socket is heavy, press a clean gauze pad and have the person bite on it (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Toothache, lost fillings, and abscess

I could not open an authoritative source on lost fillings, temporary repairs, clove oil, or abscess care, so this guide gives no tips for them. Do not trust a folk remedy to fix a tooth infection.

A toothache with fever, facial swelling, or any trouble breathing may mean a spreading infection. Treat it as an emergency (Mayo Clinic lists trouble breathing with fever as a reason for immediate care). Ibuprofen or acetaminophen may ease pain; use as directed on the label.

Urinary Tract Infections

A urinary tract infection (UTI) is most often a bacterial infection of the bladder. Women get them far more often than men. Risk factors include being sexually active, using a diaphragm or spermicide, pregnancy, menopause, diabetes, kidney stones, and having a recent catheter (Office on Women's Health).

Signs of a bladder infection

  • Pain or burning when urinating.
  • A strong urge to go often, with only a little coming out.
  • Pressure or pain in the lower belly.
  • Cloudy or strong-smelling urine; sometimes blood in the urine.
  • In older adults, a UTI may show up only as tiredness, shakiness, confusion, or weakness.

Signs of a kidney infection

Fever and back pain point to a kidney infection. The University of Michigan adds one-sided upper back pain with nausea and vomiting. It needs medical care. A UTI in pregnancy also needs care right away, because it more easily spreads to the kidneys.

What you can do in the field

  1. Drink plenty of safe fluids. The Office on Women's Health suggests six to eight glasses a day for prevention. The University of Michigan says about 2 liters (a little over 2 quarts).
  2. Empty the bladder when you need to. Do not hold it for hours; the Office on Women's Health says not to go longer than three to four hours.
  3. Wipe front to back, urinate before and after sex, change out of wet swimsuits, and avoid douching.
  4. OTC pain relievers such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen may ease discomfort; use as directed on the label.

A bladder infection that does not clear up with fluids generally needs a prescription antibiotic from a clinician. Plan your way out (see Part 7 › Evacuation & Rescue Decisions).

Get care now if

There is blood in the urine, fever or chills, back or side pain, nausea or vomiting, or confusion. Also act at once for a pregnant woman with any UTI symptoms. If you have had two UTIs in six months or three in a year, see a provider after the trip.

Skin Infections

I could not open authoritative pages on fungal infections such as ringworm and athlete's foot, so this guide leaves out their treatment. For bacterial skin infection, see also Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection.

Cellulitis and spreading redness

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the skin and the tissue just below it. The skin gets red, hot, swollen, and tender. It spreads, it does not stay put. Watch the edge.

  1. Trace the edge of the red area with a pen. Write the date and time next to the line.
  2. Check again every few hours, and at the same time every day.
  3. If the redness crosses your line, the infection is winning. Plan to evacuate (Figure 8).

Cellulitis usually needs prescription antibiotics, so the field plan is to watch, rest, and move toward care. Red streaks, fever or chills, a fast pulse, or confusion signal a spreading infection. Treat them as an emergency.

Boils and abscesses

An abscess is a pocket of pus under the skin. Do not squeeze it or cut it open yourself. Warm, damp cloths held on a small boil several times a day may help it drain on its own. Get care for a large one, one on the face, or one with fever or spreading redness.

Caring for a Sick Person in a Group

One sick camper can sicken the rest of the group. Basic habits stop most spread. The CDC and Mayo Clinic pages on pink eye stress hand washing and not sharing towels or eye products. The same logic works for stomach illness, colds, and skin infections.

  • Pick one caregiver. Fewer people touching the patient means fewer people exposed. The caregiver should rest too.
  • Wash hands with soap and clean water after helping the patient, after using the toilet, and before cooking or eating. (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation).
  • Separate dishes, cups, and towels for the sick person. Someone with diarrhea or vomiting should never cook for others.
  • Handle waste carefully. Bury stool and vomit well away from water and camp (see Part 7 › Hygiene & Sanitation). Wear gloves or a plastic bag over your hand for soiled cloths.

These are this guide's practical rules, built on the hygiene advice above. I found no single wilderness guideline for camp isolation.

Keep a log

When help is days away, a written log shows trends. Every few hours, note the time, temperature, pulse, breathing, fluids drunk, urine, stools, vomiting, pain, and mental state. Use it to decide when to leave (see Part 7 › Emergency Childbirth & Long-Term Care).

Leave for care when

  • Confusion, fainting, or trouble breathing.
  • Stiff neck with fever, or a new rash with fever.
  • Blood in stool or urine, or fever with diarrhea.
  • A child or baby who will not drink, or passes no urine for many hours.
  • Redness spreading past your pen line, or red streaks.
  • Any chemical eye injury, a cut or punctured eye, or vision loss.
  • The person is getting worse, not better.
