A medical kit is only as good as the person using it. This chapter shows how to build kits in four sizes, which over-the-counter (OTC) medicines are worth carrying and what each one can hurt, how to plan extra prescriptions with your doctor, and how to store, date, and rotate everything so it works when you need it. Pair every item you pack with training in how to use it.

Use as directed on the label

This chapter names medicines by their generic ingredient and tells you what they treat and what to watch for. It gives no doses on purpose. Doses depend on age, weight, kidney and liver health, and the other medicines a person takes. Read the Drug Facts label every time. Children need products and amounts made for their age. If the label says to ask a doctor or pharmacist, do it before you leave home. In the United States, Poison Help is 1-800-222-1222.

Build Around Skills, Not Gear

NOLS, the wilderness school, makes a simple point: there is no perfect first aid kit. Start from your own trip length, group size, and destination. Pack only items you already know how to use, and practice with each one before you need it. A pre-made kit that you add to is usually cheaper than building one from scratch (NOLS). The Red Cross agrees that kits should fit the climate, the number of people, the terrain, how remote you are, and any known medical needs.

Every item below points to the chapter that teaches it. If you do not know how to use something, either learn it (see Training, at the end of this chapter) or leave it home.

Bleeding-Control Items

These items save lives, and fakes and poor copies fail. Buy with care.

Tourniquets: buy genuine

The Committee on Tactical Combat Casualty Care keeps a list of recommended devices. The December 2021 version I could read names these non-pneumatic limb tourniquets: Combat Application Tourniquet (CAT) Gen 7, SOF Tactical Tourniquet-Wide (SOFTT-W), SAM Extremity Tourniquet, Tactical Mechanical Tourniquet, Ratcheting Medical Tourniquet, and the TX2 and TX3. It also lists two pneumatic (air-filled) models. That list may have been updated since, so check the current one before you buy.

Counterfeits are real. A New Hampshire EMS bulletin reports counterfeit CAT tourniquets whose tension rod (the windlass) bends or breaks, so the device fails to stop the bleeding. It says to buy only from the maker or an approved seller, to be wary of prices that look too good to be true or items shipped from abroad, and notes that authentic CATs are made in the United States. If you think you own a fake, contact the manufacturer.

Hemostatic gauze

The same CoTCCC list names three clotting gauzes: Combat Gauze, Celox Gauze, and ChitoGauze. Buy a sealed, in-date package and check the date on the wrapper at every kit check. Packing technique is in Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection.

Do not

  • Do not buy a tourniquet from an unknown online seller because the price is low.
  • Do not pack a tourniquet you have never practiced with. Take a Stop the Bleed class first.
  • Do not leave your only tourniquet buried at the bottom of a pack. Keep it where you or a partner can grab it in seconds.

Splinting, Wound Care, and Blister Supplies

  • Splinting: The Red Cross and NOLS both list a moldable (SAM-type) splint, elastic wrap, rolled bandages, and triangular bandages. Triangular bandages double as slings and swathes (NOLS). Learn the techniques in Part 7 › Bones, Joints, Head & Spine.
  • Wound care: Irrigation syringe, gauze pads, non-stick pads, roll gauze, transparent film dressings, tape, and wound-closure strips are on the NOLS and Red Cross lists. Antibiotic ointment packets and antiseptic wipes are on the Red Cross list too. Wash the wound itself with clean water, not strong antiseptic (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).
  • Blister care: Moleskin and second-skin gel dressings (NOLS), plus athletic tape. Cut a doughnut hole in the pad so it does not press on the blister.
  • Sterile items: NOLS advises sealing sterile dressings in clear plastic to protect them from moisture. Replace any that are opened or damaged.
  • Tools: Tweezers, shears, safety pins, thermometer, and a CPR mask (NOLS and Red Cross).

Pain relievers: know the risks

Acetaminophen. MedlinePlus quotes the label: taking too much can cause liver damage, severe enough to require a transplant or cause death. Accidental overdose happens when people ignore the directions or take more than one product that contains it. It hides in many cold and flu medicines, sometimes written as "APAP." Check the label of every product. Do not take it if you have three or more alcoholic drinks a day (per the label), and tell your doctor about liver disease. Symptoms of too much can include nausea, vomiting, upper belly pain, yellow skin or eyes, and unusual bleeding. Call Poison Help or a doctor right away, even if you feel fine.

NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin used for pain). They can cause ulcers and bleeding in the stomach or gut, sometimes without warning. Risk is higher with age (the FDA-hosted ibuprofen label says 60 or older), past ulcers, blood thinners, steroids, heavy alcohol use, and long use. Long-term, continuous use may raise the risk of heart attack or stroke. Ask a doctor first if you have heart disease, high blood pressure, kidney disease, asthma, or ulcers, and if you are pregnant. MedlinePlus says to avoid ibuprofen at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later unless a doctor directs it. It also says to ask a doctor before giving it to a child who has not been drinking or has lost fluid from vomiting or diarrhea. Stop and get help for black or tarry stools, bloody vomit, or chest pain.

Children, aspirin, and Reye syndrome

Reye syndrome is a rare, serious illness that has followed aspirin use in children during chickenpox or flu. MedlinePlus says never to give a child aspirin unless the child's provider says so. It also names Pepto-Bismol and oil of wintergreen products as containing salicylates, which are aspirin-like compounds, and advises not giving them to a child with a cold or fever. Confusion or unusual sleepiness in a sick child is an emergency.

Myth vs. fact

Myth: Over-the-counter means harmless. Fact: OTC medicines can seriously harm the liver, stomach, heart, and kidneys when misused. The label is the dose.

Prescription Medicines: Plan with Your Doctor

Prescription medicines are chosen and dosed by a clinician who knows your health. This guide does not give names with doses and does not tell you to treat yourself with antibiotics or other prescription drugs. What you can do is plan ahead, and that matters most for the medicines you already take.

  1. Make a list of every medicine you take, including OTC medicines and supplements. Note what each is for.
  2. Ask your doctor and pharmacist whether you can keep an extra supply of your own daily medicines, and how to get it. Ask how your insurance handles early refills.
  3. If you will travel somewhere remote, tell your doctor the plan. Ask whether any travel or expedition prescriptions make sense for you, and get written instructions on how and when to use them.
  4. If you or a family member has a known severe allergy, ask about epinephrine auto-injectors and how many to carry. Learn to use the trainer before you need it.
  5. Ask your doctor which symptoms mean "call me," which mean "go to the emergency room," and which medicines can be dangerous together.
  6. Keep medicines in original, labeled containers. Carry them in the cabin when you travel, not in the trunk.
  7. Get your tetanus vaccination up to date, and write the date on your card (see Part 7 › Bleeding, Wounds, Burns & Infection).

Do not

  • Do not share prescriptions or take someone else's.
  • Do not stock leftover antibiotics and use them without a clinician's advice. The wrong drug, dose, or timing can hide an illness or add side effects.
  • Do not stop a daily prescription just because your supply is short without talking to a clinician first.

Storage: Heat, Cold, Moisture, Light

Storage matters as much as the date. The Wilderness Medical Society's magazine notes that potency loss is tied to poor storage, and that radiation, vibration, moisture, and packaging matter along with temperature.

  • Heat. Excess heat can lower potency. GoodRx notes it can break medicines down into by-products, some harmful. Never leave medicines in a parked car or trunk. Keep them in the cabin.
  • Cold. Do not let medicines freeze. Insulin is a protein and can become unstable when frozen. If a product that must not freeze did freeze, replace it.
  • Moisture. Skip bathrooms. MedlinePlus says to store at room temperature away from heat and moisture, in the original container, tightly closed.
  • Light. Keep medicines out of direct sun and off windowsills. Drugs.com also advises keeping them away from light.
  • Check after a bad day. If a medicine was hot, frozen, or wet, look for clumping, color change, strange smell, or crumbling. Normal appearance is not proof it is safe. When in doubt, ask a pharmacist or the maker.
  • Epinephrine and insulin. FARE says the best auto-injector is one stored out of direct light at room temperature. Insulin and other refrigerated products follow their labels: on the move, use an insulated bag and keep ice packs from touching the product. Sources differ on whether auto-injectors need a fridge, so read your own label.
  • Children. Lock safety caps, and keep all medicines out of reach.

Expiration Dates: What the Evidence Says

An expiration date is the last day the maker guarantees full potency and safety (Drugs.com). It is not a cliff. It is also not permission to ignore it.

The military study

The Shelf Life Extension Program (SLEP) is a program the FDA ran for the Department of Defense for about 20 years. A published review looked at 122 products across 3,005 lots. About 88% of lots stayed good at least one year past their labeled date, and the average extension was 66 months. The extra shelf life varied widely, and the authors said each lot needs testing to be sure. They said many products can be extended if stored properly (PubMed).

Caveats. Military stockpiles are kept in controlled conditions, which home kits rarely match (Wilderness Medical Society). Results apply to products in original packaging, and biologics were left out (Drugs.com). Some lots failed. The WMS article names albuterol inhalant and diphenhydramine spray among failed lots, and says about half of epinephrine cartridge-needle lots failed.

Epinephrine auto-injectors

These are the hardest call. FARE, a food allergy group, says an expired auto-injector is better than none if no in-date one exists, but not if the liquid is pinkish, more than slightly yellow, cloudy, or has particles. Its advisors think properly stored devices stay safe and effective up to nine months past the date. A 2006 DoD article found most properly stored epinephrine at full strength one year past the date, but some was not. An older study cited by the WMS article found reduced delivery that grew with time past expiry. The sources do not match, so the conservative rule is: carry in-date devices, and use an expired one only when there is no other option and the liquid looks clear.

Plain rule for this guide

Keep your kit in date. Treat "may still work" as a last resort when the choice is an expired medicine or nothing, not as a reason to skip replacing it. The WMS article's rule of thumb: decide whether the expired medicine is better than no medicine.

Non-drug items

Sterile dressings, hemostatic gauze, and similar products also carry dates. Replace anything opened, wet, torn, or past its date as soon as you can. Inspect tourniquets for cracks, frayed straps, and bent or stiff rods every time you open the kit.

Rotate Your Stock

The Red Cross says to check the kit regularly, to check expiration dates, and to replace used or expired contents. It also notes that kit contents lose effectiveness over time. A schedule makes this happen.

  1. List every item with its expiration date in one notebook or file. Keep a paper copy in the kit.
  2. Write the earliest date on the outside of each pouch in marker.
  3. At each check, restock anything used. Use older stock first.
  4. Take the old medicines to a take-back program. MedlinePlus says not to flush them.
  5. Note changes in your family: new allergies, new prescriptions, kids who have outgrown pediatric products.

Organize and Label Your Kit

In an emergency you will be shaking, in the dark, with someone bleeding. A kit sorted by problem, not by item, saves seconds. Use pouches or zip bags with large labels. Do not rely on color alone, because color is hard to see in poor light, and some people are color blind. Put the name on every pouch in big letters.

  • Put the bleeding pouch on top or in an outside pocket.
  • Make everyone in the group aware of where the kit is. NOLS says to make sure everyone knows, and to keep the kit accessible at all times.
  • Use clear zip bags so you can see inside, and so water stays out.
  • Tape a short contents list inside the lid, with dates for medicines.
  • Keep a small waterproof pen and paper for notes (see Part 7 › Patient Assessment & Vital Signs).

The medical information card

Make one card for every person, adults and kids. Keep one in the kit and one in a wallet or pocket. It gives a rescuer, or a stranger who finds you hurt, the facts they need. Update it twice a year, at your kit check. Do not include your Social Security or account numbers.

Training: The Best Item in the Kit

A kit full of gear that nobody knows how to use is dead weight. Take real classes.

  • Wilderness First Aid (WFA) for anyone who spends time far from help. NOLS recommends a WFA course to learn how to use the supplies on common outdoor injuries and illnesses.
  • Wilderness First Responder (WFR) if you lead groups or live remote. It goes deeper into assessment and long-term care.
  • CPR and AED from the Red Cross or American Heart Association.
  • Stop the Bleed for tourniquets and wound packing, with hands-on practice.
  • A talk with your pharmacist once a year about your medicines and your kit.
